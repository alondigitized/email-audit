# Drunk texting?
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-05-14**
## Executive summary

- Drunk Elephant's "Drunk texting?" send is a classic SMS list-building play dressed in the brand's signature playful voice. The pun is the whole thesis: open out of curiosity, land on an SMS signup offer with 15% off as the carrot. The hero section executes cleanly — a phone-in-hand visual, "STAY CLOSE" as the headline, and the 15% incentive form a coherent story. The secondary module, featuring The Littles 8.0 Kit, does real work by giving shoppers something tangible if they're not ready to surrender their phone number.
- The core problem is the subject line's opacity. "Drunk texting?" is witty for loyalists who already know the brand, but it gives zero indication of the offer inside. The 15% discount — the strongest reason to open — is completely hidden from the inbox. That's a missed open-rate opportunity on everyone who isn't already brand-obsessed.
- Overall, this is a polished, on-brand execution of a legitimately useful campaign goal. It just leaves opens (and thus new SMS subscribers) on the table by burying the lead.

## What's working

- Brand voice is fully committed: "Drunk texting?" as the subject is audacious, memorable, and unmistakably Drunk Elephant.
- The 15% offer is visible immediately in the hero subhead — no scrolling required to understand the value.
- "GET ON THE LIST" is a clean, direct CTA with no ambiguity about what you're signing up for.
- The Littles 8.0 Kit module provides a concrete product anchor for readers who aren't ready to convert on the SMS ask — solid fallback path.
- Clean render: no broken images, no layout issues, tight visual hierarchy top to bottom.

## What's weak

- Subject "Drunk texting?" communicates nothing about the 15% offer — the most compelling incentive is completely invisible from the inbox.
- SMS signup is a higher-friction conversion than a standard shop CTA, and no objection-handling (e.g., "reply STOP anytime") appears anywhere in the hero.
- The top banner about National Hospital Week / healthcare workers feels bolted on and tonally disconnected from the rest of the email.
- No deadline or expiry on the 15% offer — zero urgency means "I'll do it later" is the dominant exit behavior.
- The Littles Kit module appears to lack a dedicated shop button; it may be relying on the product image as the link, which is low affordance.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Put the 15% in the subject line.** This is the single highest-impact change. The pun is fun but leaves the best hook off the table for everyone who isn't already a Drunk Elephant devotee.
- 2. **Add an expiry to the SMS signup offer.** "Sign up by Sunday" or "Offer valid 48 hours" cuts through the "I'll do it later" dropout loop that kills list-build campaigns.
- 3. **Add a dedicated shop CTA on the Littles Kit module.** If someone scrolls past the SMS ask, they need a direct path to purchase — don't make them guess the image is a link.
- 4. **Address phone-number hesitation in the subhead.** One short line ("Reply STOP anytime. No spam, ever.") reduces friction without cluttering the design.
- 5. **Remove or relocate the Hospital Week banner.** It competes with the primary message, and the tonal whiplash (healthcare awareness → brand SMS pitch) is jarring enough to distract.

## Technical Audit — Drunk Elephant "Drunk texting?" (2026-05-14)

---

### 1. Technical Summary

The email is built on a standard XHTML 1.0 Transitional table layout. Two confirmed rendering defects are present: CSS rules wrapped in HTML comment syntax will be silently dropped by all email clients, and custom fonts are loaded redundantly from two separate third-party CDNs with no controlled fallback. The HTML source is truncated so link, compliance, and UTM sections are partially assessable.

---

### 2. Link & Tracking Issues

**Partially assessable** — HTML is truncated before body links appear.

- No UTM parameters or click-tracking wrappers are visible in the truncated source. Cannot confirm presence or absence across CTA links.
- Font asset URLs point to `db.onlinewebfonts.com`, a third-party CDN not controlled by Drunk Elephant. If that CDN is blocked by a corporate firewall or link-scanner, the `<link>` prefetch requests will fire before the email is opened — registering a false open event in some ESP tracking systems.

---

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Critical — CSS rules silently dropped (mobile layout broken):**

Inside the `@media only screen and (max-width: 600px)` block, two rules use HTML comment syntax instead of CSS comments:

```css
<!-- *[class=contenttable] { width:414px !important;} -->
<!-- *[class=mobile] { width:414px !important;height: auto !important;} -->
```

`<!-- ... -->` is not valid CSS. Every email client will silently discard these two rules. Elements with `class="contenttable"` and `class="mobile"` will not receive their mobile-width overrides, likely causing layout overflow on small screens.

**Font loading — redundant and split-source:**

`BrownBold`, `BrownLight`, and `BrownThin` are declared twice each: once via `<link rel="stylesheet">` in `<head>` and again via `@font-face` in the `<style>` block, both pointing to `db.onlinewebfonts.com`. This doubles HTTP requests per font. `BrownRegular` is loaded only from `drunkelephant.com` (self-hosted), creating an inconsistent dependency model — three weights rely on an external CDN, one does not.

**Third-party font CDN dependency:**

`db.onlinewebfonts.com` is a public font distribution service. Its uptime and caching headers are not under the sender's control. All four `@font-face` stacks lack a reliable system-font fallback defined in the actual element-level `font-family` declarations (not assessable from truncated source, but a known risk pattern for this architecture).

**Global `line-height` reset:**

```css
*{line-height: 100%}
```

Applied after `ExternalClass` line-height rules. The `*` selector overrides inherited `line-height` on all elements including `<td>` and `<p>`, which can compress multi-line text in Outlook. This is a known Outlook rendering hazard.

**`lang` attribute:** The `<html>` element has no `lang` attribute. Screen readers will default to the user's OS language. Not a blocker but flags in WCAG 3.1.1.

**Partially assessable:** Image `alt` attributes, button/CTA contrast ratios, and link text cannot be evaluated from the truncated source.
