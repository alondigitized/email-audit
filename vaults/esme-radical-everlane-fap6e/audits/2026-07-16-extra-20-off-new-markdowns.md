# Extra 20% Off New Markdowns
**Score:** 9/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-16**
## Executive summary

- After nine consecutive editorial sends — culminating with the polished "Mini, Midi, Maxi" on July 15 — Everlane finally drops the storytelling mask and mails a straight promotional email. The pivot is jarring in tone but the underlying offer is the strongest thing in my inbox in this entire run: extra 20% off items already marked down up to 70%. The parenthetical transparency in the hero copy ("ALREADY UP TO 70% OFF") is doing real trust work — this doesn't read like a manufactured discount.
- The execution is minimal to a fault. A blurry, product-free hero image, four flat-lay category tiles with no prices, no urgency signal, and no editorial connective tissue between the run that built brand warmth and this ask for conversion. The machinery for a high-converting sale email is all here — strong offer, clean CTA, recognized sender — but the levers that actually move clicks (deadline, specific product at specific price, social proof) are entirely absent.
- This will pull some clicks on offer alone. But for a subscriber who's been reading Everlane's careful editorial voice all month, the tonal whiplash is real, and there's nothing in the body to bridge it.

## What's working

- The stacked offer ("Extra 20% off" on items "already up to 70% off") is the most compelling deal Everlane has surfaced in this entire run — and the parenthetical makes the math honest rather than hiding it in fine print.
- "SHOP SALE" — black button, white background, above the product grid — is the clearest CTA Everlane has used since "OOO On" (July 10).
- Subject line is 27 characters, mobile-safe, zero spam signals, and delivers the complete offer without truncation.
- Coming off nine consecutive editorial sends, this promotional break lands as earned rather than spammy — the cadence context matters here.
- The four flat-lay category tiles (Dresses, Tees, Denim, Sandals) are clean and give a subscriber who already knows what they want instant category navigation.

## What's weak

- No urgency signal anywhere in the email — no end date, no "limited sizes," nothing that creates a reason to click today versus bookmarking it and forgetting.
- The hero image is a blurry, abstract texture (olive/grey soft-focus fabric) — it shows zero product and contributes nothing to desire or context.
- All four tiles link to sale category pages, not specific products — a subscriber who's on the fence will arrive at a browse experience rather than a conversion one.
- No prices are visible anywhere; "better prices" in the body copy is abstract without an anchor (crossed-out original price, even one example).
- Zero editorial voice bridging the run — after "Mini, Midi, Maxi" and "Summer Foundations" established genuine warmth, this email opens cold with an offer overlay and never acknowledges the register shift.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Add a deadline to the hero and subject.** "Ends Sunday" or "48 hours only" transforms a bookmarkable browse email into a time-sensitive click. The single highest-impact change available.
- 2. **Put one specific hero product at its sale price.** Show the brown dress: $98 → $62. Crossed-out price anchors the discount emotionally in a way that "up to 70% off" never can.
- 3. **Replace the blurry hero background with a product shot or lifestyle image.** The editorial run succeeded partly because the images earned attention. An abstract texture tells subscribers nothing.
- 4. **Add a low-inventory nudge to one category tile.** "Only a few sizes left" on Denim creates scarcity without being deceptive.
- 5. **Write one sentence of bridge copy** between the nine editorial sends and this promotional pivot — something like "The pieces you've been watching just got more affordable." It costs nothing and closes the whiplash gap.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Extra 20% Off—Ends Sunday`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Sale on Sale: Up to 70% Off + 20% More`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Dresses, denim, sandals, and tees — now at their lowest price of the season.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `The pieces you've been watching just got more affordable. This weekend only.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Empty `<title>` tag:**
```html
<title> </title>
```
Contains only whitespace. Some clients (Apple Mail, Gmail web) surface the title as a preview label. Should be set to match the subject line or left intentionally blank — not just whitespace.

**All link states styled identically with no hover differentiation:**
```css
a:not([name]) {color:#000;text-decoration:none}
a:link   {color:#000;text-decoration:none}
a:visited{color:#000;text-decoration:none}
a:active {color:#000;text-decoration:none}
a:hover  {color:#000;text-decoration:none}
```
Links are visually indistinguishable from surrounding body text (both black, no underline, no hover change). WCAG 1.4.1 requires a non-color differentiator when foreground and link colors match. This is a hard fail for accessibility.

**Custom font name inconsistency:**
- `h1` uses `"MaisonNeue-Light"` (no space)
- `h2`, `h3`, `h4` use `"Maison Neue"` (with space)

These are two different CSS font-family name strings. If the `@import` above registers the font under one canonical name, one of these selectors will miss it and fall through to Arial. Verify the registered name in the Klaviyo font CSS response.

**`img` block declared twice in `<style>`:**
```css
/* First declaration */
img { border: 0; line-height: 100%; outline: none; text-decoration: none; -ms-interpolation-mode: bicubic }
/* Second declaration (later in same <style> block) */
img { border: 0; height: auto; line-height: 100%; outline: none; text-decoration: none; max-width: 100% }
```
`height: auto` and `max-width: 100%` are only in the second block; `-ms-interpolation-mode` is only in the first. No functional breakage, but the duplication indicates template bloat that may cause specificity surprises if inliner runs over it.

**Alt text on images:** Not verifiable from truncated source. Flag for full-source review — Klaviyo templates commonly omit `alt=""` on decorative images, which triggers screen reader noise.
