# The Natural Choice
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-07**
## Executive summary

- After fourteen consecutive promotional emails — many of them near-identical sale blasts — Everlane finally breaks the pattern with a brand/editorial send focused on natural materials. The creative is clean: a strong hero image of a woman in a black shirt, a tight product grid of six shirt styles, and a credible sustainability callout. This is exactly the kind of palette-cleanser the list needed.
- The problem is the subject line. "The Natural Choice" is so vague it reads like a placeholder. After two weeks of "70% Off" and "LAST CALL" subject lines, subscribers conditioned to expect deal language will glide right past this. The email's quiet editorial tone is a feature — but the inbox entry point doesn't earn the open. And burying a "READ OUR LATEST IMPACT REPORT" CTA in the lower half of the email is a momentum killer; nobody who opened to shop shirts will detour to a PDF.
- On balance: the creative is the best Everlane has looked in two weeks, but the subject undersells it and the sustainability block undercuts the purchase funnel.

## What's working

- **Hero image is compelling and on-brand.** Woman in black, minimal styling, uncluttered background — the strongest hero creative in this recent run.
- **Headline lands cleanly.** "Naturally Better Shirts" is specific, benefit-led, and readable at a glance.
- **Product grid shows real range.** Six shirts across neutrals — cream, navy, black, denim blue, gray — gives the shopper a reason to explore without feeling like a catalog dump.
- **Material callout copy is tight.** "Organic cotton. Masters of Flax Fibre™ certified linen." — short, differentiated, and signals quality without puffery.
- **No render bugs.** Clean layout, no broken images, text and images properly spaced throughout.

## What's weak

- **Subject line is generic.** "The Natural Choice" signals nothing — no product, no category, no season, no benefit. After 14 sale emails, this reads like background noise.
- **No offer.** Zero price incentive or urgency in an email that followed a sustained 70%-off campaign. The contrast is jarring without a bridge ("now shop the full-price styles we're proud of").
- **"READ OUR LATEST IMPACT REPORT" is a dead-end CTA.** Placed right before the footer, it asks someone primed to shop to instead read a sustainability document. Kills momentum at the worst moment.
- **Preview text invisible in webview.** Can't confirm it exists or what it says — a missed opportunity to reframe the vague subject with something specific.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Rewrite the subject to be specific.** "Shirts made better" or "Linen, cotton, certified." A product noun in the subject lifts open rates — especially against a weeks-long backdrop of sale subjects.
- 2. **Kill or relocate the Impact Report CTA.** Move it to a footer text link; it doesn't belong as a prominent button mid-funnel. Replace with a secondary "Shop Linen" or "Shop Organic Cotton" CTA segmenting the product grid.
- 3. **Add a thin bridge sentence for the list.** One line of copy — "Back to the good stuff."  or "Full price, worth every cent." — acknowledges the sale is over and resets expectations without being defensive.
- 4. **Give the product grid a shop link per style.** Each shirt tile should be clickable to the PDP. A flat grid with a single SHOP NOW button loses cart intent.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Shirts made from better stuff`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Linen. Cotton. Certified.`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Six shirt styles in organic and certified natural materials — shop now.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Naturally better materials for everyday wear — see the shirts.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**`<title>` tag is empty (whitespace only)**
```html
<title> </title>
```
Some clients (notably Outlook desktop) render the `<title>` value in window chrome or tab labels. A blank title is a minor but sloppy artifact.

**`<html>` element has no `lang` attribute**
```html
<html xmlns="..." xmlns:o="..." xmlns:v="...">
```
No `lang="en"` (or equivalent). Screen readers cannot infer document language; WCAG 2.1 §3.1.1 failure.

**Custom font loaded via CSS `@import` — will fail in Gmail and Outlook**
```css
@import url(https://static-forms.klaviyo.com/fonts/api/v1/USb9mf/custom_fonts.css);
```
`@import` inside a `<style>` block is stripped by Gmail (all variants), Outlook 2007–2021, and Yahoo Mail. `MaisonNeue-Light` and `Maison Neue` will not render in those clients. The declared fallback stack (`Arial, "Helvetica Neue", Helvetica, sans-serif`) will apply — confirm this is visually acceptable.

**All anchor link colors are `#000` with `text-decoration: none` across all states**
```css
a:not([name]) {color:#000;text-decoration:none}
a:link {color:#000;text-decoration:none}
a:visited {color:#000;text-decoration:none}
a:active {color:#000;text-decoration:none}
a:hover {color:#000;text-decoration:none}
```
Links are visually indistinguishable from body text in all states. This is an intentional design pattern for editorial emails, but it fails WCAG 1.4.1 (use of color) if no other visual differentiator (underline, weight, position cue) is present. Flag for content/design review if links appear inline within paragraphs.

**Duplicate `img` CSS rule**
The `img` reset rule appears twice in two separate `<style>` blocks. Not breaking, but produces unnecessary bloat.

**`a:not([name])` selector unsupported in Outlook 2007–2019**
CSS3 negation pseudo-class is ignored by Outlook's Word rendering engine. The intent (excluding named anchors from link styling) will silently fail — named anchors, if any, will inherit the `#000`/no-underline rule in Outlook. Low severity given named anchors are rarely used in marketing email.
