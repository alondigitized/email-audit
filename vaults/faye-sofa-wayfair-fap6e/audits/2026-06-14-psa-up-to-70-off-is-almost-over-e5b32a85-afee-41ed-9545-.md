# 📣 PSA: Up to 70% OFF is almost OVER 📣
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-14**
## Executive summary

- This is Wayfair's finale for the HOT SUMMER SALE — the "ENDS TOMORROW" hero and "24 HRS ONLY flash deals" banner make clear this is meant to close out the same cadence that's been running since at least June 11. The urgency mechanics are correct for a closer email: two time-anchors, a prominent 70% OFF claim, and star-rated product cards. The structural instincts are right.
- The problem is that the "PSA" subject hook is completely abandoned the moment the email opens. A PSA implies voice, editorial authority, or a breaking-news moment — the body delivers none of that. It's the same HOT SUMMER SALE tile-grid chassis, visually indistinguishable from the June 13 pools send, the June 13 rugs send, and the June 11 dressers send. A subscriber who opened those three and didn't buy has already pattern-matched this template to "noise." The subject earns a second look; the body wastes it.
- The email does its mechanical job — urgency is clear, offer is visible, products are linked, ratings are shown. But it's doing nothing to re-engage the holdout who has seen this layout five times in five days.

## What's working

- **Double urgency framing**: "ENDS TOMORROW" in the hero and "24 HRS ONLY" in the flash-deals banner give two distinct reasons to act now, which is stronger than a single deadline callout.
- **70% OFF above the fold**: The highest discount tier is front-and-center in a high-contrast orange block — scannable in under a second.
- **"Picked just for you" framing**: Even if editorially curated rather than truly personalized, the heading reduces the generic-catalogue feel slightly.
- **Star ratings embedded in product cards**: Social proof baked directly into the grid removes one click-uncertainty barrier.
- **Clean render**: No broken images, no overlapping text, layout is structurally solid.

## What's weak

- **PSA hook abandoned immediately**: The subject's "PSA" framing promises a voice and urgency that the tile-grid body never delivers — the hook is unearned.
- **70% OFF hero vs. 40–50% body reality**: Most product tiles show 40–50% OFF. The "up to 70%" claim in both the subject and hero reads as bait relative to what's actually displayed.
- **Five stacked product sections with no connective tissue**: Grid after grid after grid with no copy thread between them — it reads like a database export, not a curated send.
- **Free shipping buried in the footer**: "free & easy delivery over $35" is one of Wayfair's strongest conversion drivers and it's below the fold in small text.
- **Cadence fatigue is real**: The June 11 dressers, June 13 pools, June 13 rugs, June 14 beds — this is the fifth or sixth HOT SUMMER SALE send in the same week. "ENDS TOMORROW" urgency has been implied in each one.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Pay off the PSA subject with one line of real copy**: Open above the hero grid with a single editorial sentence — "Last call: everything you bookmarked this week is 40–70% off and gone at midnight." One sentence of voice earns the subject's promise.
- 2. **Lead with the actual 70% OFF products**: If real products at 70% exist, surface them in a dedicated hero row above the main grid. If they don't, drop the 70% hero claim to 50% and stop over-promising.
- 3. **Collapse to two product sections max**: One "flash deals" block and one "picked for you" block. Cut everything else. Fewer targets, more intent.
- 4. **Move free shipping into the hero row**: "$35 free shipping" alongside the discount callout — not the footer.
- 5. **Remove the app QR code from this email**: App acquisition is a retention play; a last-chance urgency email is a conversion play. The QR block is a distraction here.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Last call: your HOT SUMMER SALE ends tonight`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Up to 70% off disappears tomorrow — final picks`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Flash deals, picks made for you, and free shipping over $35 — going fast.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `These 40–70% off deals disappear tomorrow. Here's what's left.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Issue — Fixed pixel heights on mobile content containers will clip text:**

```css
.ProductRec-name { height: 48px !important }         /* + overflow: hidden */
.ProductRec-stars, .ProductRec-shippingCopy { height: 16px }
.ProductRec-price { height: 40px; vertical-align: top }
.ProductRec-salesCopy { height: 28px; vertical-align: top }
```

Combined with `overflow: hidden` on `.ProductRec-name`, any user with OS-level large-text accessibility settings or a system font larger than the design baseline will have product names, prices, and shipping copy silently clipped. These heights appear to be fixed to align a product grid; consider `min-height` instead.

**Issue — `word-break: break-all` on product names:**

```css
.ProductRec-name { word-break: break-all; overflow: hidden; display: block; height: 48px !important }
```

`break-all` splits at any character boundary, mid-word. Prefer `overflow-wrap: break-word` (`word-wrap: break-word` for legacy clients) which only breaks when a word cannot fit the line.

**Issue — Custom font is WOFF-only, no WOFF2:**

```css
@font-face {
  font-family: "Sofia";
  src: url(https://secure.img.wfrcdn.com/st4/stores/common/fonts/sofia/sofia_reg.woff) format("woff");
  mso-font-alt: "Arial"
}
```

No `format("woff2")` source listed. WOFF2 is ~30% smaller and supported by all modern email clients that render web fonts (Apple Mail, Samsung Mail). The `mso-font-alt: "Arial"` fallback is correct for Outlook. Low severity — most clients ignore `@font-face` entirely — but worth adding a WOFF2 source for clients that do honor it.

**Issue — Duplicate `@media` blocks for `.u-Button` and `.u-Button--primary`:**

Both classes receive identical padding declarations (`padding: 16px 0 !important` on the td, `padding: 0 32px !important` on the link) in separate media query blocks. This is redundant CSS weight but causes no rendering defect.
