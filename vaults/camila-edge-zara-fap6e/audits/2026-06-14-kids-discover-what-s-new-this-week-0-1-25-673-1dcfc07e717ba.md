# Kids | Discover what's new this week
**Score:** 5/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-14**
## Executive summary

- This is Zara's standard kids new-arrivals editorial send — a grid of six-plus summer lookbook images organized loosely by category (Little Girl, Summer Collection, Girl + Boy, and a few more labels that are barely legible at this size). The aesthetic is on-brand: bright natural light, outdoor settings, children styled in muted-but-summery tones. The problem is that the email is entirely visual inventory, zero commercial tension. There is no price, no discount, no sale window, no "shop before it's gone" — and the most prominent CTA in the entire email is "Download Our App," which is a retention play buried at the very bottom.
- Compared to the June 11 "THE FOLD-ER" send (scored 8/10), which used editorial structure with named contributors and actual narrative pull, this one has nothing to anchor a reader. It's a scrollable mood board that ends in an app download request. The Kids category also creates an immediate relevance filter — subscribers without young children have zero reason to engage.
- Judgement: this is a catalog distribution email dressed in editorial clothes. It will generate a browse session for highly motivated parents and nothing for anyone else.

- **Clean render** — no broken images, no overlapping text, grid layout is intact at this viewport.
- **Zara brand aesthetic is consistent** — minimal copy, editorial photography, whitespace; recognizably Zara at a glance.
- **Category segmentation visible** — section labels (LITTLE GIRL, GIRL + BOY, etc.) do create a light navigation structure within the email.

## What's weak

- **No offer whatsoever** — pure editorial with no price incentive, no urgency, no reason to click today vs. tomorrow vs. never.
- **"Download Our App" as the primary CTA is the wrong ask** — a parent browsing kids clothing does not want to be funneled to an app install; they want to shop.
- **Visual hierarchy is flat** — six-plus image blocks of roughly equal size compete for attention; there's no hero, no anchor, no pull toward a single action.
- **Category-level subject line ("Kids | Discover what's new this week") targets a subset of the list** — subscribers without children have no hook, and the subject doesn't acknowledge or reward parents who do.
- **Text labels on images are nearly illegible** at standard email widths — the section category names are very small and add no scannable value.

- 3. **Add a limited-time framing even without a discount** — "New this week — styles sell fast in summer" creates soft urgency without a markdown.
- 4. **Rework the subject to reward parents specifically** — the current subject is a generic inventory alert; make it feel like something arrived for their kid.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Three confirmed issues:**

**3a. Invalid `<div>` as direct child of `<table>` (structural defect)**
```html
<table role="presentation" width="640" ...>
  <!-- Copiar aquí -->
  <!--[if !mso 9]><!-->
  <div style="mso-hide:all;">   ← invalid: div cannot be child of table
    <tr>
      <td ...>
```
A `<div>` is not a permitted child of `<table>` in HTML. Browsers and email clients will relocate or discard it via error recovery, potentially breaking the preheader row entirely or producing phantom whitespace.

**3b. `line-height: auto` is an invalid CSS value**
```css
.heightmobile { height: auto!important; line-height: auto!important; }
```
`line-height: auto` is not a valid CSS value (valid: `normal`, unitless number, length, percentage). All clients will ignore this declaration. If the intent was `line-height: normal`, it should be corrected.

**3c. Viewport meta uses wrong separators and blocks zoom**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width; initial-scale=1.0; maximum-scale=1.0; user-scalable=no;">
```
- Properties must be comma-separated, not semicolons. Most clients parse this leniently, but it is malformed.
- `maximum-scale=1.0` + `user-scalable=no` prevents pinch-to-zoom. This violates WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text, Level AA) and is increasingly flagged by accessibility auditors.
