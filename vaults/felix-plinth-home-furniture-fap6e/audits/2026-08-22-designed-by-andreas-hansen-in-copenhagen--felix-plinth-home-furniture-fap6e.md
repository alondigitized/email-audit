# Designed by Andreas Hansen in Copenhagen
**Score:** 5/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-08-22**
## Executive summary

- Okay, CB2 again — and this time they've actually surprised me a little. After "Floor Sample Sale," "Just added to clearance," and "Future Heirlooms Under $500" the last three days in a row, I opened expecting another discount dump, and instead I got a whole design-history lesson: Andreas Hansen, Gianfranco Frattini, Paul McCobb, Steen Ostergaard, the Ackermans, Bill Curry. That's a genuinely different beat than anything else in my inbox this week.
- Problem is, it's buried under a banner screaming "ONE DAY LEFT — END OF SUMMER SALE" that has nothing to do with any of the six design legends scrolling underneath it. So which email is this — a sale email or an editorial one? It can't decide, and neither can I. The "Andreas Hansen" hook that got the subject line only gets about 15% of the actual scroll before it dissolves into a designer clip-show that reads more like a catalog history page than a targeted send.
- Would I click? Maybe, out of curiosity about the Hansen chair — it's a genuinely nice piece, mid-century, exactly my style. But I'm not forwarding this to a friend, and I'm definitely not clicking the sale banner up top since it never tells me what percentage or dollar amount I'm actually getting.

## What's working

- The Andreas Hansen chair photography is legit — moody, well-lit, looks like something out of a design magazine, not stock furniture-catalog stuff.
- Naming actual mid-century designers (Frattini, McCobb, Ostergaard) is a real point of differentiation from the C&B family's usual "just added" clearance blasts — this is closer to what Article did with the An Trieu home-tour send, just with more history and less lifestyle staging.
- The $5,000 Design Refresh sweepstakes block is a nice mid-scroll break, and it's clearly labeled as a sweepstakes, not disguised as a discount.
- "MORE FOR YOU" personalized product grid at the bottom is genuinely useful — armless chairs, dining tables, credenzas all in my wheelhouse.

## What's weak

- The hero banner ("ONE DAY LEFT — END OF SUMMER SALE") and the entire rest of the email (design legends) are two completely disconnected campaigns crammed into one send — no percentage, no dollar figure, no actual offer visible anywhere near that banner.
- Six designer bios in a row is a lot to scroll through on a phone before you hit anything shoppable — by designer #4 (Ostergaard) I've lost the thread of why I opened this.
- No pricing anywhere on the Hansen chair or any of the legend pieces — I have to click through blind, which after CB2's fourth-plus email this week, I'm not doing.
- Cadence fatigue is real: this is CB2 again on top of "Future Heirlooms" yesterday and "Floor Sample Sale" the day before — my brain is fully in C&B-family snooze mode at this point, same as I flagged on the "NOW & FUTURE CLASSICS" send.

## Recommendations

- 1. Split this into two emails — one sale-focused with an actual percentage/dollar offer, one design-story send about Andreas Hansen alone. Trying to do both in one scroll dilutes the sale urgency and buries the one genuinely interesting story (Hansen) under five other designers nobody asked for in this send.
- 2. Cut the legends list from six designers to two, max — Hansen (the subject-line hook) and one contrast piece. Six bios reads like a museum wall placard, not a shoppable email.
- 3. Put a price or "from $X" under the Andreas Hansen chair photo — right now there's zero pricing signal on the hero product.
- 4. Drop the "END OF SUMMER SALE" banner if there's no visible offer, or replace it with the actual percentage — a banner with no number is just noise at this cadence.

**2. Link & Tracking Issues**
- 67 tracking/click-redirect links were skipped by the automated HTTP probe (tracked via `mi.cb2.com` and similar redirect domains) — not independently verified as live, but this is expected behavior for click-tracked ESP links and not itself a defect.

Mixed-content HTTP images in an HTML email can be blocked or stripped by mail clients (notably Outlook/Office365 and Gmail proxy in strict environments), silently breaking that pixel's tracking.
