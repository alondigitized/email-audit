# FREE design help for warm-weather hosting
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-23**
## Executive summary

- Thirty-six. I know it's thirty-six because I literally counted my Pottery Barn folder last week. And in a pile that has been almost exclusively "UP TO 70% OFF" and "rugs to ground your space" and "nightstands with storage" since June 20, this one actually made me stop. The sender is "Pottery Barn Design Services" — a sub-brand, technically — and for once they're not shouting a sale number at me. They're offering something different: free design consultations framed around warm-weather hosting. That's a genuine pattern break after five consecutive sale blasts.
- Here's the honest tension: I'm mid-renovation on two houses simultaneously, which means I have been quietly daydreaming about calling an actual design consultant for months. This email reaches me at exactly the right moment in life. But "warm-weather hosting" is not where my head is at right now. I'm thinking about furniture layouts, bedroom storage, living room anchor pieces, traffic flow — the structural decisions. The hosting vignettes (tablescape, planters, alfresco lounge) feel like they're talking to the person I'll be in October, not the person I am today who is arguing with a contractor about crown molding.
- I'd click the BOOK A FREE APPOINTMENT button. I would not forward this to a friend. The offer is useful to me specifically; it's not the kind of "oh you have to see this" email.

## What's working

- **The hero pulls hard**: "RSVP for Free Design Services" feels like an invitation rather than a broadcast. After 36 PB emails, that's legitimately refreshing.
- **Pattern break in a fatigued inbox**: Every prior send this month has been a sale. This one sells a service. My brain registered the difference immediately, which is most of the battle.
- **Three-vignette structure works**: Lounge comforts → planters → alfresco tablescape tells a coherent warm-weather hosting story. It's not a random product dump.
- **"RECOMMENDED JUST FOR YOU" section**: Product cards at the bottom show some personalization logic is running, which signals they're treating me like a known customer.
- **Clean layout, no visible render bugs**: No broken images, no overlapping text, no lorem ipsum. After some of the Wayfair emoji-grenade subject lines I've been processing, clean is a genuine win.

## What's weak

- **"Warm-weather hosting" is the wrong angle for someone mid-renovation**: I'm not hosting yet. I'm buying. Article's "Bedroom storage essentials" from earlier this week was blunt and aimed exactly at my current problem. This email is aimed at who I want to be in three months.
- **Multiple BOOK NOW buttons across three vignettes dilutes the ask**: One clear appointment-booking CTA would be stronger than three "BOOK NOW" buttons plus a second "BOOK A FREE APPOINTMENT" at the bottom. The repetition makes it feel like they expect me to bounce off the first one.
- **No specifics on what a design appointment actually means**: Is this in-store? Virtual? 30 minutes? Can I bring floor plans? The email assumes I already know how PB design services work, and I don't.
- **Botanical/tablescape imagery skews lifestyle-domestic**: It's not off-putting, but the visual language here talks more to the person setting a dinner table than the person who is trying to figure out if a sectional will fit around a load-bearing wall.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Reframe the copy for the renovation customer, not the hosting customer**: Lead with "planning a room from scratch?" or "two spaces, one vision?" — something that meets me where I actually am. The free consultation is valuable regardless of the season; the warm-weather framing is unnecessarily narrow.
- 2. **Explain the service**: One line under the hero — "Virtual or in-store, 45 minutes, your floor plan welcome" — would remove every friction point I have about clicking.
- 3. **Collapse to one strong CTA**: Three "BOOK NOW" buttons plus a "BOOK A FREE APPOINTMENT" is four asks. Pick one CTA and make it hit harder.
- 4. **Suppress or reframe for customers currently in sale mode**: I've been conditioned by five consecutive 70%-off emails. A sudden shift to "book a service" with no product offer underneath it feels slightly disconnected from the sale cadence.

### 2. Link & Tracking Issues

**[CRITICAL] PHP header() calls inside `<style>` block**
The second `<style>` tag in `<head>` begins with raw PHP:
```
header('Content-Type: image/jpeg');
header("Cache-Control: no-store, no-cache, must-revalidate, max-age=0");
header("Cache-Control: post-check=0, pre-check=0", false);
header("Pragma: no-cache");
```
This is server-side PHP code that was not executed — it was emitted literally into the CSS. It is likely a dynamic image-serving snippet accidentally merged into the email template. It is syntactically invalid CSS and will be ignored by mail clients, but it indicates a broken template pipeline that could mask other substitution failures.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

Every content image, all footer slice images (`slices_01.jpg` through `slices_18.jpg`), product thumbnails, and tracking pixels have empty or absent `alt` attributes. With images blocked (common in B2B clients, Outlook by default), the email renders as a blank page with no informational fallback.

The nav/header image at `edm.potterybarn.com/pb/2016/nav/nav0.jpg` does have `alt="POTTERY BARN"` — the only image with populated alt text.

**[FAIL] Two images served over HTTP**

Mixed-content blocking in Gmail, Apple Mail, and Outlook will suppress these images silently. Both appear to be legacy credit card banner assets.

**[INFO] Breakpoint inconsistency**
Two separate `@media` blocks use different breakpoints: `max-width: 480px` (first block, `table[class="wrapper"]`) and `max-width: 650px` (second block, `.mobile-hide`/`.mobile-show`). This creates a gap window (481px–650px) where mobile hiding logic fires but the wrapper width is not constrained.

**[INFO] Attribute selector CSS pattern**
Responsive styles use `table[class="wrapper"]` (exact match) rather than `.wrapper`. This is a legacy Gmail hack; it breaks if the element ever has multiple classes.
