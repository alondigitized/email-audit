# STEALS OF THE WEEK
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-05-31**
## Executive summary

- Coming off yesterday's AD100 editorial pivot — which I genuinely respected as CB2 saying "we are a design brand, not just a sale machine" — this send lands and immediately cancels that energy. "STEALS OF THE WEEK" is the most generic subject they could have written. No category. No percentage. No reason to stop scrolling. I'm on email fourteen-plus from the CB2/Crate & Barrel family since mid-May, and my patience for "just trust us, it's a steal" is gone.
- Inside, the structure is honest enough: Midseason Sale hero, outdoor at 40% off, something up to 60% off, then a design services pitch. Four distinct modules, four separate decisions. Compare this to "OUTDOOR IS ON SALE" — which I gave a 9 and which I clicked the same day — because that email did exactly one thing loudly. Here I'm doing inventory management. The single most compelling number in this whole email, the 60% off, is buried *third*. I still don't know what it covers. That's the failure this email doesn't recover from.
- I'd flag this in my Pinterest-adjacent folder and revisit it mid-week when I'm back in buying mode. I would not click today and I would not forward it.

## What's working

- **Midseason Sale hero is clean** — neutral-toned sofa imagery, modern palette, no clutter at the top. Fits my aesthetic and renders sharp.
- **Outdoor at 40% off is timely** — I'm actively sourcing outdoor pieces for the second home right now, and this module lands at the right moment in the season.
- **The "up to 60% off" block is genuinely interesting** — if it covers furniture categories I'd consider, this changes my week. The number itself earns attention even buried third.
- **Complimentary interior design + $1,000 credit is differentiated** — Pottery Barn's recent barrage has been all "ready to ship!" logistics copy. CB2 pitching design expertise is a real point of distinction. I noticed it.
- **"More for You" product grid at the bottom** — shows they're using browse or purchase data. The variety is good and it's where I actually spent time scanning.

## What's weak

- **"STEALS OF THE WEEK" is a dead subject line** — compare Crate & Barrel's "Outdoor furniture. Up to 30% off. Your oasis is calling →" which told me the category, the number, and the benefit before I ever opened. This tells me nothing.
- **Four stacked offers shred the hierarchy** — "OUTDOOR IS ON SALE" worked because it didn't blink. This one blinks four times.
- **60% off buried third is a real mistake** — that's the loudest number in the email and it doesn't appear in the subject, the hero, or even second. If this block were the hero, this is a completely different email.
- **"Up to 60% off" with no category name** — if that's furniture I'm calling the store. If it's throw pillows I'm closing the tab. I cannot act without knowing.
- **The design services module is disconnected** — a $1,000 credit pitch deserves its own editorial send, the kind of treatment yesterday's AD100 email built toward. Dropping it fourth in a deal stack buries what could be the highest-intent offer for someone mid-renovation.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Lead with the 60% off and name the category** — make it the hero. "Up to 60% off [sofas / case goods / lighting] — this week only" turns a buried signal into the reason to open.
- 2. **One offer per email** — the kitchen-sink approach is why people unsubscribe. Pick the 60% story or the outdoor story, not both plus design services plus a general midseason tag.
- 3. **Give design services its own send** — standalone "Free design session: $1,000 credit toward your renovation" email aimed at customers who've recently browsed large-ticket items. That's a genuinely differentiated pitch versus what Article, Pottery Barn, or Wayfair is doing right now.
- 4. **Put a deadline on "STEALS OF THE WEEK"** — "ends Sunday" or "ends June 1" turns a passive folder-flag into an action.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Up to 60% off [category] — plus outdoor from 40%`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Midseason: 60% off [category], outdoor deals inside`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `This week's biggest cuts — plus a complimentary $1,000 design session`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `60% off ends Sunday. Free interior design consult for your next room.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

This production artifact was not removed before send. Also contains a typo (`IMPUT` → `INPUT`). No functional breakage, but exposes internal build tooling in public source.

**[MEDIUM] Conflicting `img` max-width media queries:**
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img { max-width: 100% !important } }
@media only screen and (min-width: 690px) { img { max-width: 690px } }
```
At viewport widths ≥ 768px, both rules match. The second rule (690px cap) is declared after the first and carries equal specificity, so it wins — overriding the `100%` rule at all desktop widths. The `768px` block is effectively dead for `img`. Intended behavior is unclear; if images should be uncapped on wide desktop, the order or specificity needs correction.

**[MEDIUM] Mobile breakpoint at `max-width: 418px`:**
The responsive block triggers only below 418px. This misses common viewport widths (419–767px) including landscape phone and many Android devices, which receive neither desktop nor mobile styles.

**[LOW] `maximum-scale=1` in viewport meta:**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Suppresses user-initiated zoom on mobile. Fails WCAG 2.1 SC 1.4.4 (Resize Text). Common in email but worth flagging for accessibility posture.

**[LOW] Global text-size-adjust disabled:**
```css
* { -webkit-text-size-adjust: none; -ms-text-size-adjust: none; }
```
Applied universally via the `*` selector. Disabling on `body` only is safer and less likely to cause rendering artifacts in non-email contexts.

**[LOW] Duplicate `@font-face` for "Lineto Brown Sub":**
The family is declared three separate times — once with `.eot`/`.woff2`/`.woff` sources, and twice more with individual `.woff2` files at `font-weight: 400` and `600`. The first declaration lacks weight/style descriptors, creating ambiguity in font matching. Consolidate into a single, well-described face-per-weight pattern.
