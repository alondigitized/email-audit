# See our new & exclusive Nuna clothing collection!
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-05-30**
## Executive summary

- Twenty. That's where the Crate & Barrel family sits in my inbox this week, and this one is the clearest evidence yet that their segmentation is completely busted. I renovate homes. I buy sofas and pendant lights and tile samples. I have never, in the history of my relationship with this brand, bought a baby onesie. And yet here I am, looking at an infant in a linen romper next to a soft-colored wardrobe display. The "Nuna" brand I know makes strollers and car seats — and apparently now they make clothing sold exclusively through Crate & Kids. Fine. That's a business decision. What's not fine is that this email landed in my inbox at all.
- The email itself is clean. Soft neutrals, good hierarchy, the photography is warm and appealing — if you're the person this was meant for. There's a stroller grid further down, which at least acknowledges Nuna's actual brand equity. But there are no prices in the hero, no discount, no reason to act now — just a "new collection" announcement for a category I don't shop.
- I'm marking this read and moving on. I would not click, I would not forward. If anything, this one accelerates how close I am to hitting unsubscribe on the Crate & Kids sub-list entirely.

## What's working

- **Clean visual execution.** Soft pinks and warm taupes, no render bugs, photography is well-lit and styled — if I were the target audience, I'd trust this brand immediately.
- **Sender brand is recognizable.** I know Crate & Kids is the C&B family and I do shop the parent brand, so the trust transfer is intact.
- **Nuna stroller grid lower in the email is the right call.** Nuna has real brand equity in gear; using clothing as a halo and pointing back to strollers at least acknowledges the broader product story.
- **Subject under 50 characters.** "See our new & exclusive Nuna clothing collection!" hits 49 — renders intact on mobile, doesn't truncate.

## What's weak

- **Complete demographic mismatch.** I'm a 36-year-old male renovating two homes. Every image in this email is a parent with an infant. The brand has never sold me kids' clothing because I don't buy kids' clothing. This is a segmentation failure, full stop.
- **No offer, no reason to act.** It's a new collection announcement with no price, no introductory discount, no free shipping threshold — nothing to act on even if I were vaguely curious. CB2's "OUTDOOR IS ON SALE" email earlier this week had a concrete reason to click within the first scroll; this has none.
- **Cadence fatigue is real.** This is the 20th Crate & Barrel family email I've seen this week. The "Selling fast: up to 60% off clearance!" Crate & Kids send from earlier today already tested my patience. Two Crate & Kids sends on the same day is aggressive; the second one being for a category I don't shop makes it worse.
- **"New & exclusive" is not a value proposition.** Pottery Barn led with "Starting at $179" in their Coastal Washed Quilt send — that's an anchor. This has no price, no scarcity, no deadline. "Exclusive" means nothing to me without context.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Suppress this send to non-parent subscribers.** This is a segmentation problem, not a creative problem. If Crate & Kids can identify accounts with no historical kids' category purchases, those accounts should not receive clothing sends. Full stop. The creative is fine — it just shouldn't have reached me.
- 2. **Add a launch price or introductory offer to the hero.** "New collection, starting at $X" or "Free shipping on your first Nuna Wardrobe order" gives me a reason to click even if I'm casually curious. Compare to Wayfair's table lamp send this week — they led with "UP TO 60% OFF," which is blunt but effective. No price = no action.
- 3. **Lead the hero with the stroller/gear angle for crossover subscribers.** Nuna gear is the hook for the crossover customer (someone in the C&B ecosystem who might have a baby). Clothing-first buries that.
- 4. **Dial back the C&B family cadence generally.** Twenty sends in one week from one brand family is eroding trust fast. Batch or suppress non-sale sends.

- **Preheader Alt A:** `Soft, thoughtfully made pieces starting at $XX — shop the launch.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `The brand behind your stroller just dropped a wardrobe. Here's what's new.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**`maximum-scale=1` disables user zoom**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Prevents pinch-to-zoom on mobile; WCAG 1.4.4 failure. Remove `maximum-scale=1`.

**Global `-webkit-text-size-adjust: none` on `*`**
```css
*{-webkit-text-size-adjust:none; -ms-text-size-adjust:none; ...}
```
Blocks iOS automatic text resizing for all elements. The later client CSS block correctly applies it only to `body`; the earlier rule on `*` undermines that.

**Conflicting desktop `img` max-width queries — cascade bug**
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img {max-width: 600px} }  /* earlier */
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img {max-width: 640px} }  /* later — wins at 768px+ */
```
At ≥768px viewports both rules match; the 640px rule is declared last and overrides the 768px rule, so `max-width: 640px` applies everywhere instead of the intended 600px cap.

**`<title>` mismatch — wrong brand**
```html
<title>Crate & Barrel</title>
```
Email is from "Crate & Kids"; the title tag names the parent brand. Affects screen reader announcements and some preview pane renderers.

**Duplicate `format-detection` meta tags (4×)**
```html
<meta name="format-detection" content="telephone=no,address=no,email=no,date=no,url=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="date=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="address=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="telephone=no">
```
The combined tag is sufficient; the three trailing singles are redundant noise.

**Duplicate `<meta charset>`**
```html
<meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />
<meta charset="utf-8">
```
Both are present. One is sufficient; some parsers may flag the redundancy.

**Global `line-height: 100%` on `*`**
```css
*{... line-height: 100%; ...}
```
Applied universally, this collapses multi-line text in several email clients (notably older Outlook). Typically scoped only to `.ExternalClass` children.

**Duplicated `.ReadMsgBody` / `.ExternalClass` rules across two `<style>` blocks**
Rules for both selectors appear in both the first and second `<style>` block with partially different declarations. Cascade order depends on block position; no explicit conflicts exist here, but dead rules add parse weight and maintenance risk.
