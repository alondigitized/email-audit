# ON NOW! Up to 50% off furniture!
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-28**
## Executive summary

- Thirty-six. Or thirty-seven. I've genuinely lost track of where the C&B family sits in my June inbox, and the reason I know this is an exact repeat is because I opened "It's here! Up to 50% off our 4th of July Warehouse Sale" from *this same sender* earlier today. That email had the same discount, the same campaign, and a subject line that was one synonym away from this one. C&B sent me two emails in the same morning to tell me the same sale exists.
- What saves it — partially — is that when you get past the dupe fatigue and actually scroll, the email is doing the right things. Living room hero, neutral linen palette, sofa front-and-center. A dining vignette mid-scroll that is genuinely relevant to me right now since the second house still doesn't have a table. The "we love these for you" block near the bottom has specific pieces that look like someone either ran a browse-history algo or made a solid editorial call. Either way, it's more targeted than anything Wayfair has served me this month.
- But I'm not clicking. I'm marking this read, possibly screenshotting the sideboard for my designer, and opening CB2's "Furniture on sale right now" from this week instead — that one ran the same discount category with half the noise and a tighter grid. Might forward the dining vignette image to my partner. That's the ceiling here.

## What's working

- **Furniture in the hero, no detours.** No glassware, no throw pillows as the lede — sofa up top, furniture in the subject line. That's what I opened it for.
- **Discount visible before the first scroll.** "Up to 50% off furniture" is in the hero text, not buried below a lifestyle paragraph. That's the move.
- **The dining-table vignette mid-email is timely.** Dark stained wood, clean lines — I've been shopping this exact category for the second property and it showed up without me having to hunt for it.
- **"We love these for you" gives the email a personalized floor.** Whether it's algorithmic or just editorial curation, specific product thumbnails beat a generic "Shop All Furniture" button.
- **Clean render throughout.** No broken images, no layout collapse, no lorem ipsum making it through QA.

## What's weak

- **This is the second "up to 50% off furniture" email from this sender today.** Changing the subject from "It's here! Up to 50% off our 4th of July Warehouse Sale" to "ON NOW! Up to 50% off furniture!" is not sending a different email, it's relabeling the same one.
- **"ON NOW!" in all caps reads like a clearance-bin subject line.** C&B shoots for aspirational shelter-mag energy and then opens with copy that belongs on a TJ Maxx circular. Article's 4th of July send kept the urgency without screaming.
- **No expiry date visible anywhere in the email.** "ON NOW!" implies I should act, but if there's no "ends July 4 at midnight" anchor, I'll assume it runs until they tell me otherwise and deprioritize the click.
- **No loyalty or rewards hook.** I'm on a high-rewards card and I optimize every major purchase. Not a single mention of a C&B Rewards program, a Design Trade angle, or any member benefit across the several June sends I've reviewed.
- **The product grid gets crowded below the hero.** Pottery Barn's Design Services email on the same day ran fewer categories with more breathing room; this one tries to show everything and loses the hierarchy by the third scroll.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Enforce a one-send-per-day rule during sale windows.** If the morning email is the sale launch and the afternoon email is a "reminder," that's a retention problem waiting to happen. Pick one creative per day.
- 2. **Add a hard sale end date above the fold.** "Ends July 4" in the hero converts better than "ON NOW!" in the subject. CB2's tightest sends in this category always close with a deadline.
- 3. **Pin one hero product with a before/after price.** Article anchored a specific sofa SKU in their June 19 Cillian send and it made me look up the piece. A lifestyle shot is pretty; a priced product is a hook.
- 4. **Surface the expiry date in the preheader** so inbox clients show it before the open — images don't render in preview panes, text does.
- 5. **Audit the sender name cadence.** "Crate & Barrel SALE" is fine for pure promo; when the send has editorial content (the rug materials piece, the DesignDesk section), the "SALE" label trains me to skip it.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Confirmed issues:**

**3a. Duplicate and conflicting image `max-width` media queries**
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) {
  img { max-width: 600px }
}
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) {
  img { max-width: 640px }   /* overrides the 768px rule at all widths ≥ 640px */
}
```
The `min-width: 640px` rule appears after `min-width: 768px` in the same `<style>` block. At viewport widths ≥ 768px, both rules match and `max-width: 640px` wins due to cascade order, capping images at 640px rather than the intended 600px on wide viewports. If the template container is 600px, images will be allowed to expand to 640px on desktop clients that honor these queries (e.g., Apple Mail), potentially breaking layout.

**3b. Unresolvable zoom restriction**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
`maximum-scale=1` prevents pinch-to-zoom on iOS/Android. This is a WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text) violation and is blocked by iOS Safari 10+ regardless — but the declaration signals an accessibility gap in the build process.

**3c. Redundant `format-detection` meta tags**
The combined suppression tag and three individual tags are all present:
```html
<meta name="format-detection" content="telephone=no,address=no,email=no,date=no,url=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="date=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="address=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="telephone=no">
```
Functionally harmless but adds ~200 bytes of head bloat and indicates copy-paste template debt.

**3d. Duplicate charset declaration**
```html
<meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />
<meta charset="utf-8">
```
Both are present. Redundant; the `http-equiv` form is the XHTML-compliant version and the standalone `charset` is HTML5. No rendering defect, but flagged as template sloppiness.

**3e. Global `line-height: 100%` override**
```css
* { line-height: 100%; }
```
Applied via the universal selector with `!important` implications from surrounding rules. This suppresses line-height inheritance and can compress multi-line text rendering in Gmail Web and some Android clients. Typically overridden per-element in well-structured templates, but risky as a global default.
