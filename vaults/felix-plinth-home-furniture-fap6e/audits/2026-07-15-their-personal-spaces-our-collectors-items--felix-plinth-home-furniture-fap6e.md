# Their personal spaces. Our collectors' items.
**Score:** 6/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-15**
## Executive summary

- Twenty-one. That's where CB2 sits in my July inbox after this one lands, and I'm counting because at some point the counting is the only thing that keeps me from hitting "unsubscribe" purely out of spite. The subject "Their personal spaces. Our collectors' items." is genuinely the best copy CB2 has written all month — it made me pause in a way that "GET IT ON SALE" on July 13th and the all-caps "NEW COLLECTION" on July 14th absolutely did not. The editorial voice, the first-person structure, the suggestion of curation — I opened it.
- And then the email does something that drives me insane: it stacks three completely different campaigns into one scroll. There's a hero editorial about artists and creatives who love CB2. Fine. Then immediately below it, a Summer Edit sale banner with up to 40% off. Then a $5,000 Refresh sweepstakes with a free design appointment entry. These are not one story. These are three separate marketing meetings that got stapled together, and the subject line — which sold me on "collectors' items" — does nothing to prepare me for a discount sale banner and a contest entry CTA.
- I would not click anything meaningful here. I'd read the hero image, feel mildly curious, scroll once, hit the sale banner and go "okay this is just another promo," and mark it read. Definitely not forwarding to anyone.

## What's working

- The subject line is the best copywriting CB2 has produced all month — "Their personal spaces. Our collectors' items." has actual editorial tension and made me genuinely curious who "they" are.
- The hero image is beautifully shot — the warm interior with the portrait on the wall is aspirational without feeling staged, and the styling reads like something I'd actually want in the second home.
- The "DISCOVER" section framing around artists and creatives is smart positioning — it leans into the collector identity the subject line promised, and the color palette is sophisticated enough to match.
- The Summer Edit "UP TO 40% OFF" offer is concrete and visible — when the sale does appear, it's readable and not buried.

## What's weak

- Three narrative pillars — editorial/collectors story, Summer Edit sale, $5,000 sweepstakes — in one email actively undermine each other. The subject line sells "collectors' items," the body sells "40% off," the bottom sells "win a prize." These are three different emails.
- The $5,000 Refresh sweepstakes feels like a paste-in from a separate campaign entirely — it breaks the visual flow and the editorial register the top half established.
- The product grid ("MORE FOR YOU") at the bottom is too small and scattered to read on mobile; the pricing is barely legible at thumbnail scale, and the items look generic next to the editorial hero.
- The 40% off sale banner actively undercuts the "collectors' items" positioning — people curating meaningful pieces don't want to feel like they're clearing warehouse stock.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Pick one story per send.** The subject line earned an open with the editorial/collector angle — commit to that. Run the artist profiles, the product-in-context shots, the "why creatives choose CB2" narrative. Save the Summer Edit sale for a separate email that owns that positioning honestly.
- 2. **Cut the sweepstakes entirely from this send** or make it a dedicated campaign. The $5,000 Refresh is a real hook for someone renovating two homes simultaneously — I'd actually engage with that — but it's buried after two other competing messages and reads like an afterthought.
- 3. **Make the "MORE FOR YOU" grid shoppable at a glance** — larger thumbnails, legible price points, and one clear category anchor (e.g., "pieces for a living room like the one above") so the product section earns the scroll.
- 4. **Surface specific products in the editorial section.** Article's July 11th entryway email named actual pieces in editorial context and scored well because it connected the story to the shop. Right now the hero just says "FIND OUT WHY" — find out why what? Give me one product name.

**`maximum-scale=1` viewport:**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Disables pinch-to-zoom on mobile. WCAG 1.4.4 failure for users with low vision. This is common in email but worth flagging given the subscriber base.

**Global `-webkit-text-size-adjust: none`:**
```css
*{-webkit-text-size-adjust:none; -ms-text-size-adjust:none; ...}
```
Applied via universal selector, this prevents OS-level text scaling across all elements. Compounds the `maximum-scale=1` issue for accessibility.

The first two declarations share the same family name with no weight discriminator. Browsers process these in order; the final rule wins, making the initial two declarations dead weight that add parse time without effect.
