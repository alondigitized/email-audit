# Let’s make your bathroom work for you →
**Score:** 6/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-05-20**
## Executive summary

- Sixteen. Or seventeen. I've genuinely stopped keeping count, and I'd be lying if I said I opened this one with any goodwill left in the tank after the cookware email that landed this same morning — same arrow glyph in the subject, same send window. At some point the C&B family starts to feel less like a brand I follow and more like a neighbor who knocks every twenty minutes.
- That said: bathroom. I'm renovating two bathrooms simultaneously across two properties, and a well-timed bathroom email from a brand I trust is actually the rare overlap between what they're selling and what I need right now. Crate & Barrel main — not C&B Kids, not the third Memorial Day blast — talking to me about storage and vanity organization is the right idea. But the execution here is a tall-stack scroll with what looks like storage cabinetry leading the hero and then a bunch of loosely related home content trailing below, which dilutes the premise fast.
- I'd mark as read. Maybe revisit on a weekend when I'm in renovation planning mode. I would not click today, and I would not forward — I'd feel guilty passing this volume to a friend.

## What's working

- **Bathroom relevance is real for me right now.** Two homes, two bathrooms in progress — this subject hit the one actual need I have this week that C&B can address.
- **C&B main, not C&B Kids.** After two C&B Kids emails that had zero bearing on my life, landing back on the mothership brand is itself a small win.
- **Layout is clean and bug-free.** No overlapping text, no broken image placeholders, no lorem ipsum. The tall shelving/vanity storage unit in the hero renders properly and communicates the category immediately.
- **Multiple product blocks are structured.** The grid sections below the hero give me multiple shots at relevance — closet org, accent pieces, bedroom — which is how I actually browse C&B in catalog mode.

## What's weak

- **The "→" arrow is doing a lot of repeated lifting.** The cookware email this morning used the exact same arrow convention in the subject. At this send cadence it signals template fatigue, not intentional voice.
- **The hero stopper is more "storage" than "bathroom."** What I see reads more like a bookcase or tall shelving unit than a vanity or bath-specific solution. For someone actively pricing out bathroom fixtures, that's a mismatch in the first half-second.
- **Offer specificity isn't legible.** The sender label says "Crate & Barrel SALE" but I can't see a concrete percentage or dollar threshold anchored to anything in the hero. Article opened their Memorial Day blast this week with a specific number front and center; CB2 did the same with "NOW & FUTURE CLASSICS ON SALE." This email makes me hunt.
- **Lower-scroll content strays far from the premise.** By the time I hit the bedroom and broader home sections, the "make your bathroom work" hook has evaporated. Pick a lane or give me a navigation row that makes the drift feel intentional.
- **Cadence is genuinely eroding trust.** Pottery Barn sent fifteen in four days; C&B is matching that pace. This isn't a knock on this specific email — it's a knock on whoever controls the send calendar.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Lock the offer to a number in the hero.** "Up to 40% off bath storage" in the hero subhead — something that makes me stop scrolling before I hit the bedroom section. This is the single highest-leverage change.
- 2. **Swap the subject arrow for actual specificity.** The "→" arrow after every subject feels canned after the cookware email this morning. A specific product type or saving amount would do more work.
- 3. **Trim the scroll or add a nav row.** If the email is going to span bathroom → closet → bedroom → beyond, give me a section nav at the top so I can jump. CB2's recent "NOW & FUTURE CLASSICS" email kept the drift from feeling random by anchoring everything to "your home, curated." This one doesn't earn the multi-category wander.
- 4. **Rest the cadence.** I know this is a calendar problem not a design problem, but the brand history context matters: I've been auditing these for days and even when they're good I'm less likely to click purely from send-fatigue.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**`maximum-scale=1` in viewport meta** — prevents users from pinching to zoom on mobile. WCAG 1.4.4 failure.
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```

**Conflicting `img` max-width media queries** — the `min-width: 768px → 600px` rule is dead code. The later `min-width: 640px → 640px` declaration overrides it at all widths ≥ 640px (CSS cascade, equal specificity, last declaration wins). Net effect: images cap at 640px on desktop, not 600px.
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img {max-width: 600px} }  /* overridden */
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img {max-width: 640px} }  /* wins */
```

**Global `p { text-align: center }` reset** — forces center-alignment on all paragraphs, including any hidden preheader text. Can break left-aligned body copy if paragraph tags are used in content cells.

**Global `* { -webkit-text-size-adjust: none }` with `line-height: 100%`** — applied to all elements via the universal selector. `line-height: 100%` on non-text elements (tables, images) causes rendering inconsistencies in Gmail and Outlook on Windows.

**No dark mode support** — `color-scheme` and `supported-color-schemes` are both locked to `light`. Background colors set to `#e6e7e8` (light gray) will invert unpredictably in Apple Mail dark mode.

**Duplicate `format-detection` meta tags** — `telephone=no` and `address=no` each appear twice. Redundant but harmless; indicates template hygiene issues.
