# Summer’s heating up! Shop up to 60% off →
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-17**
## Executive summary

- Email number nine from the Crate & Barrel family in roughly thirty hours. Not exaggerating — I checked. And this one is doing the classic Hail Mary: lob a 60%-off headline at the top, then jam in a sweepstakes, a Le Creuset callout, a dining table module, and an app promo before you hit the footer. It's not *bad* — C&B's production quality is consistently solid — but it reads like a marketing team that couldn't agree on a single message so they shipped four of them. The summer sale hero is genuinely interesting for me (I'm actively sourcing pieces for two renovations), but the sweepstakes module immediately undercuts the authority. I went from "okay, 60% off, I might look" to "wait, enter to win $200?" The tonal whiplash is real.
- Compare this to the Pottery Barn color story email I got earlier this week — the one anchored around indigo blue across every room. That had *editorial discipline*. One lens, one POV, products woven in naturally. This feels like three departments with competing slide decks. Would I click? Probably yes, barely, on the 60% off hero alone — but only because I have specific gaps to fill. Would I forward it? No chance. It's not curated enough to be worth sharing.

## What's working

- **Hero offer lands hard and fast.** "Up to 60% off" in large text, above the fold, coastal imagery behind it — it registers immediately without hunting.
- **Recognized sender with editorial context.** "Crate & Barrel SALE" in the from field does exactly what it should; I know what I'm opening before I open it.
- **Le Creuset callout is a smart secondary.** 20% off select Le Creuset breaks the furniture monotony and it's a credible, specific sub-offer — not just more sofas.
- **Compact dining table module is actually relevant.** I'm actively looking at dining situations for the second home. Surfacing a curated "compact dining" story mid-email earns points even if it's buried.
- **App/rewards mentioned.** Points to C&B knowing I track loyalty — even if it's footer territory.

## What's weak

- **The sweepstakes module killed my momentum.** I went from "summer sale, maybe" to "enter for a chance to win $200" in about three seconds. That's a mass-market, email-list-building tactic, not something for a customer who's spent real money at this brand. Article would never.
- **"Up to 60% off" is the oldest trick in the book.** I know going in that one discontinued accent pillow is 60% off and the sofa I actually want is 15% off. Wayfair runs the same "wall of percentages" playbook and I'm conditioned to distrust the headline number immediately.
- **Four distinct offers, zero coherent narrative.** Summer sale → sweepstakes → Le Creuset → dining tables → app. No through-line. This is the CB2 "NOW & FUTURE CLASSICS ON SALE" problem — something for everyone means nothing for me specifically.
- **Cadence is at the breaking point.** This is the ninth email in the C&B ecosystem in two days. Each individual email might be passable; the aggregate is noise.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Kill the sweepstakes module or move it to a dedicated send.** It cheapens the brand and fractures attention. If they want to run a contest, it earns its own email.
- 2. **Anchor the hero on a category, not a blanket percent.** "Up to 60% off living + dining" — or better, name a specific piece — for someone like me in active renovation mode. This is exactly what Pottery Barn's indigo color story did well: one focused angle.
- 3. **Add a deadline.** "Summer sale" without an end date is toothless. Even "ends Sunday" would convert me faster.
- 4. **Lead the dining table module higher.** If compact dining is the story worth telling, lead with it. It's the most credible editorial choice in the email and it's buried in the back half.
- 5. **Subject Alt A:** `60% off furniture. Summer sale, open now.`
- 6. **Subject Alt B:** `The summer sale is live — save up to 60%`
- 7. **Preheader Alt A:** `Outdoor seating, dining tables, Le Creuset — all on sale through the weekend.`
- 8. **Preheader Alt B:** `The pieces you've been watching just got cheaper. Shop the Summer Sale.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Issue 1 — Conflicting desktop image breakpoints (likely rendering bug)**

Two media queries in `<head>` set conflicting `max-width` values for `img` on desktop:

```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img { max-width: 640px } }
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img { max-width: 600px } }
```

At 640–767 px (tablet landscape / small desktop), `max-width: 640px` applies. At 768 px+, `max-width: 600px` overrides it — images become **40 px narrower as the viewport widens**. This is almost certainly unintentional and will produce layout narrowing on standard 768 px+ desktop renders in WebKit/Blink preview panes (Apple Mail, Gmail web).

**Issue 2 — `maximum-scale=1` disables pinch-to-zoom**

```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```

Prevents user zoom. Violates WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text, Level AA). Several major email clients (iOS Mail) respect this tag.

**Issue 3 — Global `-webkit-text-size-adjust: none`**

```css
* { -webkit-text-size-adjust: none; ... }
```

Applied to all elements. Blocks OS-level font scaling for low-vision users on mobile Safari. Use `-webkit-text-size-adjust: 100%` (present on `body` separately but overridden by the `*` rule due to specificity).

**Issue 4 — Global `p { text-align: center }`**

All `<p>` elements are center-aligned by default. Any plain-text fallback content or dynamically injected copy that isn't wrapped in a container with an explicit alignment override will render centered regardless of intended layout.
