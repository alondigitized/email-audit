# Need a new look? Just add stripes!
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-07**
## Executive summary

- Fifteen. That's where the C&B family count sits after this one lands, and I'm putting that number on the table before I say a single word about stripes. Two Crate & Kids emails in one calendar day — the space-themed bedroom this morning that I gave a seven because at least it had creative ambition, and now this in the afternoon. The stripes angle itself isn't the problem. Stripes in early June is genuinely seasonal, and if I'm honest, I'd look at striped textiles for the second home's master. But I don't have kids. Nothing in my purchase history or browse behavior suggests I do. And this email is wall-to-wall kids' bedrooms.
- Every product block is a children's room. The hero is a kids' bedroom. The shelving units are kids' storage. There is not a single image in this scroll that maps to my actual situation — renovating a primary and a second home simultaneously, buying adult furniture, pricing out dining chairs, looking at sofas. Even when the creative is clean and the layout works, a complete demographic miss kills the email before the first CTA.
- I'd pull the promo code from the footer in case it's stackable on a future adult order, mark it read, and move on. No click. No forward. Pottery Barn ran a warehouse sale this week that actually reached me — this one didn't come close to that bar.

## What's working

- **Seasonal hook is real:** "Just add stripes" in June is light and timely. The creative throughline holds across all the product blocks — every image reinforces the theme, which is more than Wayfair manages when it fires five unrelated categories in one send.
- **Clean visual hierarchy:** The hero image leads, the CTAs follow. No broken images, no overlapping text. The layout is uncluttered and tracks well on mobile.
- **Multiple CTAs without chaos:** Each product section has a clear shop button. CB2's "NOW & FUTURE CLASSICS" email buried its category links under editorial copy — this is considerably more direct about where the click goes.
- **Brand consistency end to end:** Typography, palette, and photography style are all on-brand Crate & Kids. Nothing breaks the visual trust.

## What's weak

- **Complete demographic miss:** Every image is a kids' bedroom. I don't have children. If C&B's CRM shows zero kids purchases and no kids-category browse, this should not have reached me.
- **Second Crate & Kids send today:** The morning space bedroom email already spent my kids-sub-brand patience for the day. There is no scenario where I needed a second Crate & Kids email before 6pm.
- **Cadence has become a drone:** Fifteen sends from the C&B family in roughly two days. Even well-executed emails get punished by this volume. The earlier "beautifully organized home" send — which was actually relevant to my renovation situation — deserved more breathing room than this cadence allows.
- **No adult textile crossover:** Not a single striped throw, adult duvet, or living room textile made the cut. Article broadens its audience within a single send without diluting the hero concept; a single adult bedding block here would have at least kept me scrolling.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Segment non-parents off this send.** Zero kids purchases plus no kids-category browse behavior in a subscriber's account means they shouldn't receive Crate & Kids sends at all. This is an audience fix, not a creative fix — it's also the single highest-leverage change.
- 2. **Add one adult textile module.** A striped throw for a living room, a striped accent rug, adult duvet — anything. Pottery Barn routinely blends adult and kids products under a single seasonal theme so the subject line works for a wider segment. One swap here could rescue this for the non-parent half of the list.
- 3. **One Crate & Kids per day, hard cap.** The morning send was the better email. This one should have been tomorrow's, or not at all. Cannibalizing your own goodwill is the fastest way to train subscribers to skip the preview entirely.
- 4. **Add urgency.** "Just add stripes" is charming but cost-free. "Through Sunday" or "while summer stock lasts" gives a reason to act now versus filing it for a hypothetical someday.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Stripe it up — fresh textiles for every room`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Stripes for the bedroom (kids', yours, both)`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `New striped bedding, throws, and storage for kids' rooms and beyond. Free shipping over $75.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Summer stripes just landed — kids' rooms, adult bedding, and more now in stock.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**[HIGH] Conflicting image `max-width` media queries**
Two rules overlap and produce unintended behavior on desktop (≥768 px):

```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img { max-width: 640px } }
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img { max-width: 600px } }
```

On screens ≥768 px both fire; cascade order gives the 600 px rule the win, capping images 40 px narrower than the 640 px container. On 640–767 px screens only the correct 640 px cap applies. The 600 px figure looks like a stale value from a 600-wide template version never cleaned up.

**[MEDIUM] `maximum-scale=1` in viewport meta**

```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```

Prevents pinch-zoom on mobile. Fails WCAG 2.1 SC 1.4.4 (Resize Text). Several major ESPs flag this in accessibility scoring.

**[MEDIUM] `-webkit-text-size-adjust: none` on wildcard selector**

```css
* { -webkit-text-size-adjust: none; -ms-text-size-adjust: none; }
```

`none` disables automatic text scaling entirely in Safari/WebKit. The accepted safe value is `100%`. The correct rule appears on `body` earlier in the stylesheet; this wildcard override is redundant and harmful.

**[LOW] Duplicate `format-detection` meta tags**
The combined meta appears first, then individual entries repeat two of the same directives:

```html
<meta name="format-detection" content="telephone=no,address=no,email=no,date=no,url=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="date=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="address=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="telephone=no">
```

Lines 2–4 are redundant. Not functionally broken but adds unnecessary parse overhead and signals template hygiene debt.

**[LOW] `.showmobile` hardcoded to `width:414px`**

```css
.showmobile { width: 414px !important; }
```

iPhone-specific pixel value; does not adapt to 375 px (SE), 390 px (iPhone 14 base), or Android widths. Should be `100%` within a fluid container.
