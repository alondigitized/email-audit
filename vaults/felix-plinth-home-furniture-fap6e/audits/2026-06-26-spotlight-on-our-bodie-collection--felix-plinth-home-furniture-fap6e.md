# ✨ Spotlight on: our Bodie collection
**Score:** 5/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-26**
## Executive summary

- Twenty-three. Give or take — that's where I'm at with the Crate & Barrel family inbox this month, and this one drops on the same day they sent me the Crate & Kids "Growth spurt" sale email *twice* and the wine glasses send. So walking into "✨ Spotlight on: our Bodie collection" I'm already running low on patience. And when I open it, the vibe confirms my suspicion: this is a catalog page dressed as an email. Beautiful bunk beds, four finish swatches, warm neutrals, tasteful lifestyle photography — I get it. But there's no offer, no urgency, no hook beyond "look at this furniture."
- The Bodie collection itself is handsome — that organic, Scandinavian-leaning wood-and-oatmeal palette reads well to me. I've got two homes I'm actively furnishing right now, and I do use Crate & Kids for guest rooms. But I am not in the market for a kids bunk bed this week, and nothing in this email knows that or cares. It treats me like a cold stranger walking past a showroom window. I've been "subscribed" to this brand for years and this email has no idea.
- Mark as read. I'd pull the Bodie URL and add it to my "maybe someday" Pinterest folder, but I'm not clicking anything today. Would not forward — this is a browse-it-alone email, not a share-with-partner email.

## What's working

- **The hero photography is genuinely good.** The bunk bed vignette is styled in that warm-neutral palette I keep gravitating toward — cream, taupe, natural oak. It doesn't feel cheap or dated.
- **Four finish swatches visible early.** I actually want to know if a piece comes in walnut vs. natural — showing me the options near the top of the email means I'm not hunting for it. Pottery Barn usually buries this.
- **Clean layout, no render bugs.** Single-column, readable text size, nothing broken. After some C&B emails this month that felt hurried, this one is at least disciplined.
- **Brand voice is consistent and quiet.** No ALL CAPS, no exclamation stacking — it's calm, which matches how I actually shop furniture.

## What's weak

- **No offer whatsoever.** We are in 4th of July sale week. Article dropped their sale email the same day. Pottery Barn ran "Extra 20% off select styles" and "4th of July Sale STARTS NOW" back-to-back. Crate & Kids sent me a collection spotlight. That is a scheduling misfire.
- **"Spotlight on: our Bodie collection" is not a reason to open.** "Bodie" is a brand-internal name I've never heard — it carries zero pull. Wayfair's "Open Box steals" made me open it. This subject line made me hesitate.
- **Nothing speaks to me as a buyer, just as a viewer.** I'm renovating a second home. I have specific rooms to fill. This email makes no effort to connect the collection to a use case — guest room, kid's room, nursery — it's just "here's a thing."
- **The cadence makes it worse.** This is the 23rd email from the C&B family this month. Even if this email were flawless, the frequency is eroding my willingness to engage with any of them.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Tie the collection to a use case, not just a name.** "The bunk bed your second home actually needs" or "Furnish the whole room for under $X" — give me a reason to care about Bodie specifically. CB2 does this better when they run "NOW & FUTURE CLASSICS ON SALE" — at least the hook is a value proposition.
- 2. **Add a sale hook or ship it next week.** A collection editorial with zero offer during the most promotional week of June is a wasted slot. Even "free shipping on the Bodie collection this week" would double my click probability.
- 3. **Show one specific hero product with a price.** Not a gallery — a single featured piece with the price, one room, one story. Wayfair's "Open Box steals" format works precisely because it's concrete.
- 4. **Address me like a returning customer.** "Your rooms, your rules" or even just "Back for more?" — anything that signals I'm known, not randomed into a list.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Confirmed issues:**

- **Conflicting desktop `img` max-width rules.** Two separate media queries both target desktop-width images with different constraints:
  ```css
  @media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img { max-width: 600px } }
  @media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img { max-width: 640px } }
  ```
  The 640px rule appears *after* the 768px rule in source order, so on viewports ≥ 768px the 640px cap wins (later rule wins in CSS cascade). Likely unintentional — images may render 40px wider than designed on wide desktop clients.

- **Dark mode explicitly suppressed.** `<meta name="color-scheme" content="light">` and `<meta name="supported-color-schemes" content="light">` force light rendering. In Gmail/Apple Mail dark mode this can produce hard-to-read white-on-white or inverted-background artifacts if no dark mode fallback styles are declared.

- **`maximum-scale=1` on viewport meta** restricts pinch-to-zoom. Accessibility best practice (WCAG 1.4.4) advises against preventing user scaling on mobile.

- **Hardcoded pixel widths inside mobile media query.** `.mobile { width:460px !important }` and `.showmobile { width:414px !important }` use fixed px values inside a `max-width: 460px` breakpoint. On devices narrower than 414px (e.g., iPhone SE at 375px) these force horizontal overflow.
