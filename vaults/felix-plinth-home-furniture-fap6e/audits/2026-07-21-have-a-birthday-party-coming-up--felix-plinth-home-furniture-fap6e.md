# Have a birthday party coming up?
**Score:** 4/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-21**
## Executive summary

- Twenty-six. That's where the Crate & Barrel family lands in my July inbox after this one, and I'm not going to bury that number — it's the only lens that matters right now. Four emails from this exact family yesterday alone, and now Crate & Kids opens my morning asking if I have a birthday party coming up. I don't. I don't have kids. I'm knee-deep in sourcing dining chairs for two houses simultaneously, and this sub-brand just asked me a question I cannot answer yes to.
- The email itself renders cleanly — three-column product grid, tight photography, consistent brand voice. If you are a parent, this probably lands fine. But I'm not, and the targeting here is pure spray-and-pray off the parent company's list. There's no offer, no urgency, no hook beyond the question in the subject line. It's basically a gift guide in a trench coat pretending to be a personalized send.
- Mark as read. Wouldn't click. Wouldn't forward. The only thing I'd forward is the entire C&B suppression form to their email team.

## What's working

- **Clean layout, no render bugs.** Three-column product grid loads correctly, photography is consistent, nothing is broken or overlapping. That's the floor, but it clears it.
- **Recognizable sender.** Even as a non-parent, I know Crate & Kids is the C&B children's sub-brand. No identity confusion, which is more than some off-brand senders can say.
- **Subject line length is efficient.** Thirty-three characters — reads fine in a mobile inbox snippet without truncation. The question format at least generates a beat of "wait, do I?" before the brain says no.
- **Photography is on-brand and warm.** The product styling matches what I'd expect from C&B's kids line — not cheap, not garish. If I had a kid's birthday on my calendar, I'd trust the aesthetic.

## What's weak

- **Completely wrong segment.** I'm a home renovator, not a parent. I'm on C&B's main list buying dining chairs and bar stools. Crate & Kids should be firing at confirmed parents, not anyone who once bought a throw pillow. Article's July 17 send — which I scored an 8 — came after I was browsing dining tables. *That's* targeting.
- **No offer anywhere in the visible render.** No percent off, no free shipping threshold, no member pricing. Pottery Barn's "New arrivals — classic, comfortable, collected" send that I scored a 9 at least had an implicit new-drop urgency. This is just a product parade.
- **Fifth C&B family email today.** The cadence is genuinely damaging trust. Even if the content were relevant, I'd be fatigued. The kids' room lighting guide already hit me this morning. Twice in a day from the same brand family on different sub-brands feels like a routing failure, not a strategy.
- **No CTA specificity.** Whatever the buttons say — I'd bet "Shop Now" — they lead to a browse page, not a curated birthday guide with filtering. That's a missed conversion moment even for their actual audience.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Suppress non-parents entirely.** The biggest ROI change isn't copy or design — it's list hygiene. If C&B has no purchase signal for children's products from a subscriber, Crate & Kids shouldn't be mailing them. Full stop.
- 2. **Add a concrete offer.** "Free gift wrap through Sunday" or "Spend $75, get a $15 gift card" would turn a browse prompt into a transaction prompt. Even Wayfair — who I dinged for recycling the same free-shipping subject line twice in two days — at least has a visible offer anchor.
- 3. **Tighten the hero to one gift solution, not a grid.** Lead with one curated gift set at a specific price point — something like "The $60 birthday kit" — before revealing the broader assortment. The grid says "we have things," the hero should say "here's the answer to your problem."
- 4. **Frequency cap the C&B family.** One email per day across all sub-brands. Not four. Not five. One.

- **Subject Alt B:** `The birthday gift that lands every time`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Curated picks under $75 — with free gift wrap this weekend only.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Skip the toy aisle. These ship fast and look good.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Brand name mismatch in `<title>`**
```html
<title>Crate & Barrel</title>
```
Sender is "Crate & Kids"; the `<title>` resolves to the parent brand. Gmail's tab label and some screen readers expose this value. Should read `Crate & Kids`.

**Conflicting desktop `img` max-width rules — last rule wins incorrectly**
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img {max-width: 600px} }
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img {max-width: 640px} }
```
Both queries match at ≥768px; the 640px block appears later in the stylesheet, so `max-width: 640px` overrides the 600px cap on all desktop viewports. The 600px rule is effectively dead.

**`maximum-scale=1` blocks user zoom (accessibility)**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text, AA) requires text to be resizable up to 200% without assistive technology. `maximum-scale=1` prevents pinch-zoom on iOS, which is a failure for users with low vision.

**Global `text-size-adjust: none` applied via wildcard selector**
```css
* { -webkit-text-size-adjust: none; -ms-text-size-adjust: none; ... }
```
Applying this to all elements rather than just `body` can suppress browser-side font scaling for users who have increased their default font size — compounding the zoom issue above.

**Duplicate `format-detection` meta tags**
Three auto-detection directives appear twice: once in the combined meta and once as individual entries:
```html
<meta name="format-detection" content="telephone=no,address=no,email=no,date=no,url=no">
<!-- then separately: -->
<meta name="format-detection" content="date=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="address=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="telephone=no">
```
Redundant but harmless; minor HTML hygiene issue.

**Mobile breakpoint at 460px (below industry standard)**
The responsive trigger is `max-width: 460px`. Most email clients use 600px as the mobile threshold. Devices with viewports between 461–599px receive the desktop layout, which may be too wide for portrait phones.
