# Vintage reissues on sale. Only at CB2.
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-08**
## Executive summary

- Twenty-three. That's where we are with CB2 since mid-May, and I'm going to put that number in the room right at the top because it colors everything. But — and this is the most genuine "but" I've offered in three weeks — the angle here actually earns a pause. Naming the actual designers, putting Gianfranco Frattini and Paul McCobb front and center like they're the headline act and not just catalog filler, is the kind of editorial swing CB2 should be taking more often. The mid-century aesthetic, the warm amber sofa as the centerpiece image, the structured layout with designer callouts stacked like a poster — this is the CB2 I subscribed to, not the "MIDSEASON SALE" blast I've already processed multiple times this week.
- That said, the execution doesn't fully close what the subject line opens. "Vintage reissues on sale. Only at CB2." is a clean, confident subject — probably the best they've written in this entire 23-email run. But inside the email, the designer provenance gets compressed into names and thumbnail images, and I'm left wanting dimension. What makes a Frattini piece a Frattini piece? Why should I care about McCobb? The names are dropped, not explained. For a subscriber who knows their stuff, that's a tease. For someone who doesn't, it's noise.
- I'd save this one to my inbox folder — the Pinterest-adjacent one where I stash things I want to reference when I'm deep in sourcing for the second home. But I wouldn't click through today. I'd use it as a lead to go hunt directly. That's a partial win for CB2 and a missed conversion.

## What's working

- **"Design Legends Sale" framing is genuinely differentiated** — calling out real designers by name is a level above "shop the sale," and for someone renovating two homes who actually knows these names, it stopped my scroll.
- **The warm amber sofa hero image is doing real work** — the color palette is right, the product is aspirational without being sterile, and it reads as a considered edit rather than a clearance dump.
- **"Only at CB2" in the subject is a real claim, not filler** — reissued licensed designs are a legitimate CB2 differentiator vs. Pottery Barn's Warehouse Sale or Wayfair's volume play. That specificity reads as confident rather than defensive.
- **Three-designer layout stacked vertically reads like editorial** — Frattini, McCobb, and Eitvgaard each get their own moment, which gives the email structure and pacing instead of a grid shotgun.
- **Clean render, no obvious layout breaks** — after some of the Crate & Kids chaos earlier this week, a clean mobile render is worth noting.

## What's weak

- **The cadence deficit is real and cumulative** — this is email twenty-three. Even a genuinely good email gets a ceiling imposed on it by the volume that preceded it. I'm fatigued before I open.
- **Designer provenance is name-dropped, not earned** — three names, three small images, no context. Who's buying a $1,000 sofa because they see "Gianfranco Frattini" and nothing else? Tell me one sentence about why these pieces matter.
- **The price point ($1,000 visible in the layout) lacks anchoring** — is that the sale price? From what? Without the before/after, I don't know if this is a real deal or just the normal price with a "sale" label slapped on. Pottery Barn's warehouse email at least showed the from/to clearly.
- **No urgency signal anywhere in the layout** — the midseason sale window has to be finite, but nothing in the email's visible hierarchy tells me when this expires.
- **CTAs are small and don't distinguish between products** — after the designer callouts, what am I clicking? Shop all Frattini? Just "view sale"? The button hierarchy gets lost in the lower half of the scroll.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Add one sentence of provenance per designer** — "McCobb's 1950s Planner Group was the first flat-pack furniture sold at department stores." That's all it takes. Turns a name-drop into a reason to believe, and differentiates this from the Article outdoor email (also strong editorial) which at least explained *why* the pieces were selected.
- 2. **Show the from/to price** — "Was $1,400, now $1,000 through June 15" anchors the deal and creates urgency simultaneously. Pottery Barn does this better on every warehouse send.
- 3. **Add a deadline to the visible hierarchy** — "Sale ends June 15" in the sub-hero doesn't need to be screaming; it just needs to exist. Right now the urgency lives nowhere.
- 4. **Consolidate to one primary CTA per designer callout** — "Shop Frattini" / "Shop McCobb" / "Shop Eitvgaard." Specific CTAs outperform generic "view all sale" every time.
- 5. **Dial back overall send frequency** — not actionable for this specific email, but at 23 sends the marginal value of each email is declining. Article sent me three emails in June and I've saved two of them.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Confirmed issues:**

- **`maximum-scale=1` in viewport meta** blocks pinch-to-zoom on mobile, which is an accessibility violation (WCAG 1.4.4 — Resize Text).
  ```html
  <meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
  ```

- **`-webkit-text-size-adjust: none` on `*` selector** — applied globally, not just to body. This suppresses OS-level text scaling across all elements, compounding the zoom-lock above.

- **Conflicting `img` max-width media queries** — two separate `@media` blocks both target desktop and set conflicting `max-width` values. The 690px rule will override the 768px rule due to cascade order, likely unintentionally:
  ```css
  @media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img { max-width: 100% !important } }
  @media only screen and (min-width: 690px) { img { max-width: 690px } }  /* wins */
  ```

- **Mobile breakpoint is 418px** — `.mobile` class targets `max-width: 418px`, which misses devices 419–479px wide (e.g., Android mid-range). Standard email practice is 480px or 600px.

- **Alt text on images** — cannot verify from truncated source. Flag for manual check.
