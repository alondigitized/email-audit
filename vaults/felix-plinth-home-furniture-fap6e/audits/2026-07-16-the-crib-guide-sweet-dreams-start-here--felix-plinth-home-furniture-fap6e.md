# The Crib Guide: Sweet dreams start here
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-16**
## Executive summary

- Five. That's the C&B family email count for July 16 by the time this one dropped. I've already been through two fairy-tale Crate & Kids lookbooks this morning and a pair of kitchen-sale emails — the Breville/Le Creuset send actually earned my attention because I'm outfitting two kitchens right now — and now Crate & Kids comes back with a crib guide. I am 36. I do not have children. I am not expecting a child. I am renovating two adult homes simultaneously and my inbox is a war zone. This email walked into the wrong fight.
- To be fair to the production team: the guide format is genuinely smart if you're in the market. Comparing crib options side by side with consistent photography is exactly how a first-time parent shops — I've watched friends do it. The warm wood tones look cohesive, there's what appears to be a 0% financing call-out mid-scroll that would actually matter to a new parent, and nothing visually breaks. The brand knows how to make a category editorial. The problem is they sent it to me.
- Mark as read, no click, no forward. The only person I'd send this to is a friend who's actually expecting, and that's manual effort I'm not expending on a promotional email.

## What's working

- **Comparative guide format** — showing multiple crib options with consistent photography is the right UX for this purchase. High-consideration item, parents want to compare. Executed correctly.
- **Warm neutral photography** — the nursery imagery is cohesive and premium. Consistent with the C&B aesthetic I recognize and have purchased from.
- **Financing detail surfaced mid-body** — 0% something is a real hook for a $400–$900 nursery purchase. New parents care about this. Smart inclusion even if placement is too deep.
- **No render bugs** — five C&B family emails in and this one still holds together visually. Low bar but worth crediting.
- **Sub-brand clarity** — "Crate & Kids" in the sender name tells me exactly what I'm walking into before I open. No bait-and-switch on the brand identity.

## What's weak

- **Complete category mismatch** — cribs are not sofas. They're not lamps. They're not kitchen gear. There's no version of this email that converts me, and any purchase-history or browse-history data C&B holds on me should have flagged this before send.
- **Sixth C&B family email today** — the cadence is genuinely broken. Pottery Barn Design Services earlier this week felt like a considered send. This feels like the brand is just draining whatever budget they have left before end of month.
- **No crossover module** — even one "while you're here, adult furniture on sale" block at the bottom would give me a reason to engage. CB2 at least tries to surface something adjacent to where I've shopped. This is pure single-category editorial with zero escape hatch.
- **Zero personalization signal** — treated like a cold prospect. Pottery Barn's design services email earlier felt like they knew I'd been browsing. This feels like a broadcast.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Suppress based on browsing/purchase history** — if someone has never clicked a nursery product, they should not receive a crib guide. This is a segmentation failure, not a creative failure. Fix the list before rewriting the email.
- 2. **Add an adult furniture exit module** — one row at the bottom, "Also new in living and dining," would rescue the send for non-nursery subscribers. Article does this well — they don't abandon you just because the editorial lead isn't your category.
- 3. **Move the financing offer to the hero** — 0% APR (or whatever it is) belongs above the first scroll break, not buried. That's the hook that converts anxious new parents.
- 4. **Cap C&B family sends to three per day per subscriber** — four fairy-tale/sofa/kitchen emails before this one hit means each subsequent send cannibalizes the previous. The Breville email from this morning deserved a longer lifespan without this one stepping on it.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**`img` max-width conflict (dead CSS rule):**
The stylesheet contains two competing desktop media queries in this order:
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img { max-width: 600px } }
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img { max-width: 640px } }
```
At any viewport ≥ 768px both queries match; the `640px` rule appears *later* in the cascade and wins, making the `768px` rule permanently unreachable. The 600px cap the developer intended for desktop is never applied — images render at 640px on all desktop clients. This is benign in most cases but indicates stale/conflicting CSS.

**Pinch-zoom disabled:**
`<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1">` — `maximum-scale=1` blocks user pinch-zoom, a WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text) accessibility violation.

**Dark mode not supported:**
`<meta name="color-scheme" content="light">` / `supported-color-schemes: light` only. No `@media (prefers-color-scheme: dark)` block. In dark-mode email clients (Apple Mail, iOS Mail), white backgrounds will be forced-inverted by the client unless overridden, which can cause rendering issues for any white-background image assets with transparency.

**Duplicate meta tags:**
- `<meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=utf-8">` AND `<meta charset="utf-8">` — redundant; harmless but untidy.
- `format-detection` appears three times with different values (combined and split). Not a bug in practice, but indicates copy-paste template construction.
