# New, bestselling & it ships free…
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-15**
## Executive summary

- Fifty-eight. That's where the C&B family counter sits with this one — Crate & Kids lands on June 15, the morning after I got four emails from the parent ecosystem in a single afternoon. I'm giving it its own tally since it's a different sender address, but mentally it's one long conversation I didn't start and cannot end fast enough.
- Here's the core problem: Crate & Kids is a kids' furniture brand. I'm renovating two houses simultaneously and I am absolutely the kind of person who saves these emails to an inbox folder that functions as a second Pinterest board — but I have no evidence that anyone at C&B corporate has ever asked whether I have children. I'm getting beautifully curated sage-green nursery content because I once bought a throw pillow. That is the targeting hole this whole email falls into.
- The free-shipping line in the subject saved it from an immediate archive. That is the one signal I genuinely act on when buying furniture, because freight quotes on a dresser or bed frame can add $100–150 to any order. If I were furnishing a kids' room — and I might be — this email would be useful. But it's not working hard to *earn* that relevance. Mark as read, revisit if the kids' room project materializes.

## What's working

- **Free shipping is the right offer for this category.** Furniture shoppers think in total landed cost. Putting "ships free" in the subject line is the correct play and is the only reason I didn't file this immediately.
- **The visual aesthetic is coherent.** The sage greens and warm neutrals carry across sections without feeling like a mash-up of disconnected product shots — whoever curated this palette did their job.
- **Clean render, no obvious bugs.** Every product image appears to load; no lorem ipsum, no broken layout columns. After the CB2 Warehouse Sale email had a text overlap on mobile last week, clean is worth naming.
- **Known brand.** Even when the targeting is off, I'm not hitting unsubscribe because Crate & Kids has brand equity with me. It's still a C&B product.

## What's weak

- **I may not have kids.** More precisely: C&B has never confirmed I do. Sending a Crate & Kids email because I'm on the master C&B list is lazy segmentation. Pottery Barn Kids runs a separate sender stream with a separate opt-in. C&B should match that.
- **"New, bestselling & it ships free…" is three subject lines duct-taped together.** New arrivals and bestsellers are competing value props. Adding free shipping as a third makes none of them land. Pick one, own it.
- **No single product to anchor on.** The email is a grid of many things — bedding, furniture, room accessories. After Article's "Summer hosting made simple" with one hero product and one clean CTA, this scattershot approach feels like a catalog index, not an email.
- **Zero urgency.** "New" and "bestselling" are not deadlines. CB2's Warehouse Sale last week had 100+ items and an implied depleting-inventory signal. This has nothing. The ellipsis at the end of the subject is doing emotional labor it hasn't earned.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Earn the kids segment first.** Add a preference step or trigger Crate & Kids emails only after someone clicks into the kids' category. Blasting the full C&B list is the same blunt-force move that got Wayfair filed under "thirteenth last call since Memorial Day."
- 2. **Cut the subject to one job.** Free shipping is the strongest hook for furniture buyers in this category. Lead with that plus a specific product type and nothing else.
- 3. **Give me one hero product with a real detail.** Age range, dimensions, lead time, swatch options — anything that tells me this is worth a click rather than a scroll-and-close.
- 4. **Fix the family cadence.** Four C&B emails on June 14, one Crate & Kids on June 15. The family is burning through subscriber goodwill faster than it's building purchase intent.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Confirmed issues:**

**a. Conflicting `img` `max-width` media queries**
Two rules are both active on screens ≥ 768px but disagree:
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img { max-width: 600px } }
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img { max-width: 640px } }
```
The 768px rule is declared last, so on viewports ≥ 768px the cap regresses from 640px to 600px — images shrink as the viewport *widens*. Intended max should be 640px at all desktop widths; the 768px block should be removed or consolidated.

**b. `maximum-scale=1` in viewport meta**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Blocks pinch-to-zoom on iOS/Android. This is an accessibility failure (WCAG 1.4.4 — Resize Text). Remove `maximum-scale=1`.

**c. `*` selector with `-webkit-text-size-adjust:none`**
```css
*{-webkit-text-size-adjust:none; ...}
```
Applied globally via the wildcard selector. Combined with the zoom lock above, users with visual impairments cannot scale text. Should be scoped to `body` only with value `100%`, not `none`.

**d. Dark mode not declared**
```html
<meta name="color-scheme" content="light">
<meta name="supported-color-schemes" content="light">
```
Opts out of dark mode entirely. Apple Mail and Outlook will still attempt dark mode auto-inversion on unprotected elements. If dark mode is intentionally unsupported, background colors on all containers must be explicitly set to prevent inversion artifacts.

**e. Duplicate CSS class definitions**
`ReadMsgBody`, `ExternalClass`, and associated rules are defined in two separate `<style>` blocks. The second definitions override the first silently. No functional breakage observed from what's visible, but increases parse weight and creates maintenance risk.
