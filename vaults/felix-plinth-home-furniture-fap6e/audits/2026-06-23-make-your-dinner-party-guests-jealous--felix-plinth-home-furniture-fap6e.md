# Make your dinner party guests jealous
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-23**
## Executive summary

- Thirty-eight. That's my CB2 count since mid-May — and this one shows up the same day as the "Warehouse Sale is selling fast" send that was already sitting in my inbox this morning. Two CB2 emails on June 23, same pattern as June 20. I almost archived it on the sender name alone — "CB2 Free Design Services" reads like a sub-list I never intentionally signed up for, not a continuation of a brand I'm actively in a relationship with.
- But here's the thing: the actual offer is legitimately one of the more useful things CB2 could put in front of me right now. I'm trying to solve two dining rooms simultaneously — different vibes, different footprints, decisions piling up faster than I can process the catalog. A free design session where someone who knows the line tells me what goes where? That's not a gimmick. That's load-bearing for someone in my situation. The "3 Ways to Meet" breakdown — in-person, virtual, and whatever the third mode is — is the most actionable content in the email. Unfortunately it's below a full atmospheric scroll of a very moody, very styled tablescaped dinner party shot that leads with "make your guests jealous" instead of "we'll help you solve this."
- The dinner party angle is the wrong frame. I'm not trying to impress anyone — I'm trying to finish two rooms before fall. I'll probably come back to the BOOK NOW link this weekend, but I won't forward it. Pottery Barn just ran a "FREE design help for warm-weather hosting" send with cleaner hierarchy and a clearer path to actually booking, and that one felt less like a lifestyle fantasy and more like a tool I could use.

## What's working

- **The core offer is genuinely high-value.** Free design services mid-renovation isn't a promotional trick — it's actual help I'd use, and CB2 earns points for putting it front and center in the hero.
- **"3 Ways to Meet" gives real logistical information.** In-person vs. virtual optionality matters to me; I'm not always near a CB2 location, so seeing that breakdown earns credibility.
- **BOOK NOW is unambiguous.** No "Explore" or "Discover" vagueness — the CTA tells me exactly what clicking does.
- **CB2's editorial aesthetic is consistent.** The dark, moody table scene is on-brand, and I know what house I'm in.
- **Seasonal timing is right.** Late June, pre-fall hosting setup — not a stale promo.

## What's weak

- **This is the second CB2 email today.** After June 20's double-send and now a repeat on June 23, the pattern is becoming a reflex skip. The Warehouse Sale email and this one undercut each other — one should own a day.
- **"CB2 Free Design Services" as the sender name creates unnecessary distance.** It doesn't read like CB2 reaching out; it reads like a CRM sublist blast from a service I marginally signed up for.
- **The "make your guests jealous" framing doesn't match my actual use case.** I'm not shopping for a dinner party aesthetic moment — I'm solving two rooms simultaneously. The lifestyle angle misfires when the real pitch (free professional design help) is so much stronger.
- **The product grid at the bottom is too small to be actionable.** Article's "Bedroom storage essentials" send this week showed piece names and rough pricing at the thumbnail level — I could evaluate from the email. Here I'm squinting at dark ceramics with no data attached.
- **No acknowledgment that I'm a known customer.** Nothing in the email treats me like someone who's already bought from CB2, has a wishlist, or has interacted with design services before. It's stranger-addressed all the way down.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Lead with the renovation utility, not the social aspiration.** "Make your guests jealous" is a fine subject line for a general acquisition blast — but for a high-intent renovator, "Two rooms, one free designer" or "Free design session: your dining room in 45 minutes" lands harder. The offer is the hero; let it speak.
- 2. **Move "3 Ways to Meet" above the atmospheric table scroll.** The consultation format details are the most conversion-relevant content in the email. Putting them third — below an editorial mood shot and a "finer touches" product block — buries the actual sales mechanism.
- 3. **Fix the sender display name.** Send this from "CB2" or "CB2 Design Services" at minimum. The current "CB2 Free Design Services" reads like a cold marketing arm, not the brand I have a drawer full of receipts from.
- 4. **Don't double-send on the same calendar day.** The Warehouse Sale email owns June 23 and should have. This one deserved its own slot — Tuesday, maybe — where it's not competing with a sale email from the same brand.
- 5. **Show one solved dining room.** Give me a full-room vignette — table, chairs, rug, pendant — with a "this is what a session can produce" link. Give me the outcome, not just the process.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**3a. Double-slash paths in font URLs (3 occurrences)**
```
https://www.cb2.com//Styles/CB2/fonts/BrownLight/lineto-brown-light.woff2
https://www.cb2.com//Styles/CB2/fonts/BrownRegular/lineto-brown-regular.woff2
https://www.cb2.com//Styles/CB2/fonts/GTSectra/GT-Sectra-Display-Regular.woff2
```
Servers typically normalize `//` to `/`, but it is an unnecessary defect and will 404 on any server with strict path handling.

**3b. Cross-brand font dependency**
`HNW05` typeface is loaded from `https://www.crateandbarrel.com/webfonts/` (4 declarations). A CB2 email depending on crateandbarrel.com font assets creates a cross-origin hard dependency — if that domain's font CDN path changes, CB2 emails degrade silently to fallback fonts.

**3c. Conflicting `@font-face` declarations for "Lineto Brown Sub"**
Three separate `@font-face` blocks share the family name `Lineto Brown Sub`:
- Block 1: EOT + WOFF, no `font-weight` declared (implicitly `normal`)
- Block 2: WOFF2 only, `font-weight: 400`
- Block 3: WOFF2 only, `font-weight: 600`

Block 1 and Block 2 both resolve to weight 400 with different source sets. Browser behavior on the conflict is undefined; the EOT/WOFF sources in Block 1 may never be reached.

**3d. `maximum-scale=1` in viewport meta**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Prevents users from pinching to zoom. Violates WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text, Level AA).

**3e. Universal selector text-size override**
```css
*{-webkit-text-size-adjust:none; -ms-text-size-adjust:none; ...}
```
Applied via `*` selector — disables OS-level text scaling for users who rely on it. This compounds the `maximum-scale=1` issue above.

**3f. Non-standard mobile breakpoint**
```css
@media only screen and (max-width: 418px) { .mobile { width:414px !important; } }
```
414px targets iPhone 6/7/8 viewport exactly. Devices with widths between 418px and ~599px (many Android handsets, iPhone SE 3rd gen in some orientations) receive neither mobile nor desktop layout. Standard practice is `max-width: 600px`.
