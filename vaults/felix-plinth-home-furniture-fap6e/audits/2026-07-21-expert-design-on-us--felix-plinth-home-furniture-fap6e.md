# EXPERT DESIGN, ON US
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-21**
## Executive summary

- Thirty-one. That's the CB2 count for July and I want that number sitting on top of everything I say about this email, because it is the single biggest context filter I bring to it. When I saw "CB2 Free Design Services" as the sender name, my thumb was already halfway to the archive. Then I read "EXPERT DESIGN, ON US" and I actually paused — because this isn't the fifth sale email of the week, it's a service pitch, and it's aimed directly at a person who is simultaneously gutting two houses and running out of steam trying to make every finish decision alone.
- The hero does real work. Dark, warm, moody living room with a sofa I'd genuinely consider — and a "$5,000 REFRESH" hook big enough to stop the scroll. That number is doing heavy lifting. Free design consultation is table stakes at this price point, but putting a dollar figure on the value of what you're getting feels more honest than "complimentary styling session." The explainer section about what CB2 Design Services actually covers is structurally right — I want to know what I'm booking before I book it — but it's dense, small-type copy that I would not read in an email. That's a landing-page job.
- I would not click today. I already opened the "THINGS OF QUALITY" CB2 this morning and my CB2 willpower is tapped. But I'd drag this one into the folder I save for "things I'll circle back to when I'm ready to start the living room in the second house." That's not nothing — that's a save, which for email is a quiet win.

## What's working

- **The $5,000 hook lands.** Putting a concrete dollar value on the design service is genuinely persuasive — it converts "free consultation" from a throwaway line to something that feels worth scheduling.
- **Hero photography is on-brand and aspirational.** The dark-toned, high-contrast living room is exactly what I'd screenshot for reference — it's the right aesthetic signal for a CB2 subscriber who's trying to make two renovations look intentional, not ad-hoc.
- **The service explainer exists.** Most brands bury what a "design service" actually means. Having an "ABOUT CB2 DESIGN SERVICES" section with multiple columns of info is the right instinct — even if execution is too small to read comfortably.
- **The CTA hierarchy is clear.** There are two book-appointment buttons in a logical sequence — one after the hero, one after the explainer — which mirrors how I'd actually make a decision. Skim, consider, re-read, click.
- **Sender specialization signals this isn't a mass blast.** "CB2 Free Design Services" as the sender name — annoying as a subject-line spoiler — at least implies some segmentation effort, which Pottery Barn's generic sends don't bother with.

## What's weak

- **It's the 31st CB2 email in July.** I counted. The goodwill required to open email thirty-one from the same brand in three weeks is running extremely low, and this morning I already opened "THINGS OF QUALITY." Two CB2s in one day is two CB2s too many.
- **The sender name blows the reveal before you open.** "CB2 Free Design Services" as the display name tells me exactly what's inside — that's fine for a cold list, but for a subscriber like me who already knows CB2 offers design services, it removes any reason to open out of curiosity.
- **ALL CAPS subject, again.** CB2 has been doing this all month. I called it out on "SO MUCH NEW," on "$1K COULD BE YOURS TO WIN," on "THINGS OF QUALITY" this morning. At some point it stops reading as bold and starts reading as shouting.
- **The multi-column copy block is unreadable at email size.** I can see there's substance in the design services explainer — but the type is so small in the rendered layout that I'd have to pinch-zoom to read it. That content belongs on the landing page, not the email.
- **The room grid below the fold loses focus.** After the hero and the service explainer, there's a grid of styled room photos that feels like a CB2 catalog append rather than a designed flow. Article's recent dining room email kept a single throughline — every image served the one CTA. This one meanders.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Rein in the send cadence before anything else.** No single creative fix matters as long as CB2 is hitting me 10+ times a week. Suppress any subscriber who opened two CB2 emails in the last 72 hours. Give the list room to miss you.
- 2. **Lead with a specific outcome, not a service name.** "Let's figure out your living room" or "We'll spec the whole space — on us" beats the abstract "expert design." Tie it to renovation language — I'm not redecorating, I'm building a room from scratch.
- 3. **Drop the multi-column copy block from the email body.** Replace it with three crisp bullets — what you get, how long it takes, whether it's in-store or virtual — and push the full explainer to the landing page.
- 4. **Swap the generic room grid for one specific styled room linked to a consultation booking.** The grid dilutes attention. Article used a single dining room and a single CTA and it worked cleaner.
- 5. **Lowercase the subject line.** Every time CB2 goes lowercase or sentence-case it outperforms the shouted variants on feel. "Expert design, on us" reads warmer and less like a clearance rack.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Viewport zoom lock — WCAG 1.4.4 failure:**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
`maximum-scale=1` prevents user zoom on mobile. Fails WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text, Level AA). Remove `maximum-scale=1`.

**Conflicting `text-size-adjust` rules:**
```css
body { -webkit-text-size-adjust: 100%; -ms-text-size-adjust: 100%; }
* { -webkit-text-size-adjust: none; -ms-text-size-adjust: none; }
```
The `*` rule overrides `body` due to specificity. `none` suppresses OS-level text scaling entirely. Change the `*` rule to `100%` to match intent.

**Aggressive global `line-height`:**
```css
* { line-height: 100%; }
```
A 100% line-height on every element compresses descenders in many email clients (especially Outlook). This is a known rendering defect pattern.

**Mobile breakpoint at 418px:**
```css
@media only screen and (max-width: 418px) { .mobile { width: 414px !important; } }
```
Modern Android devices (e.g., Pixel 8: 412px logical width) and some iPhones (SE 3rd gen: 375px) are within range, but devices between 419–599px wide receive neither the mobile stack nor a true desktop layout. Standard breakpoint is 600px.

**Image alt text:** Cannot confirm — HTML truncated before `<img>` body tags.
