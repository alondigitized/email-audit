# GET IT ON SALE
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-13**
## Executive summary

- Twenty. That's the CB2 tally since July 1st, and I counted because at this point counting is the only ritual that makes opening another one feel like a choice. This one arrives with "GET IT ON SALE" — three words that communicate approximately nothing — and when I opened it I found exactly what I found two days ago on July 11th: "The Summer Edit, Up to 40% Off, Shop the Sale." Pixel-perfect repeat. Same percentage, same hero category, same blue-water poolside vibe. CB2 just photocopied their own email and resent it with a worse subject line.
- What saves this from a complete write-off is the section nobody will scroll to: "Win a $5,000 Refresh — Book a Free Design Appointment." I am renovating two homes simultaneously right now. A free design consultation from a brand whose aesthetic I actually respect has real utility for me. That's a legitimate click trigger buried in the middle of an email I almost didn't open. That's a problem of its own making.
- I'd click through to the "New & Best Selling" bed frame and the "More For You" product grid, close the tab when the generic sale landing page loads, and not forward this to anyone. Compared to Article's clean new-arrivals pivot four days after their 4th-of-July run, CB2 looks like they forgot they already sent this.

## What's working

- The mid-email room scene — warm wood, round mirror, layered textiles, green plant — is the kind of composed, livable styling that actually helps me visualize furniture in my spaces; more useful than a white-background product shot
- "Win a $5,000 Refresh / Book a Free Design Appointment" is the one genuinely differentiated hook in the email, and for someone actively furnishing two homes it's a real offer, not a marketing placeholder
- "New & Best Selling" section surfaces a specific natural linen/wood bed frame — right category, right aesthetic moment for me; this is a product I might actually research
- The "More For You" personalized grid at the bottom has chairs and lighting that map to where I actually am in my renovation checklist — it doesn't feel completely random
- Layout is clean, images render without gaps or breakage, and the hierarchy from hero → design consult → product moves logically top to bottom

## What's weak

- "GET IT ON SALE" is the laziest subject line CB2 has sent this month — Article ran "New arrivals just landed" last Tuesday and I opened that because it implied discovery; this implies I've never checked email before
- I already got this exact 40% off summer sale on July 11th — two days ago — same percentage, same hero; by the third repetition "up to 40% off" stops reading as a discount and starts reading as the regular price with extra steps
- "Save the Summer Pro" in the secondary section is tiny, unexplained, and confusing — I can't tell if "Pro" is a loyalty tier, a professional discount program, or just copywriting filler
- The hero's blue-water/poolside imagery doesn't connect to interior renovation at all; Pottery Barn's "New Arrivals in florals, plaids & stripes" send last week felt immediately spatial, this feels like CB2's Instagram grid got pasted into an email
- The Crate & Barrel and Crate & Kids cross-promo logos at the footer are clutter — I just endured a Crate & Kids convertible toddler furniture email and this reminds me of that waste of time

## Recommendations

- 1. **Lead with the design appointment in the subject.** The 40% off sale is not news. A free $5,000 room refresh consultation is. The email buries its most differentiated offer under a hero I've seen twice this week.
- 2. **Escalate or retire the offer.** Running 40% off on July 11 and again on July 13 without escalation destroys the credibility of the number. Call it "Final days" or add a specific deadline, or change the frame. Wayfair at least changes the product hook every send even when the underlying sale is the same.
- 3. **Name and price the hero product.** The linen bed frame in "New & Best Selling" is compelling — give it a name, a price, and a direct PDP link. That's what makes a "shop now" button worth pressing.
- 4. **Explain "Pro."** If CB2 has a professional or loyalty tier, this is a renovation-active customer who would use it. Earn it or cut the label.
- 5. **Subject Alt A:** `Free design consult + the summer sale is still on`
- **Subject Alt B:** `New & best selling: the pieces worth buying right now`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Book your free room refresh appointment — plus up to 40% off furniture and decor.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `The summer edit's standout pieces, now with a design consult option.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**`maximum-scale=1` blocks user zoom**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Prevents visually impaired users from zooming. WCAG 1.4.4 violation.

**Universal text-size-adjust suppression**
```css
* { -webkit-text-size-adjust: none; -ms-text-size-adjust: none; }
```
Applied to all elements, not just `body`. This suppresses OS-level accessibility font scaling globally.

**`line-height: 100%` on universal selector**
```css
* { line-height: 100%; }
```
A percentage line-height on `*` is computed relative to each element's own font size, which collapses line height unpredictably on nested elements. Should be a unitless value (e.g., `1`) or scoped to specific selectors.

**Malformed CSS comment embedding HTML comment syntax**
```css
/* <!-- MI styling starts--> */
```
HTML comment syntax inside a CSS block comment. Technically benign in most parsers but non-conformant and a sign of template injection issues.

**Duplicate `@font-face` declarations for the same family/weight**
Three separate `@font-face` blocks declare `font-family: "Lineto Brown Sub"` — two with `font-weight: 400` — which creates a resolution ambiguity. The first declaration (for `.eot`) lacks a `font-display` property entirely.

**Double-slash in asset URLs**
```css
url("https://www.cb2.com//Styles/CB2/fonts/BrownLight/lineto-brown-light.woff2")
```
Double slash after domain appears in 3+ font URL declarations. Most servers normalize this, but it's technically malformed and can fail on strict origin servers or CDN rules.

**`<title>` does not match subject line**
