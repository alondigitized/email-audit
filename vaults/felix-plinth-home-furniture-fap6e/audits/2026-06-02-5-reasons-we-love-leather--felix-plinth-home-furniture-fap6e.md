# 5 reasons we love leather →
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-02**
## Executive summary

- Thirty-six. That's the C&B family count in my inbox now, and I'll say this upfront: as someone who has been legitimately researching leather sofas for one of my two renovations, "5 reasons we love leather" is exactly the kind of editorial content I *should* want to receive. The hero image stops the scroll — a curved, warm-toned sectional that looks like it belongs in the room I'm trying to build. The brand is leaning into education over promotion, which I respect in principle.
- But here's my issue: I just got the "sofa raves" email (#32) four sends ago. That one was user-reviews-as-social-proof. This one is brand-voice-as-argument. They're both trying to sell me a leather sofa from two different angles in the same week — and neither one is giving me a price, a dimension, a delivery window, or a members offer. Two swings at the same target with no ask attached feels like a waste of the at-bat.
- Would I click? Probably one CTA out of curiosity. Would I forward it? No. Would I apply what I see here to a purchase decision? Maybe — but the Pottery Barn Design Services email I got recently (*"Get the look with FREE design services"*) actually moved me closer to pulling a trigger because it offered something actionable. This one just makes leather sound pretty.

## What's working

- **The hero image earns it.** A curved leather sectional in a warm, layered living room is the right visual for someone spec'ing a real sofa, not a showroom filler.
- **The listicle format is actually smart.** "5 reasons" creates a content contract — I know what I'm getting, I know it ends, I trust it won't waste my time. Better than a vague "Explore our leather collection" opener.
- **"The lasting beauty of leather" lands.** Durability and aging-well are genuinely relevant signals for a considered, long-term purchase. It's the right angle for a person buying for a house they'll own for 15 years.
- **Clean layout, no render breaks.** Every image loaded, the hierarchy reads top-to-bottom cleanly, the CTAs are visually distinct. No clutter. Given how broken some Wayfair sends have looked at this same cadence, clean counts for something.
- **Warm, brand-coherent palette.** The amber/cognac/cream tones are consistent all the way down — this doesn't feel like three teams patched it together.

## What's weak

- **No price, no offer, no urgency.** This is 100% editorial. I'm fine with that once in a while, but I've had four C&B sends this week already. One of them should have a number with a dollar sign on it.
- **The listicle reasons felt familiar.** "Leather ages beautifully," "it's durable," "it gets better over time" — I've heard this pitch in every leather sofa showroom since 2014. Nothing in this email told me something I didn't already know or believe.
- **No sizing or product-specific detail.** I've got a specific wall width I'm working with. A mention of modular configurations or dimensions — anything grounding the fantasy in logistics — would push me toward clicking.
- **Cadence drag is real.** This is #36 in my C&B inbox. Compare that to Article, which sent me a genuinely useful kitchen island editorial (#8 or so from them) and felt fresh. At 36, I'm starting to mentally route everything from this sender to "read later," which means never.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Add one concrete offer to at least one CTA.** "Shop leather — members save 15%" or even "Free swatch kit with your email" gives me a reason to click that isn't just curiosity. CB2 managed to attach actual markdowns to their editorial ("New markdowns are here") — this send doesn't bother.
- 2. **Swap one "reason" for a real customer story.** The sofa raves email (#32) was stronger because it used actual quotes. This one argues in brand voice. Pull one owner testimonial into the listicle and it earns the claims instead of just making them.
- 3. **Surface at least one product with dimensions/configurability.** "Available in 2- and 3-seat configurations, starting at X" gives renovation-mode shoppers something to mentally place in their room.
- 4. **Throttle the parent send cadence.** I know this is above the email marketer's pay grade, but at 36 emails in a few weeks, suppression rules need a look. Pottery Barn's design services send (#7) felt scarce and valuable; C&B #36 feels like noise.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Viewport blocks user zoom:**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
`maximum-scale=1` prevents pinch-zoom — a WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text) violation and an accessibility flag for visually impaired users.

**Conflicting image `max-width` media queries:**
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img { max-width: 600px } }
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img { max-width: 640px } }
```
The 640px rule fires first in cascade order; the 768px rule then overrides it for wider viewports, producing a narrower max-width (600px) on larger screens than on 640–767px screens. Likely unintentional — images will appear constrained on desktop.

**`-webkit-text-size-adjust: none`** appears in the client CSS block (`html { -webkit-text-size-adjust:none; }`). Using `none` (rather than `100%`) hard-suppresses text scaling on iOS Safari, overriding user accessibility settings.

**Dark mode not supported:**
```html
<meta name="color-scheme" content="light">
<meta name="supported-color-schemes" content="light">
```
No `@media (prefers-color-scheme: dark)` rules present. On Apple Mail and iOS Mail (which account for ~50%+ of opens in typical retail lists), this forces light rendering with no dark-mode adaptation — white backgrounds may invert unintentionally in forced-dark clients that ignore `supported-color-schemes`.
