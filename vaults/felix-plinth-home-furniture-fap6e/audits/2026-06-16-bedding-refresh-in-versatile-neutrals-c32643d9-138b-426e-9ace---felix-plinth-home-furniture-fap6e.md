# Bedding refresh in versatile neutrals
**Score:** 9/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-16**
## Executive summary

- Twenty-one. That's where the Pottery Barn counter sits in my inbox and I refuse to stop leading with it because it is the only context that matters for any email from this brand right now. The June 14 "100+ new sale styles" send was #18. The June 15 outdoor refresh and the 70% off send were #19 and #20. Now we have "Bedding refresh in versatile neutrals" — and honestly? It's one of the better subjects this campaign has produced. Specific. Editorial. Not just "SALE SALE SALE." The cream-and-sand bedding imagery is genuinely beautiful and the neutral palette is dead-on for both houses I'm trying to pull together right now.
- But I'm numb. The Summer Sale banner at the top is the same banner I've seen on at least a dozen Pottery Barn emails since Tuesday. My eye skips it on autopilot. The product tiles — gingham shams, striped pillowcases, a tufted duvet — are appealing in isolation, but the "Recommended Just For You" block at the bottom is doing more interesting work than the hero. At this cadence, that personalization signal is the only thing that feels like Pottery Barn actually knows I exist.
- I'd mark this as read, not delete it. Might revisit it Saturday when I'm in a slower scroll mindset and actively comparing bedding options for the primary bedroom. I would not forward it — the deal isn't interesting enough to share and anyone I'd forward it to is probably already getting their own copy.

## What's working

- **Subject line is the strongest from this campaign so far** — "Bedding refresh in versatile neutrals" is specific, editorial, and under 40 characters. Pottery Barn finally wrote a subject that isn't just discount escalation.
- **Neutral palette is exactly right for where I am** — sand, cream, off-white across every product tile. This is the color direction I've been working toward in both properties. Zero friction, all signal.
- **"Recommended Just For You" section landed** — I can see a bedroom styled in white linen and what looks like living room/outdoor furniture below it. Actually makes me think the algorithm knows I've been browsing upholstered pieces.
- **No render bugs visible** — the images loaded cleanly, the layout is solid top to bottom, no overlapping text or broken grid anywhere I can see.
- **Summer Sale percentage visible in the hero** — the discount is in the banner, prominent, doesn't require hunting.

## What's weak

- **Cadence has completely eroded the open impulse** — by email #21 the Summer Sale banner is wallpaper. I register it as background noise before I even read the copy below it.
- **Gingham skews a little soft for a male-coded send** — the plaid pillow covers in the second product tile read more Pottery Barn Teen/feminine Pinterest board. Not a dealbreaker but it's not directing at me.
- **No specific deadline anywhere I can see** — "Summer Sale" without an end date feels infinite, which kills urgency. Article managed to make "summer hosting" feel seasonal and time-bound in its June 12 send without even a percentage off. PB can't land urgency at all with this much volume.
- **Multiple CTAs, no clear hierarchy** — I count at least four or five separate tap targets in the product grid, plus the "Recommended" section. Wayfair's June "8' X 10' OUTDOOR RUGS" email won because it funneled me to one specific thing. This scatters me.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Kill at least three emails per week from this cadence.** Nothing in the creative fixes what twenty-one emails in six days does to engagement. One well-curated weekly digest would outperform this full-court press on click rates.
- 2. **Put a hard deadline on the Summer Sale** — "Ends Sunday" or "Sale ends June 22" in the hero banner. Article and even CB2 manage time-bound language without being obnoxious about it. PB is leaving urgency money on the table on every send.
- 3. **Hero into one product, not a grid** — lead with the single best-selling neutral duvet, link directly to its PDP, then let the tiles below be supporting. CB2's "The rug that ties it all together" — which I got from a separate CB2 design services sender last week — did exactly this and I clicked because I knew exactly what I was getting.
- 4. **Lean into the "Recommended Just For You" placement** — move it higher, maybe second block after hero. That's the only part of the email that signals Pottery Barn knows I'm a real customer.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**[CRITICAL] PHP template bleed into `<style>` block.** Raw PHP `header()` calls appear verbatim inside a `<style>` tag in the rendered HTML:
```css
header('Content-Type: image/jpeg');
header("Cache-Control: no-store, no-cache, must-revalidate, max-age=0");
header("Cache-Control: post-check=0, pre-check=0", false);
header("Pragma: no-cache");
```
These are server-side directives that were never executed — the template engine emitted them as literal text. Email clients that don't ignore unknown CSS at-rules may render this as visible text or log parse errors. Root cause is a PHP include that was pasted into a static template without stripping the response headers.

**[WARN] Cross-brand HTTP asset.** A West Elm spacer GIF is embedded in a Pottery Barn email over HTTP:
```
http://edm.westelm.com/we/evergreen/spacer_0.gif
```
Clients enforcing HTTPS-only image loading will block this. Wrong brand origin is also a technical hygiene issue.

**[WARN]** Two additional footer assets served over HTTP:

http://edm.potterybarn.com/pb/2022/0311_Key_CC_Email_BrandBanner/slices_02.jpg
