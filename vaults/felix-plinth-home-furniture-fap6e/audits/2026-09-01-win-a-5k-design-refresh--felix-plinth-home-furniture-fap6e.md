# Win a $5k design refresh
**Score:** 5/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-09-01**
## Executive summary

- Okay, eighteenth CB2 email this month — I checked. This one's actually not another discount blast, it's a design-services pitch: "WIN A $5,000 REFRESH," book a free appointment with a designer between August 13 and September 13. That's a genuinely different angle than the last four sends, and as someone juggling two renovations at once, a free designer consult with mood boards and fabric swatches is exactly the kind of thing I'd bookmark — if I trusted I wasn't about to get a sales call disguised as a "consultation."
- The sweepstakes framing undercuts it though. "Win a $5,000 refresh" reads like a long-shot giveaway, and I've got zero information on odds, how winners are picked, or whether "free design appointment" locks me into anything. Below the fold it just becomes the Labor Day sale again — 50% off, same banner I've now seen from CB2 four times this week — so the "design services" premise gets abandoned halfway through the email anyway.
- I wouldn't click the sweepstakes CTA — too vague, too low-odds for my attention span on a commute scroll. I might screenshot the room categories (Living Room, Dining Room, Bedroom, Rugs) into my inbox folder since that's genuinely useful for cross-referencing against what I'm doing at the second house, but I'm not forwarding this to anyone. It's a decent change of pace after weeks of pure discount spam, but it doesn't close the deal.

## What's working

- Leading with design services instead of another "% off" banner is a legit break from CB2's own pattern — Article's Labor Day email at least had a number up top, but this one tries something CB2 hasn't done recently.
- The category grid (Living Room, Lighting, Dining Room, Decor & Mirrors, Bedroom, Rugs) is genuinely useful for someone renovating two houses — I can mentally sort which rooms still need work.
- "mood boards, color palettes, fabric swatches, product selections" is specific language that speaks to someone actually planning a room, not just browsing.
- The rewards card note near the bottom (earn 10% back in Reward Dollars) is relevant since I run everything through my highest-rewards card anyway.

## What's weak

- The $5,000 "win" framing has no rules, odds, or fine print visible — feels like bait to get me to book an appointment, not a real prize mechanic I can evaluate.
- It pivots hard into the same Labor Day 50%-off banner I've already seen from CB2 this week — so the "design services" hook doesn't even get its own email, it's sharing space with the discount treadmill.
- Product grid at the bottom ("More For You") is generic — a platform bed, a bookshelf, a dining chair — nothing tied to the design-appointment CTA above it, so the email doesn't feel personalized to me at all.
- No pricing or process detail on what the design service actually costs after the "free" consult — as a Millennial who's been burned by "free consultation" sales funnels before, I want to know upfront if there's a catch.

## Recommendations

- 1. Cut the sweepstakes framing entirely or add real terms — "$5,000 refresh" with zero rules reads as bait; either state the odds/process or drop it and just sell the free design service on its own merits.
- 2. Don't cram the Labor Day sale into the same email — it dilutes the design-services pitch and makes the whole thing feel like two unrelated emails stapled together.
- 3. Personalize the "More For You" grid to match my browsing/purchase history instead of generic bestsellers — Wayfair at least ties its subject line to what's actually in the grid; this doesn't.
- 4. Add a line clarifying the design appointment is genuinely free with no purchase obligation — kill the skepticism before it starts.

- **Subject Alt B:** `Book your CB2 designer before Sept 13`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Mood boards, swatches, and a real person to plan your room with you.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `No cost to book. Appointments fill fast through Labor Day.`

## 2. Link & Tracking Issues

Modern mail clients (Gmail, Outlook, Apple Mail) commonly block or strip mixed-content `http://` image requests, meaning these tracking calls likely fail silently in a large share of opens.
