# Your chance to win $4,199 in back-to-school prizes!
**Score:** 4/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-18**
## Executive summary

- Fifteen. That's where I am with the Crate & Barrel family after this one lands in my inbox, and I'll be honest — I almost didn't even register which sub-brand sent it until I looked twice. Crate & Kids. Right. The kids arm. The one that's sent me three consecutive emails in the last day and a half about celebrity baby must-haves and transitional nursery furniture. I don't have kids. I've never had kids. I am renovating two adult homes simultaneously and somehow I'm on a cadence that would make sense if I'd just announced a baby shower registry.
- The giveaway hook — $4,199 in prizes — is legitimately concrete, and I'll give them that. The dollar amount is specific enough that I notice it. But the prize pool is all kids and back-to-school merchandise, and the co-sponsors visible in the email are clearly oriented the same direction. Nothing in this prize pool is useful to me. The 30% off section buried below the fold is actually the only thing that could have reeled me in, but by then I've already mentally filed this under "delete."
- I'm not clicking, I'm not forwarding. I might regret the 30% off on Crate & Barrel proper if something overlaps with what I'm actually sourcing — but Crate & Kids isn't where I shop, and this email knows that even less than the three that came before it.

## What's working

- The $4,199 prize figure is genuinely specific and gets the eye — it's not "win big" vagueness, and the number registers fast on mobile.
- The hero image of the backpack/school gear is clean and seasonally coherent — they're not pretending it's January.
- The 30% off section lower in the email has a real offer attached to it, which is more than the last Crate & Kids send (the transitional furniture one) managed to show me.
- Co-branding the giveaway with multiple partner logos adds some legitimacy — it's not a black-box sweepstakes, you can see who's behind it.
- Render is clean from what I can see — no broken images, no overlapping text, layout holds together.

## What's weak

- I don't have kids. Crate & Kids knows nothing about me except that I'm in the Crate & Barrel email list. This is a targeting miss, and it's the third or fourth in a row from this sub-brand alone.
- The email is doing three jobs at once — giveaway, new fall arrivals, 30% off — and none of them get enough room to breathe. Article ran a single-focus home tour last week that landed better precisely because it committed to one thing.
- The cadence is now broken. This is the fifteenth C&B family email in roughly 36 hours. Even if this were the world's most relevant send, I'm fatigued.
- Pottery Barn just ran a 70% off promo with clean hierarchy and a real catalog moment. This feels scattered by comparison — three value propositions fighting each other in a single scroll.
- No personalization signal anywhere. Not my name, not a category cue, not even a "because you browsed X" anchor. Wayfair's wall art clearance email last week at least felt like it knew I was looking at wall art. This feels like I fell into a batch-and-blast.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Segment this off my list entirely** — or create a child-household flag and gate Crate & Kids sends behind it. I shouldn't be getting three kids sub-brand emails in one day if there's no signal I have kids.
- 2. **Pick one hero, not three.** The giveaway, the new fall arrivals, and the 30% off are fighting each other. If the giveaway is the send, the send is the giveaway. Move the 30% off to a follow-up or a dedicated module; lose the new arrivals teaser entirely.
- 3. **Add a deadline to the giveaway.** "$4,199 in prizes" is specific but "enter now" is not. "Ends July 25" would add a credible urgency layer without spam tactics.
- 4. **Use the co-brand sponsor logos harder.** If Crate & Barrel, CB2, and others are in the prize pool, lead with that for a subscriber who shops those brands. I might enter for adult-home merchandise from the family brands, not for the kids stuff.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Win $4,199 from Crate & Barrel, CB2, and more — enter by July 25`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Back-to-school giveaway: $4,199 in prizes from brands you know`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Enter once for a shot at furniture, décor, and essentials from 6+ brands.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Six brands, one sweepstakes. No purchase necessary — deadline July 25.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**`<title>` mismatch (confirmed)**
```html
<title>Crate & Barrel</title>
```
Sender identity is "Crate & Kids" but the document title reads "Crate & Barrel." Some email clients (e.g., Apple Mail tab previews) surface the `<title>` value.

**Stale development placeholder comment (confirmed)**
```html
<!--[IMPUT HERE CLIENT FONT IMPORT SCRIPT if needed]-->
```
This template directive — including the typo "IMPUT" — was not removed before send. Visible in raw source to anyone who inspects it.

**Conflicting min-width media query ordering (confirmed)**
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img {max-width: 600px} }
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img {max-width: 640px} }
```
The 640px rule is declared *after* the 768px rule. At any viewport ≥ 768px, both queries match and the 640px rule wins due to cascade order — the 768px rule is effectively dead. Intent appears reversed; images render at 640px max even on wide desktop viewports.

**`maximum-scale=1` restricts zoom (confirmed)**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Prevents users from pinching to zoom, a known accessibility barrier for low-vision users. WCAG 1.4.4 failure.

**No dark mode support (confirmed)**
```html
<meta name="color-scheme" content="light">
<meta name="supported-color-schemes" content="light">
```
Declares light-only. No `@media (prefers-color-scheme: dark)` block visible. Forces light rendering in Apple Mail dark mode, which can cause background color inversions on unstyled elements.

**Duplicate `format-detection` meta tags (confirmed)**
The combined suppression tag and three individual tags repeat the same directives (`telephone=no`, `address=no`, `date=no`). Redundant but harmless; minor HTML hygiene issue.
