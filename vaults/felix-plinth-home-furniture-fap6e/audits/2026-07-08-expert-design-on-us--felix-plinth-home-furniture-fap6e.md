# EXPERT DESIGN, ON US
**Score:** 6/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-08**
## Executive summary

- Fifteen. That's my CB2 email count since July 1st, and I know because I've been counting since around ten when it stopped feeling like marketing and started feeling like surveillance. What saves this one — barely — is that the sender name reads "CB2 Free Design Services" instead of the usual CB2 blast, which made my finger hover instead of immediately swiping. That small distinction matters. It reads like a different department, a different list, a different value proposition.
- And here's the thing: the value proposition is actually good. I am actively renovating two homes simultaneously. A free design consultation backed by a $5,000 refresh concept is exactly the kind of offer I'd pin in my "act on this" inbox folder. Pottery Barn ran a furniture sale email this week that scored high with me because it was specific and served what I actually need right now. This email is trying to do something smarter — sell me a service, not just a SKU — and in a week flooded with percent-off blasts, that's a real differentiator.
- The problem is delivery fatigue has dulled my response curve to nearly anything with a CB2 logo. I'd probably open this on the back of the different sender name, poke around the design services section, and maybe book if the calendar friction is low. I would not forward it. I'm not going to explain to a friend that I've received fifteen CB2 emails this week and here's the good one.

## What's working

- **Sender sub-brand separation.** "CB2 Free Design Services" breaks pattern-recognition fatigue just enough — my brain clocks it as something different before the delete reflex kicks in.
- **$5,000 REFRESH is a specific, evocative number.** It anchors a value (this is a real-dollar service, not fluff) and works as shorthand for "we'll take your room seriously."
- **CTA copy is unambiguous.** "BOOK A FREE DESIGN APPOINTMENT" is a full sentence — verb, adjective, noun — and it does what Pottery Barn's generic buttons often fail to do: it tells me exactly what the click delivers.
- **Product grid is category-appropriate.** The sofas, accent chairs, and lamp groupings in the lower half are exactly what someone shopping for two living rooms wants to browse. Feels curated, not a clearance dump.
- **Visual tone is on-brand.** The dark, moody hero living room with warm lighting is exactly CB2's house style — it doesn't look like it was reskinned from a Crate & Barrel template.

## What's weak

- **Cadence is the enemy of this email.** Fourteen sends before this one in eight days means I'm already half-checked-out before I read a single word. Even a legitimately useful offer suffers when the brand has been crying wolf on urgency for two weeks straight.
- **"EXPERT DESIGN, ON US" is slightly smug.** "On us" lands fine, but "expert design" reads more like a brand assertion than a proof point. I'd like to see credentials — number of rooms completed, designer bios, something — in the email body.
- **The hero text hierarchy is thin.** "$5,000 REFRESH" is the money line but it's competing with a full-bleed atmospheric photo at roughly the same visual weight. On mobile, that's a scroll-before-you-land problem.
- **No urgency signal.** Every other CB2 email this month had a deadline. This one doesn't — which is actually refreshing, but it also means there's no forcing function to book versus bookmark.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Lead the body with designer credentials, not brand copy.** One sentence like "CB2 designers have completed 4,200+ room consultations this year" gives me a reason to trust the appointment before I book it.
- 2. **Add a soft urgency hook** — not a fake countdown, but "appointments fill fast in July" or "next available slot: July 14" would move me from "interesting" to "I should do this today."
- 3. **Surface the design process visually.** Article runs cleaner editorial explanations of what you get. Show me: consult → curated product list → room plan. Three icons, three sentences. That's the decision-maker.
- 4. **Throttle the main list during a sub-brand send.** If I'm going to receive a Design Services email, suppress the next generic sale blast for 48 hours. The two-list firehose is why I missed this the first time I scrolled through.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Free design consult — we'll plan your whole room`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Your room, styled by a CB2 designer. No charge.`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Book a 1:1 appointment and get a curated $5K room plan.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Next available slot fills fast — see what's included.`

**Double-slash in font asset URLs (low severity):**
```
https://www.cb2.com//Styles/CB2/fonts/BrownLight/lineto-brown-light.woff2
```

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Unresolved template placeholder left in production HTML (high severity):**
```html
<!--[IMPUT HERE CLIENT FONT IMPORT SCRIPT if needed]-->
```
This is a developer instruction comment with a typo ("IMPUT") that was never removed. It is inert as a comment but indicates the template was not fully completed before send.

**Non-standard mobile breakpoint (medium severity):**
```css
@media only screen and (max-width: 418px) { .mobile { width:414px !important; ... } }
```
`418px` is not a recognized email client viewport breakpoint. The industry standard is `600px` (desktop→mobile fold) and optionally `480px`. This breakpoint will miss the majority of iOS and Android devices rendering at 375–428px viewport width, causing the desktop layout to render unstyled on most phones.

**`maximum-scale=1` in viewport meta (medium severity — accessibility):**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Prevents pinch-to-zoom. Violates WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text) and is flagged by accessibility scanners. Remove `maximum-scale=1`.

**`-webkit-text-size-adjust: none` on body (low-medium severity):**
```css
body { -webkit-text-size-adjust:none; }
```
Disables browser text-size accessibility adjustments on mobile. Most email guidelines recommend `100%`, not `none`.

**Duplicate `@font-face` for `"Lineto Brown Sub"` (low severity):**
The font family is declared three times — once with only `woff`, once with `eot/woff2/woff`, and twice more with `font-weight: 400` and `font-weight: 600` variant entries. The first declaration (no `font-weight` specified) will shadow the weight-variant declarations in some clients, potentially preventing bold rendering.

**Cross-brand font asset leakage (low severity):**
```css
@font-face { font-family:"HNW05";
  src: url("https://www.crateandbarrel.com/webfonts/1e07d600-...woff2") ... }
```
Four `@font-face` declarations reference `crateandbarrel.com` webfont URLs inside a CB2 email. These are sister-brand assets, not CB2 brand assets. This is almost certainly a copy-paste artifact from a shared template. If Crate & Barrel's CDN path changes or those files are removed, font fallback will silently occur — with no error surfacing to CB2's team.
