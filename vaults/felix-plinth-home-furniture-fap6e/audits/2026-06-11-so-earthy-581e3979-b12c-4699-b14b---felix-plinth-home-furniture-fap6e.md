# SO EARTHY
**Score:** 6/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-11**
## Executive summary

- Twenty-five. Still that number. And what makes today's email genuinely disorienting is the gear shift: yesterday CB2 was screaming "HOURS LEFT: Up to 40% off" in full urgency mode, and now I wake up to "SO EARTHY" — two words of pure aesthetic vibes with no offer anywhere in sight. It's like a car dealership running a screaming clearance event on Sunday and then Monday morning posting moody Instagram content about "the joy of driving." I respect the editorial voice. I actually do. But the whiplash makes it hard to take seriously.
- That said — the sofa in the hero stopped me. I'm actively looking at sofas for the second property right now, and that curved, earthy-brown piece reads like something I'd screenshot into my folder. The "WHY DESIGNERS LOVE A CURVE" headline is doing more work than the subject line did. If I'd gotten that line in my inbox preview instead of "SO EARTHY," I'd have opened this with intent instead of mild curiosity.
- The Pride section in the middle ("DESIGNED WITH PRIDE") lands awkwardly. It's June, it makes sense chronologically, but sandwiched between lifestyle photography and a product grid, it reads more like a checkbox than a conviction. I've seen Article do brand voice more authentically in their outdoor content. This feels bolted in. I'd mark this as read — wouldn't click, wouldn't forward.

## What's working

- The curved sofa hero is genuinely beautiful — earthy brown, warm lighting, the right scale for a living room I'm trying to furnish. It earned a look.
- "SHOP SOFAS" is clean, specific, and honest. Not "Explore," not "Discover." A verb and a noun. Pottery Barn could take notes.
- The "GREEN LINEN" section adds visual variety — the dark moody room with the tall lamp signals a design sensibility that matches where my head is for the second property.
- The product grid at the bottom gives me something to scan without commitment. Good for someone who browses.
- CB2's visual cleanliness is intact — no render breakage, no lorem ipsum, no layout problems.

## What's weak

- "SO EARTHY" as a subject line is the furniture email equivalent of a restaurant naming a dish "Delicious." It tells me nothing about what's inside and gives me zero reason to prioritize the open.
- The hard pivot from sale urgency yesterday to editorial vibe today breaks cadence logic — I feel like I'm being managed rather than communicated with.
- The "DESIGNED WITH PRIDE" section in the middle looks grafted on. The brand voice shifts from design editorial to CSR statement and then back to product. It reads as June-obligatory, not genuine — and that's the wrong impression for a brand I actually respect.
- No offer, no pricing, no sale context at all. After two straight high-urgency sends, this reads like a content day — which is fine — but it's not flagged as such, so I'm left wondering if I missed the sale window.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Put the hero copy in the subject line.** "WHY DESIGNERS LOVE A CURVE" is the actual hook — it has intrigue, it has category, and it would have made me open immediately as someone actively shopping sofas. "SO EARTHY" is Pinterest-caption energy, not inbox-opening energy.
- 2. **Add a price anchor to the hero, even a modest one.** "Starting at $X" or "Free shipping on orders over $Y" — something that converts a browse into a click. Article does this efficiently; they don't let editorial stand alone without a conversion path.
- 3. **Move the Pride section to a standalone email or at minimum give it its own story.** If this is real brand conviction, it deserves a dedicated send. Dropped into the middle of a furniture editorial, it feels like a traffic cone.
- 4. **Add a continuity line.** Something like "Sale ended — here's what we love right now." Acknowledge the cadence shift. Don't leave subscribers wondering if they missed the warehouse window.

### 1. Technical Summary

The template structure is standard and broadly sound, but a development placeholder comment was shipped to production, several font asset URLs contain double-slash path errors, and the mobile viewport configuration creates accessibility compliance issues. ### 2. Link & Tracking Issues

**Double-slash font asset URLs** — At least four `@font-face` declarations use a double-slash after the domain, which forces a server-side redirect on every font load:

```
https://www.cb2.com//Styles/CB2/fonts/BrownLight/lineto-brown-light.woff2
https://www.cb2.com//Styles/CB2/fonts/BrownRegular/lineto-brown-regular.woff2
https://www.cb2.com//Styles/CB2/fonts/GTSectra/GT-Sectra-Display-Regular.woff2
```

The single-path declarations (`https://www.cb2.com/Styles/...`) are correct; the duplicates are not.

**Cross-domain font dependency** — The `HNW05` font family (four weights) is loaded from `crateandbarrel.com`, not `cb2.com`:

```
https://www.crateandbarrel.com/webfonts/1e07d600-3787-4517-bd3d-b140f9e54ec2.woff2
```

This creates a hard runtime dependency on a sibling brand's CDN. If those paths change or CORS policy is tightened, fallback fonts render silently.

**Remainder of links not assessable** — Source is truncated; CTA hrefs, redirect chains, and tracking pixels cannot be verified.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Production template placeholder shipped** — The following development comment appears verbatim in the `<head>`:

```html
<!--[IMPUT HERE CLIENT FONT IMPORT SCRIPT if needed]-->
```

This is an unfilled authoring instruction, not a functional comment. "IMPUT" is also a typo. It is benign to rendering but signals the template was not fully sanitized before send.

**`maximum-scale=1` viewport** — The meta viewport tag:

```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```

Prevents pinch-to-zoom on mobile. This violates WCAG 2.1 SC 1.4.4 (Resize Text) and is flagged by Apple's accessibility guidelines for iOS mail clients.

**`-webkit-text-size-adjust:none` on universal selector** — The rule `*{-webkit-text-size-adjust:none}` in the global styles disables user-controlled font scaling across all elements. Combined with the viewport restriction above, users who rely on system font size settings (accessibility users, older users) receive no override path.

**`mso-line-height-rule: exactly` applied globally** — Applied via `*{}` and repeated on `.spacer,.divider,...`. With `line-height: 100%` also set globally, this combination can clip descenders on taller glyphs in Outlook 2016–2019 when custom fonts fall back to system fonts.
