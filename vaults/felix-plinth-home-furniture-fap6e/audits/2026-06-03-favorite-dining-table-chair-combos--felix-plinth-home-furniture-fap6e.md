# Favorite dining table & chair combos
**Score:** 6/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-03**
## Executive summary

- Nine. We are now at nine Pottery Barn emails in roughly 48 hours, and I want to be precise about what that number means emotionally: I opened this one with pure muscle memory — my thumb had already moved before my brain registered the subject line. And then my brain registered the subject line. Dining table and chair combos. Which is, not even slightly, exactly what I came here for. I have two dining rooms to furnish right now. Two. And Pottery Barn picked this moment — the ninth swing — to finally send the relevant email.
- That's genuinely frustrating in a different way than the irrelevant sends. The outdoor cushion email and the quilt email I deleted on autopilot. This one I actually had to slow down for. The hero is clean — bright room, light oak tones, those linen-y chairs I've been mentally shortlisting since March — and the combo format (table + paired chairs, packaged as a set decision) is smart for someone who keeps waffling on whether to mix or match. There's even a "recommended for you" section that, whether it's truly personalized or just injected by default, at least gestures at treating me like a known customer.
- But I'm not clicking today. Not because the email is bad — it's actually one of the better ones in this sprint — but because nine emails in two days has trained me to assume there's nothing time-sensitive here. If the dining table was going out of stock or there was a real offer attached, PB had eight prior chances to telegraph that. They didn't. So this lands as a browse, not a buy. I'll save it to my email folder, come back on Saturday, and probably lose it in the pile. Forward to a friend? No. Save for myself? Maybe.

## What's working

- **Subject nailed my brief** — "dining table & chair combos" is the literal sentence I've been saying to my partner for three weeks. First Pottery Barn subject in this entire batch that reads like it was written for me specifically.
- **Combo format respects the actual decision** — I don't just need a table; I need a table *and* chairs that go together. Showing them as curated pairings reduces the cognitive load of mix-and-match, which is real friction I hit every time I open Wayfair.
- **Visual tone is right** — the hero room is airy, warm, light-wood, exactly the palette I'm working toward in the second home. It answers "would this live in *my* space" without me having to imagine too hard.
- **"Recommended just for you" block** — even if it's not truly algorithmic, the personalization frame signals that this isn't just a blast. That matters after eight identical-feeling carpet-bombs.
- **Clean layout, no render bugs** — the hierarchy reads hero → pairings → secondary recs, and nothing is broken or overlapping.

## What's weak

- **Cadence debt is massive** — I'm at nine emails in under 48 hours. This could have been a 10/10 send and I'd still be reaching for the mute button. Crate & Barrel sent me one "UP TO 60% OFF" email this same stretch and I remember it. Pottery Barn sent nine and I can't tell you what was in half of them.
- **No discount signal** — Article's been running a clear sale-price rack; CB2 just dropped new markdowns and told me flat-out. This email shows me beautiful dining sets at… full price, presumably. That is a real ask when I know Memorial Day sales just ended and summer sales are probably two weeks out.
- **CTA copy too generic** — the buttons I can see don't read as specific (no "Shop the Amber Wood Set" or "See all dining combos under $X"). They're likely "Shop Now" variants, which is the soft CTA equivalent of a shrug.
- **No dimensions or material callouts in the hero** — as someone staring at a dining room that is 9'4" x 11'2", I need to know if the table I'm eyeing seats six or eight. A quick callout in the product tile would have converted a browse into a configurator click.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Attach a real offer.** Even "free shipping on dining sets this week" would close the gap between browsing and buying. The combo format already implies decision-readiness; give me one nudge to stop deliberating. That is the single highest-impact change.
- 2. **Surface dimensions in the tiles.** "Seats 4–8 / 72"–96" extendable" under each combo image is the one data point that takes someone from "nice photo" to "wait, does this actually fit my room."
- 3. **Throttle the batch.** Send this email *instead of* three of the weaker ones, not in addition to eight others. The outdoor and quilt sends could have been cut entirely and this one would have landed as a hero send.
- 4. **Make the personalization real.** The "Recommended just for you" section should reference something I actually browsed — I've clicked on the Benchwright table twice. Show me that.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**[FAIL] PHP `header()` calls embedded in `<style>` block**

Inside the second `<style>` tag (client styling section):
```css
header('Content-Type: image/jpeg');
header("Cache-Control: no-store, no-cache, must-revalidate, max-age=0");
header("Cache-Control: post-check=0, pre-check=0", false);
header("Pragma: no-cache");
```
These are server-side PHP cache directives, not CSS. They were not executed server-side (they appear literally in the rendered HTML) and are invalid CSS. This is a template/build error — a dynamic image server's PHP snippet was accidentally spliced into the email stylesheet. No rendering breakage in major clients since invalid CSS is ignored, but it indicates a broken build process.
