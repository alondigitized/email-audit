# In stock & in your home soon →
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-03**
## Executive summary

- Five. That's roughly how many emails from the Crate & Barrel family I've opened today — and most of them were Crate & Kids yelling about the 4th of July Warehouse Sale. So when *this* one hit, I actually paused. "In stock & in your home soon" is a completely different pitch. It's speaking directly to the anxiety I live in: I've got a dining room in the second house that needs a table *now*, not in fourteen weeks when backordered furniture finally clears the port. That subject line found the nerve.
- But then I scroll into the email and the editorial promise starts blurring into — yes — the same 4th of July Warehouse Sale that's colonized my inbox all week. The hero sets up a "new look by next week" story, the product grid follows through with furniture I'd actually consider, and then suddenly there's the sale block I've already seen in at least two Crate & Kids sends today. It's like a clean pitch that swallowed a leftover campaign asset.
- I'd scroll through this one, which is more than I can say for the fourth Crate & Kids birthday gifts email that landed this morning. But the click is conditional — I'd need to find a specific product that fits what I'm building, not just a catalog dump. I wouldn't forward it. I'd mark it read, maybe star it, and go back to the Article sale that clearly knew what it was.

## What's working

- **"A new look by next week" is the exact emotional hook for a renovator.** Supply chain anxiety is real; in-stock availability is a selling point that Pottery Barn and CB2 never bothered to surface in their July 4th blasts.
- **The hero image leads with aspirational room context**, not a flat product-on-white. Seeing the outdoor chairs in an actual setting helps me picture them at the second house.
- **The product grid is genuinely varied** — dining chairs, shelving, decorative bowls — which maps to where I actually am in the renovation (filling in the gaps after major furniture is in place).
- **Crate & Barrel proper vs. Crate & Kids is the right distinction.** This email's aesthetic is adult and considered; it doesn't feel like a clearance blitz for baby gear.
- **Visual hierarchy holds up**: hero → product grid → sale block. The eye lands in the right order.

## What's weak

- **The warehouse sale block mid-email ruptures the in-stock narrative.** You've set up a premium promise ("new look, fast delivery") and then slotted in the same 50%-off sale creative I've already seen in two other C&B family sends today. It undercuts the positioning.
- **No shipping specifics visible.** If the whole pitch is "in your home soon," I need to see an estimated delivery window or a cutoff date — not a vague "soon." Article's July 3rd email earned its 9/10 in part because the deadline was explicit and credible.
- **No single hero product I can grab onto.** The grid is a mood board, not a decision point. CB2's "On sale now: decor for indoors & out" at least centered one room story per scroll depth.
- **Cadence damage is already done.** By the time this landed, I was running out of goodwill for the C&B family. Even a good email pays the tax for the four mediocre ones that preceded it today.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Commit to the in-stock story, end to end.** Strip the 4th of July Warehouse Sale block entirely — or run it as a separate send tomorrow. The "in stock" angle is rare and differentiated; diluting it with the boilerplate sale frame wastes the differentiation.
- 2. **Add delivery windows to each product tile.** "Ships in 3–5 days" next to the dining chairs turns this from a browse email into a decision email. That's what closes a renovator who's mid-project.
- 3. **Lead with one hero product** — the outdoor chair or the dining table — before the grid. Give me something to want before you show me the catalog.
- 4. **Pull the preheader into genuine copy that extends the subject.** If I'm reading "In stock & in your home soon →" in the inbox, the preheader should name a room or a specific product. Right now it's doing dead weight.
- 5. **Reference loyalty or credit context** — if C&B has a rewards structure, the email should remind me my points are sitting there. I always route through the highest-rewards card; a loyalty nudge sharpens the click.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Your next room, delivered in days`
- **Subject Alt B:** `These ship fast — here's what's in stock now`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `No backorders. New dining chairs, shelving, and accent pieces ready to ship.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `In stock today, in your home by next week — shop what's actually available.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**[HIGH] Conflicting `img` max-width media queries — wrong cascade order**

```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img {max-width: 600px} }
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img {max-width: 640px} }
```

The 640px rule is declared *after* the 768px rule. On viewports ≥768px, both match; the later 640px rule wins via cascade, capping images at 640px — but then the *earlier* 768px rule would actually apply `max-width: 600px` since it appears first... wait, reversed: 768px query appears first in source, 640px query second. On a ≥768px screen both match; the 640px rule wins (declared last), setting `max-width: 640px`. On a 640–767px screen only the 640px rule matches. The net effect is images cap at 640px even on wide desktop viewports, and the 600px rule is a dead declaration. Swap the order so the 768px breakpoint is last.

**[MEDIUM] `maximum-scale=1` blocks user zoom**

```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```

Prevents pinch-to-zoom in mobile email clients that honor this tag. Violates WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text, Level AA).

**[MEDIUM] `*{-webkit-text-size-adjust:none}` on universal selector**

Applied globally in the first `<style>` block. Overrides the user agent's accessibility text-resize behavior for all elements, not just the intended table cells. This can cause text to render unreadably small on some iOS clients.

**[LOW] Duplicate `format-detection` meta tags**

```html
<meta name="format-detection" content="telephone=no,address=no,email=no,date=no,url=no">
<!-- then three more individual declarations: -->
<meta name="format-detection" content="date=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="address=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="telephone=no">
```

Redundant; the first tag covers all values. Not harmful but indicates template assembly error.

**[LOW] `color-scheme: light` forces light mode only**

```html
<meta name="color-scheme" content="light">
<meta name="supported-color-schemes" content="light">
```

Apple Mail and some mobile clients will respect this and suppress any dark-mode overrides. Intentional if dark mode is unsupported, but should be an explicit product decision, not a default.
