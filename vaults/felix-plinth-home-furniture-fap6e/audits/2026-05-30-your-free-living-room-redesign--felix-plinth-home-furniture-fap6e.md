# Your free living room redesign
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-05-30**
## Executive summary

- Twelve. I'm calling it twelve CB2 family emails in my inbox since mid-May, and this is the one that actually made me stop scrolling. Not because of some clever subject line trick — "Your free living room redesign" is doing pretty basic personalization work — but because it's the first send in this entire run that isn't trying to sell me a lamp or a shelf at a percentage off. This is a service pitch. And I am literally, right now, juggling room layouts for two properties at once.
- The editorial tone holds up. The hero image of that warm, well-collected living room is doing what CB2 does best — aspirational without being cold. The "5 WAYS TO MEET" section is the actual payload here, and it lands clearly: you can do this in person or virtually, there are options, here are buttons. That's useful. That's something I'd clip.
- I'd save this one. I would not forward it — I have a contractor and a design-enthusiast wife who would both be annoyed if I forwarded a brand email instead of just booking the service — but I would absolutely drop it in my inbox folder and probably follow up on the virtual option before our next renovation planning session.

## What's working

- **The subject is a genuine pattern break.** After four-plus CB2 sale emails in a row, "Your free living room redesign" reads differently in the inbox. The word "free" earns its place here because it's tied to a real service, not a shipping threshold.
- **The hero image matches my headspace.** Warm tones, curated layering, that sense of a room that accumulated good pieces over time — this is exactly the mood board I'm trying to build for the second property.
- **"5 WAYS TO MEET" is concrete and low-friction.** Breaking out in-person versus virtual with separate CTAs is smart. I'm not always near a showroom. The virtual option specifically means I don't have to schedule around a job site.
- **The product grid at the bottom is editorial, not pushy.** It's labeled "GET INSPIRED" and feels like supporting context for the design service, not a pivot back to product sales. That sequencing is intentional and I respect it.

## What's weak

- **No specifics on what "free" actually covers.** Does this mean a full room plan? A mood board? One 30-minute call? Crate & Barrel's parent-brand design service emails are clearer about scope. I'm left guessing what I'm booking.
- **The $1,000 figure near the bottom reads ambiguously.** From what I can see, it looks like it might be a spend threshold or a design credit, but it's not immediately clear and I shouldn't have to hunt for the asterisk.
- **No urgency mechanism whatsoever.** This is an evergreen service offer presented as if it's a campaign, but without any deadline it loses stakes. Wayfair's recent "ends June 3" lamp email — annoying as that send was — at least made me feel like I had a decision to make.
- **Email fatigue is real.** This is still email twelve. Even a genuinely useful service pitch carries a tax when it arrives in a stream that already included "OUTDOOR IS ON SALE," wall decor restocks, and color stories. The signal-to-noise ratio is working against them.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Spell out the service tiers in the email body.** One sentence: "Our designers will deliver a full room plan — furniture layout, fabrics, and finishing recommendations." If that's not what it is, say what it is. The ambiguity is the biggest conversion leak here.
- 2. **Give the $1,000 reference a clear label.** If that's a trade-up credit or minimum spend requirement, say so plainly. Don't make me find out at checkout.
- 3. **Add one real deadline.** Even "Book by June 15 for summer scheduling priority" would give this a reason to act now rather than passively save it.
- 4. **Compress the product grid or remove it.** The "GET INSPIRED" section dilutes the service pitch. This email should end at the meeting CTA.

- **Subject Alt B:** `Free room plan from our in-house designers`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Virtual or in-person — pick the format that works for you`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Full room layout, furniture plan, and finishing touches. No charge.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Confirmed issues:**

- **`-webkit-text-size-adjust: none` via wildcard selector.** The rule `*{-webkit-text-size-adjust:none; -ms-text-size-adjust:none; ...}` overrides the correct `body { -webkit-text-size-adjust: 100% }` declaration set 12 lines earlier. The `none` value disables automatic font scaling on iOS Safari, making small text unreadable without zoom.

- **`maximum-scale=1` in viewport meta** (`<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />`). Disables pinch-to-zoom, a documented WCAG 2.1 failure (Success Criterion 1.4.4). Recommend removing `maximum-scale=1`.

- **Mobile breakpoint too narrow.** The sole mobile breakpoint fires at `max-width: 418px`. Common modern viewports (iPhone 16 Pro Max: 430px, Pixel 9: 412px landscape) fall outside this range. The `.mobile`, `.hide`, `.block`, and font-size overrides will not apply for those devices. Recommend raising to `max-width: 430px` or `max-width: 480px`.

- **Conflicting desktop `img` rules.** Two `@media` blocks set conflicting `img { max-width }`:
  - `min-width: 768px` → `max-width: 100% !important`
  - `min-width: 690px` → `max-width: 690px`
  
  At 768px+, both rules match; the `!important` on the wider breakpoint wins, meaning images stretch to 100% of the container regardless of the 690px cap. The ordering should be reversed (690px block declared last) or collapsed into one rule.

- **Double-slash paths in font URLs.** Three `@font-face` `src` values contain `//` after the domain:
  - `https://www.cb2.com//Styles/CB2/fonts/BrownLight/lineto-brown-light.woff2`
  - `https://www.cb2.com//Styles/CB2/fonts/BrownRegular/lineto-brown-regular.woff2`
  - `https://www.cb2.com//Styles/CB2/fonts/GTSectra/GT-Sectra-Display-Regular.woff2`
  
  Most HTTP servers normalize this, but some CDN/WAF configurations return 301 or 404 on double-slash paths, causing font fallback to Helvetica/sans-serif.
