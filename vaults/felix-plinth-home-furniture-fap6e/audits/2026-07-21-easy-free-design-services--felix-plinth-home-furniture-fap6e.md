# Easy & FREE design services
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-21**
## Executive summary

- Thirty-four. That's Pottery Barn in July for me now, and I say that upfront because it's the only honest frame for evaluating this one. But here's the thing — this is actually the email from them I've been most inclined to act on all month. Not because it's a flash sale, not because there's a sweepstakes I'm not going to win, but because it's offering something I could genuinely use: a free design consultation while I'm juggling two simultaneous renovation projects. That's a service-based pitch, which is rare from PB and harder to dismiss.
- The hero is clean — "RSVP to Free Design Help," a readable breakdown of what you get (personalized suggestions, all budget ranges, virtual or in-home), and a button that says exactly what it does. Below that it gets a little scattered — lifestyle image tiles for curtains, lighting, gallery walls — but the top half earns its keep. The recommended products at the bottom are a nice pull, though small at this image scale.
- I'd probably click the appointment button. I wouldn't forward this to a friend — it's utility for my situation, not a social share. But compared to the gift card sweepstakes they sent me yesterday and the digital catalog from Sunday morning, this one at least respects that I have a real project going on.

## What's working

- **The hero offer is genuinely relevant.** "Free design help" for someone renovating two homes simultaneously is not a throwaway pitch — it's actual utility I can use, which almost no PB email this month has managed.
- **CTA copy is the most specific I've seen from them all July.** "BOOK YOUR FREE APPOINTMENT" is direct and action-oriented — the catalog email from Sunday didn't have anything close to this clarity.
- **Breaking down the service benefits in the hero block works.** Three-point list under the headline (personalized suggestions, all budgets, virtual or in-home) lets me evaluate the offer without clicking. Article did this well on their dining room send last week; PB is finally doing it here.
- **No discount being faked.** The offer is "free service," which is honest — not a sketchy 40% off with eight exclusions buried below the fold.
- **Pottery Barn credit card mention in the footer.** I see my 10% back in rewards callout, which is relevant since I'll be routing renovation purchases through that card anyway.

## What's weak

- **The lower grid undercuts the clarity of the top.** After a clean service pitch, pivoting to four lifestyle tiles (curtains, lighting, gallery walls, decor) reads like they bolted on the generic batch content underneath the real email. It dilutes the message.
- **"Pottery Barn Design Services" as the sender name is slightly disorienting.** Thirty-three previous emails this month from "Pottery Barn" — and now a sub-brand sender? My reflex was to check if this was a phishing spoof before I relaxed.
- **No urgency signal anywhere.** The free appointment is great but there's no "limited slots," no "book by X date" — it floats in time, which reduces the impulse to act now versus "maybe later this week."
- **The recommended products section is too small to be actionable.** I can barely make out what the items are. CB2 runs much tighter product cards with visible pricing — PB is making me squint.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Kill the lifestyle grid tiles below the hero or make them service-adjacent.** "See projects our designers have completed" or before/afters would reinforce the design services pitch. What I'm seeing now — curtains, lighting, gallery walls as separate shop-now tiles — breaks the coherent message. Ship the lifestyle content in a separate email.
- 2. **Add a scarcity or time signal to the appointment CTA.** "Limited appointments this month" or "Book before August — slots fill fast in summer" would move this from passive browsing to immediate action. I want to click but I also feel no pressure to do it right now.
- 3. **Make product recommendations legible.** If I'm supposedly getting personalized picks, they need to be big enough to actually evaluate. Wayfair's product grid in their free-shipping email last week at least showed prices inline. PB is making me do extra work.
- 4. **Fix the sender display name for established subscribers.** Let "Pottery Barn" be the sender even for service sub-brands — I almost filtered this before opening it.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**PHP header directives in `<style>` block (invalid HTML)**
```html
<style>
  header('Content-Type: image/jpeg');
  header("Cache-Control: no-store, no-cache, must-revalidate, max-age=0");
  ...
</style>
```
PHP server-side function calls are present verbatim inside a CSS `<style>` tag. These are not valid CSS and indicate a template rendering failure — the PHP was not executed and leaked raw into the HTML output. Email clients will ignore these as unparseable CSS, so no visual breakage, but it signals a broken template compilation step that should be investigated.

**Missing alt text — all body images (34 instances)**
Every content image in the send has `alt=""`, including:
- All 8 hero/body slices (`slices_01.jpg` through `slices_08.jpg`)
- All 18 footer slices
- 6 product recommendation images (bed, chair, towel, rug, quilt, curtain rod)
- Tracking/beacon images

Recipients with images disabled (common in corporate email clients) will see a blank email. Screen reader users receive no content description. This is a systemic template-level omission, not isolated to this send.

**HTTP (non-HTTPS) image sources — 2 instances**
```
http://edm.potterybarn.com/pb/thekey/CC_Banner/PB/Foot_NonMem_PB.jpg
http://edm.potterybarn.com/pb/2022/0311_Key_CC_Email_BrandBanner/slices_02.jpg
```

Mixed-content HTTP sources. Gmail, Outlook.com, and Apple Mail proxy or block non-HTTPS images. These footer assets will likely not render for a significant portion of recipients. Both assets are from 2022 — likely legacy footer templates that were never upgraded to HTTPS.

**Responsive breakpoint mismatch**
Two separate media query breakpoints are declared: `max-width: 480px` (in the first `<style>` block) and `max-width: 650px` (in a third `<style>` block). The 650px query hides/shows `.mobile-hide`/`.mobile-show` classes, but no elements in the truncated source visibly use those classes on the main content. Potential dead CSS or inconsistent mobile behavior depending on full template.
