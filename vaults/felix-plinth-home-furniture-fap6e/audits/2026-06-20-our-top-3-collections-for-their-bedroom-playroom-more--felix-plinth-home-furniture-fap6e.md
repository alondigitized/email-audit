# Our top 3 collections for their bedroom, playroom & more
**Score:** 6/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-20**
## Executive summary

- Here we are again. Yesterday's Crate & Kids email was all cribs, rocking chairs, and nursery dressers — I noted then that I do not have a nursery — and now they're back with collections for bedrooms, playrooms, and "more." The word "their" in the subject is doing some heavy lifting, and it is not lifting anything that maps to my life. Two homes mid-renovation, two adults, zero playrooms.
- That said, the email itself is genuinely well-produced. The photography is warm and aspirational, the three-collection structure delivers what the subject promises, and the layout holds together top to bottom with no visible bugs. If I had a seven-year-old or a niece with a birthday coming up, this would probably earn a click. I don't, and it doesn't.
- What gnaws at me is the contrast with the wine glass "Period." email earlier this week — that one actually stopped my scroll. This one doesn't even earn a pause. This is the sixth-or-so C&B family send in roughly 48 hours, and Crate & Kids has now used two of those slots on nursery and kids'-room content aimed at a demographic I'm not in. I'm marking it read and moving on.

## What's working

- **Structure matches the promise:** The email delivers three visible collection blocks exactly as the subject advertises — bedroom, playroom, and a third — without burying any of them in the scroll. That discipline is rarer than it should be.
- **Photography is strong:** The hero bedroom setup is styled the way a design-literate parent would actually pin it — pendant light, warm wood tones, considered accessories. Not generic.
- **Clean render, no visible bugs:** The layout holds. No overlapping text, no broken image placeholders, no junk preview leaking through. C&B's production quality is consistently solid across the entire family.
- **Brand identification is instant:** The Crate & Kids mark at the top is legible immediately. I knew who sent this before I read a word of copy.

## What's weak

- **Complete audience miss, second day in a row:** Yesterday was nursery furniture. Today is bedroom and playroom collections. Nothing in my purchase history or browsing behavior signals that I have young children. This is two sends in 24 hours firing at the wrong person.
- **No offer, no price anchor:** Pottery Barn's "On sale & ready-to-ship!" send last week came with a clear hook. This reads as a pure editorial lookbook — no discount percentage, no "from $X," no urgency mechanism of any kind. For a brand that ran three warehouse sale emails in the same 48-hour window, a collection showcase with no price signal lands flat.
- **Cadence has destroyed the goodwill:** Six-plus C&B family emails in two days. I've started counting, and counting means I've checked out. The wine glass email broke through because it was genuinely different. This one arrives in a dead zone of my own making.
- **"& more" in the subject is wasted real estate:** Either name the third category or cut it. "More" tells me nothing and makes the subject feel padded.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Segment out non-parents first.** Single highest-leverage change. If I've never bought a children's product from C&B or Crate & Kids, there is zero reason to fire two kids'-room sends at me in 24 hours. The data exists. Use it.
- 2. **Give me a reason why now.** Even a pure editorial send needs an anchor — "just arrived," a design-team pick, a seasonal peg. Without it this reads like automated calendar content, not a brand with a point of view.
- 3. **Add a price signal.** "From $X" under each collection header is all it takes to shift this from aspiration to action. CB2's "NOW & FUTURE CLASSICS ON SALE" email — which arrived in the same brutal inbox week — at least gave me something to act on.
- 4. **Name the collections, not the rooms.** "Maison collection" or "Scout collection" functions as a real reference. "Their bedroom" is meeting-agenda copy. Pottery Barn names its lines and so should this.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Confirmed issues:**

**a. `maximum-scale=1` on viewport meta** — blocks user pinch-zoom on mobile, which is a WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text) failure:
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```

**b. Conflicting desktop-breakpoint image rules** — two `min-width` media queries set contradictory `max-width` caps on `img`:
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img {max-width: 640px} }
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img {max-width: 600px} }
```
At ≥768px (true desktop), images are capped at **600px**, narrower than the 640px midpoint cap. If the container is 640px wide, desktop images will be clipped 40px narrower than intended. The 768px rule should likely be `max-width: 640px` (or removed).

**c. Global `line-height: 100%` via `*` selector** — applied universally, this collapses line-height on multi-line text blocks and can cause overlapping text in Gmail and Outlook Web:
```css
*{...; line-height: 100%}
```
Should be scoped to specific elements, not `*`.

**d. `-webkit-text-size-adjust: none` on `*`** — disables iOS text-size adjustment globally. Can cause unreadably small text if a table cell is narrower than the viewport. Should be `100%`, not `none`.

**e. Duplicate `format-detection` meta tags** — `telephone=no`, `address=no`, and `date=no` are each declared twice across two separate meta tags. Redundant but harmless; indicates template hygiene issues:
```html
<meta name="format-detection" content="telephone=no,address=no,email=no,date=no,url=no">
...
<meta name="format-detection" content="date=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="address=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="telephone=no">
```

**Unverifiable:** `alt` text on product images cannot be confirmed from the truncated source.
