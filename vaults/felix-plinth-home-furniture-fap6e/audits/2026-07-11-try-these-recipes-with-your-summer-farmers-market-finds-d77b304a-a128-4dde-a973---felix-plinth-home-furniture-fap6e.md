# Try these recipes with your summer farmers market finds →
**Score:** 4/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-11**
## Executive summary

- Twenty-one. That's where the Crate & Barrel family count sits as of today, July 11th, after I went back and tallied since July 8th. And the brand's chosen vehicle for send number twenty-one is... a recipe newsletter. Grilled corn with chipotle butter. A salmon bowl situation. A blush-pink cocktail. This email would be perfectly at home in my Bon Appétit subscription or in a meal kit brand's welcome series. What it is not is a communication from a home goods retailer to a man who currently has two houses in active renovation and a 14-item cart sitting in crateandbarrel.com.
- The editorial content is competently assembled — the food photography is clean, the layout is scroll-friendly, and the seasonal summer peg is at least defensible as a calendar rationale. There's a product section buried down the page featuring some cookware, which is presumably the actual commercial hook. But by the time I've scrolled through four recipe cards to get there, I've already left the email mentally.
- I would not click. I would not forward this to a friend — unless that friend had asked me "does Crate & Barrel do recipe content now?" and I wanted to provide evidence. Mark as read and move on.

## What's working

- **Seasonal coherence**: Summer farmers market is an actual moment in time, not a made-up sale occasion — that's more than I can say for half the Crate & Barrel blast cadence this week.
- **Clean layout, no render bugs**: The food photography is crisp, the green CTAs are legible, and nothing is broken or overlapping — this at least loads without insult.
- **Cookware product section exists**: Burying a dutch oven and supporting cast below the recipe content is the wrong call, but at least the commercial throughline is there somewhere.
- **Brand logo and color system are consistent**: I knew immediately this was Crate & Barrel and not a phishing attempt, which after seventeen-plus sends is a low bar to clear but worth noting.

## What's weak

- **Complete category miss**: I'm in the middle of sourcing lighting, a sectional, and bathroom accessories for two properties simultaneously. A chipotle butter recipe is not in my decision stack right now. Article just sent me a tight "new arrivals just landed" email four days ago that felt targeted. This feels like it was addressed to someone's aunt.
- **Product section is invisible from the scroll entry point**: The only commercial reason to send this email — the cookware — doesn't appear until you've committed to reading through the food editorial. Wrong order of operations.
- **No offer whatsoever**: Pottery Barn's last email led with "up to 70% off." CB2 ran a classics sale. This email asks me to make tacos. There's no discount, no free shipping call-out, no member pricing — nothing that gives me a reason to transact.
- **The cadence makes this feel disposable**: When you're on email twenty-one in under four days, sending a content email is the equivalent of using a timeout when the points are already out of reach. The trust account is overdrawn and a recipe isn't a deposit.
- **The green CTA buttons lead to recipes, not products**: Every call to action I can see in the upper two-thirds is pointing me toward food content. I don't want to cook a Crate & Barrel recipe. I want to know if that 5-qt dutch oven ships in under a week.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Invert the email entirely**: Lead with the cookware products and let the recipe be the contextual wrapper — "The tools behind this summer dish" — not the other way around. Make the commercial intent primary.
- 2. **Add a concrete offer to justify the send**: Even a "free shipping on orders $75+" in the hero would give me a reason to click before I mentally check out. CB2's "NOW & FUTURE CLASSICS ON SALE" email at least gave me a commercial reason to engage.
- 3. **Pare the recipe content to one featured item**: One hero recipe, one anchor product it pairs with, one CTA. Right now this reads like a blog post that forgot it was an email.
- 4. **Segment before you send**: If I've been browsing lighting and rugs on crateandbarrel.com for three months, a recipe email should never clear the suppression logic.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Duplicate `format-detection` meta tags (confirmed):**
```html

<!-- Then repeated individually -->

```
Redundant — does not break rendering but is dead markup.

**`maximum-scale=1` in viewport (confirmed accessibility issue):**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Prevents users from pinching-to-zoom. Fails WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text). Remove `maximum-scale=1`.

**Conflicting `img max-width` media queries (confirmed):**
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img {max-width: 600px} }
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img {max-width: 640px} }
```
At 768px+, both rules match. The 640px rule is declared last and wins, capping images at 640px — overriding the 600px intent. Reorder or collapse into a single rule.

**Aggressive global `text-size-adjust: none` (confirmed):**
```css
*{-webkit-text-size-adjust:none; -ms-text-size-adjust:none; ...}
```
Applied to `*` selector. Combined with `line-height: 100%` on the same rule, this can cause illegible text on mobile if font sizes are small. Standard practice is to apply only to `body`.

**No dark mode support declared:**
```html
<meta name="color-scheme" content="light">
<meta name="supported-color-schemes" content="light">
```
`light` only. Apple Mail and some Android clients will force-invert colors if dark mode is active, potentially inverting backgrounds and text. Acceptable tradeoff if intentional, but should be documented.
