# Early birds get the A+—shop our new back-to-school lineup!
**Score:** 4/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-11**
## Executive summary

- Thirty-eight. Maybe thirty-nine — I've genuinely lost the running count at this point. What I know is that the Crate & Barrel family has been carpet-bombing my inbox for days, and this one opened to a wall of colorful backpacks. *Backpacks.* I don't have kids. I'm not back-to-school shopping. I'm trying to figure out which dining table survives two households simultaneously, and Crate & Kids just slid a rainbow of children's book bags in front of me at 9am.
- To their credit, the email itself is visually competent. The hero — a grid or wall display of multicolored backpacks — reads immediately. The Crate & Kids sub-branding is clear, the layout has decent hierarchy, and the lifestyle shots of organized kids' rooms are honestly pretty well-styled. This is a competent kids' email. It's just not an email for *me*, and at this point in the C&B relationship I'm reading it the same way I read a Pottery Barn Kids catalog that accidentally ended up in my mailbox: I acknowledge that it exists and someone cares about it, and then I put it down.
- I would not click. I would not forward. I would mark-as-read and quietly wonder if their list segmentation is running on a spreadsheet from 2014.

## What's working

- **Hero is immediately legible.** That colorful backpack wall hits fast — you know exactly what the email is about before you read a single word, which is good email craft.
- **Sub-brand clarity.** "Crate & Kids" is distinct enough from the parent that I know immediately this is the children's division, not the main line. Clean co-branding.
- **Room lifestyle imagery mid-scroll.** The styled kids' bedroom shots are genuinely good — organized, aspirational, consistent with the C&B aesthetic I actually like. If I had a kid, these would go in my saved-emails folder.
- **No render bugs.** After all the C&B sends I've processed this week, at minimum everything loads. No broken images, no lorem ipsum, no overlapping text.

## What's weak

- **Complete demographic mismatch.** I do not have children. This email is zero percent aimed at me. Wayfair managed to hit me with a summer sale that at least covered furniture and decor; this is entirely juvenile products with no crossover for an adult buyer.
- **Back to school in June feels aggressive.** Even if I *did* have kids, we're on June 11. The "early birds" framing is doing a lot of heavy lifting to justify why I should think about pencil cases before my kid's school year is even officially over.
- **Cadence fatigue is a real tax on engagement.** Article sent one outdoor email four days after another, and it felt like good timing. CB2's warehouse sale landed and I actually read it. This is the C&B family's Nth send in a matter of days, and the category switch to kids does nothing to reset the clock for me — it just adds a wrong-audience penalty on top of the existing fatigue penalty.
- **No offer hook visible in the hero.** I can see the products and the concept, but I didn't immediately see a concrete discount or promo in the above-fold section. "Early birds get the A+" is a subject-line gag, not a savings number.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Suppress this from non-parent segments entirely.** If there's any behavioral or declared data suggesting I don't have kids — and there should be, given my purchase and browse history skews entirely toward adult home goods — this email should not reach me. That's table stakes for a brand at C&B's tier. CB2 managed to target me with a warehouse sale that made sense; their sibling brand should be doing the same work.
- 2. **Lead with an offer number, not a wordplay subject line.** "Early birds get the A+" is cute, but it doesn't tell me anything about savings, timing, or product. The Pottery Barn "Feel at home faster: Quick-ship furniture & more" subject I read recently was less witty but miles more useful because it answered an actual question I had. Give me a percent or a deadline.
- 3. **Add a crossover module for adults if the email must reach me.** Even within a kids send, you could add a "finishing touches for the whole home" rail — a lamp, a rug, a storage solution that works in any room. Wayfair does this constantly: kid stuff and then "while you're here, here's the adult version." Missed revenue.
- 4. **Rethink send frequency from the whole C&B family.** This isn't a single-email fix, but someone at that company needs to look at the combined sends across Crate & Barrel, CB2, and Crate & Kids for a single subscriber and set a household cap. I'm in all three lists and it's become white noise.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Confirmed issue — `<title>` brand mismatch:**
```html
<title>Crate & Barrel</title>
```
Sender is "Crate & Kids" but the title element says "Crate & Barrel". Some clients (Apple Mail, Outlook web) surface `<title>` in tab labels or accessibility trees. Should be `<title>Crate & Kids</title>`.

**Confirmed issue — conflicting desktop `img` max-width breakpoints:**
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img {max-width: 600px} }
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img {max-width: 640px} }
```
The 640px rule appears after the 768px rule and has equal or lower specificity. At viewports ≥768px, both rules match — cascade order gives `640px` the win, overriding the intended `600px` cap. Images will render 40px wider than intended on desktop. Reorder so the higher breakpoint is last, or use a single rule.

**Confirmed issue — template developer comment left in production:**
```html
<!--[IMPUT HERE CLIENT FONT IMPORT SCRIPT if needed]-->
```
"IMPUT" is a typo for "INPUT". This raw placeholder comment is visible in source. Remove before send; it exposes build template internals.

**Minor — duplicate `format-detection` meta tags:**
`format-detection` is declared three times (once combined, twice individually). Redundant but harmless. Clean up for hygiene.

**Minor — aggressive global `line-height` reset:**
```css
* { line-height: 100% }
```
Combined with the `max-height: 1000000px` and `-webkit-text-size-adjust: none` on `*`, this is standard email boilerplate but can cause accessibility issues in some screen readers (NVDA, VoiceOver) when line-height is inherited at 100% on interactive elements. Not a blocker but worth noting.
