#  ⏱️  BIG summer DEALS ⏱️ 
**Score:** 6/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-09**
## Executive summary

- Email number — I've lost count. After the bath mats email that had no bath mats, after the third consecutive 8'×10' outdoor rug subject line, after the "Surprise" 70% off that opened to a generic storefront, Wayfair is back with timer emojis and the word "BIG" in mixed case. That subject line alone tells me everything: no one sat down and thought about what I might actually need this week. Someone scheduled a batch send and called it summer marketing.
- Inside the email it's exactly what I expected — a purple-and-yellow grid of competing category tiles. TV stands, camping furniture, bookcases, outdoor sets, bathroom, bedding. Every tile has a percentage off. Nothing connects. There's no through-line, no story, no "here's what's relevant to someone who just bought a sectional from us." The "picked just for you" row in the middle is doing heavy lifting with zero evidence it actually knows me.
- I will not click. I will not forward. I will mark it read and move on the same way I moved on from the last four. The only outcome of this send is that my Wayfair folder is getting a filter.

## What's working

- **Free delivery threshold is front and center.** "$35+" callout is visible mid-email — that's a useful nudge and Wayfair deserves credit for keeping it in the layout consistently.
- **Star ratings on product tiles.** I can see review counts on individual products, which is more than CB2 typically drops in a sale grid. Small thing, but it builds purchase confidence faster than a bare product photo.
- **The 70% off callout is hard to miss.** The yellow "70% OFF" tile in the top row catches the eye. If you're browsing for opportunistic furniture, that number stops the scroll.
- **Wayfair Rewards is visible in the footer.** I use my rewards, and at least they're reminding me it exists.

## What's weak

- **No hierarchy whatsoever.** Every tile is competing at the same visual weight. This is the opposite of how Pottery Barn runs their indigo email — one hero, one message, one click. Here I don't know where to look first, so I look nowhere.
- **Subject line is doing the wrong kind of work.** Two timer emojis plus "BIG" in caps plus "DEALS" in caps reads like a spam filter test, not a trusted brand. Article managed "Last chance: 4th of July Sale ends today" with zero emoji and it felt more urgent.
- **"Picked just for you" is unconvincing.** Outdoor furniture next to bookcases next to patio sets. Either the algorithm is broad to the point of uselessness, or they're not actually using my purchase history.
- **No actual deadline in the body.** The timers in the subject imply urgency; the email body gives me no end date. That's bait-and-switch at the subject-to-body level. Article showed "ends today" in both places. This shows it in neither.
- **Cadence fatigue is real.** This is landing after what feels like a daily barrage. CB2's "NOW & FUTURE CLASSICS" email — despite being deep in its own volume problem — at least carried a consistent creative angle. This feels like a gap-fill between campaign sends.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Pick one category and own it.** I have two homes in active renovation. If Wayfair looked at my order history, they'd know I'm in outdoor furniture mode this month. One tight email — "Upgrade the back patio before summer ends" — would have a click rate this scatter-grid never will.
- 2. **Put a real deadline in the body.** If the timer in the subject means something, say "Sale ends Sunday, July 13." If it doesn't mean anything, drop the emoji.
- 3. **Cut the tile count in half.** The "picked just for you" row should be three products max, pulled from my browse/purchase history. Right now it's a category dump with a personalization label on it.
- 4. **Rotate cadence.** At this volume, even a great email gets ignored. One per week, category-focused, would outperform five-per-week generic blasts on any metric.

- **Subject Alt B:** `Your patio upgrade: 40–70% off, free delivery over $35`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Ends Sunday. Rugs, seating, and shade — handpicked from your recent saves.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Your Wayfair Rewards apply. Shop the sale before it closes.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Confirmed issues:**

- **Fixed heights on mobile product elements** clip content when text wraps beyond expected length:
  - `.ProductRec-stars, .ProductRec-shippingCopy { height: 16px }` — star ratings and shipping copy will be clipped if font scaling is active
  - `.ProductRec-price { height: 40px }` and `.ProductRec-salesCopy { height: 28px }` — prices with long strings (e.g., multi-currency or longer sale copy) will be cut off silently
  - `.u-HalfWidthBlock .EventBlock-name { height: 48px !important }` — same risk on event name blocks

- **`word-break: break-all` on `.ProductRec-name`** — overly aggressive; breaks at any character boundary, not word boundaries. Should be `word-break: break-word` or `overflow-wrap: break-word` to avoid mid-word breaks on product names.

- **`min-width: 100vw` via `u ~ div`** — the Gmail dark-mode/width hack is present and correct, but it applies globally. If any non-full-width container follows a `<u>` tag, it will be forced to 100vw on mobile Gmail.

- **XHTML 1.0 Transitional DOCTYPE** — appropriate for email; no issue.

- **Sofia font `@font-face`** — `mso-font-alt: "Arial"` fallback is correctly set for Outlook. No issue.

- **iOS-specific `@supports (-webkit-touch-callout: none)` block** for half-width image stacking — present and correct.

- **Alt text on images** — not auditable from truncated source.
