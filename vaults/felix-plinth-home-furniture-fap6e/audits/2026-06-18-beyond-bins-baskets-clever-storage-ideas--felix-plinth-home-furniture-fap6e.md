# Beyond bins & baskets: clever storage ideas!
**Score:** 5/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-18**
## Executive summary

- Seventy-seven. That's the C&B family inbox counter now, and I need to say it plainly: June 18 has already handed me two warehouse sale emails from Crate & Barrel proper and a "Grow with them furniture for every kids' space" from this exact address earlier today. This storage editorial is the fourth C&B family email in a single day. By the time I get here, I'm operating on fumes of goodwill toward the parent brand.
- Taken on its own, the email is competent. The photography is clean and aspirational — naturally finished wood shelving, woven baskets, that soft pink-and-cream palette that the Crate & Kids catalog does well. The editorial angle of "storage beyond the obvious" is a better hook than just another product dump. But there's no offer here. No percentage off, no free shipping threshold, no member pricing. It's a long scroll of look-at-this-stuff with scattered CTAs and no anchor to pull me toward the cart. I can feel myself saving it to my "nice ideas" folder and moving on.
- The deeper problem: I'm not sure this was meant for me at all. Everything in this email speaks to someone with a toddler's room or a nursery to outfit — cube organizers, low-profile bookshelves with bin rails, pegboard wall setups scaled for small humans. I'm renovating two homes, but nothing in my behavior said "I need kids' storage." Pottery Barn has been doing a better job of reading the room lately — their "NEW finishes for pieces you love" send earlier this week felt targeted at someone with a real project. This feels like a batch-and-blast to the full C&K list. I'll mark it read, not click.

## What's working

- The editorial frame "beyond bins & baskets" is genuinely sharp copywriting — it implies curation and sets an expectation of surprise, which is more inviting than "shop our storage collection"
- Photography is consistently styled and well-lit; the room vignettes feel livable, not catalog-stagey
- Layout is clean with no broken images or render bugs — this email renders correctly and is readable without zooming on mobile
- Multiple product sections are organized into digestible visual rows, which suits my browse-and-pin workflow
- Brand voice is coherent throughout — the soft, natural-materials tone is consistent from hero to footer

## What's weak

- Zero offer — no discount, no shipping incentive, no loyalty pricing — so there's no behavioral hook pulling me from browse to buy today versus next week
- Second Crate & Kids email of the day after "Grow with them furniture"; the cadence damage is cumulative and real
- No single hero product or focal point — the eye has nowhere to rest and everything competes equally for attention
- Skews heavily toward parents of young kids; without a signal that I have a kids' space to furnish, this targeting is a guess
- No urgency whatsoever — storage ideas don't expire, and the email gives me no reason to act now over next month

## Recommendations

- 1. **Add an offer or create scarcity.** Even a "free shipping on orders over $99 through Sunday" banner converts this from a magazine spread into an email. CB2's floor sample send last week — one hero, clear savings — is still the comparison point here. Pure editorial doesn't move carts from email.
- 2. **Pick one hero product and commit.** The multi-section layout diffuses everything. Lead with one unexpected storage solution, name a price, and let the rest of the email support it. One thing I remember is better than ten things I scroll past.
- 3. **Segment on kids'-room browse history.** If I haven't touched the kids' section, don't send me this. If I have, the subject line practically writes itself around what I looked at.
- 4. **One C&B family email per day, max.** The fourth send in a calendar day is self-defeating — at that point the brand is training me to snooze the folder, not open it.
- 5. **Push the subject line's promise into the body.** "Beyond bins & baskets" teases a reveal; the email should immediately show me the non-obvious storage move in the first scroll, not bury it in section three.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**A. `maximum-scale=1` blocks user zoom (WCAG 1.4.4 violation)**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Prevents pinch-to-zoom on mobile. Combined with the `*` rule below, this is a compounding accessibility failure.

**B. Global text-size-adjust suppressed on all elements**
```css
* { -webkit-text-size-adjust: none; -ms-text-size-adjust: none; }
```
The wildcard selector overrides the safer `body { -webkit-text-size-adjust: 100%; }` set elsewhere. Users relying on browser text scaling get no adjustment. Scope this to `body` or `.appleLinks` only.

**C. Brand/title mismatch**
```html
<title>Crate & Barrel</title>
```
Sender is `crateandkids@mail.crateandbarrel.com` / "Crate & Kids". The `<title>` is displayed in some email clients (Gmail web, Apple Mail tab) and will show the parent brand, not the sub-brand. Should be `<title>Crate & Kids</title>`.

**D. Conflicting `img max-width` media queries**
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img { max-width: 600px } }
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img { max-width: 640px } }
```
The 640px rule fires first, then the 768px rule overrides it — so viewports ≥768px cap images at 600px (narrower than the 640px cap). These are reversed: the wider viewport ends up with smaller images. Swap the max-width values or consolidate into a single rule.

**E. `line-height: 100%` on `*` selector**
```css
* { line-height: 100%; }
```
Setting `line-height` as a percentage on all elements causes inheritance issues in some clients (e.g. Outlook) where 100% resolves relative to font-size and can cause clipped descenders on large headings.

**F. Duplicate `format-detection` meta tags**
One combined tag is followed by three individual repetitions. Redundant; no functional breakage but indicates sloppy template hygiene.
