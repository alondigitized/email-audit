# Marisol, get $10 off your $50 purchase⁺ or $20 off your $100 purchase⁺! Terms & excl. apply
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-05-21**
## Executive summary

- Okay so this is the second Ulta tiered spend-and-save email I've seen *today* — the brand history is literally showing me the "Rae" version of this same exact email, same offer, same barcode energy, same Memorial Day hook, just with a different name swapped in. That's not personalization, that's a mail merge. Ulta sent the same coupon blast to Marisol that they sent to Rae, and I'm sitting here doing the exact same mental math: $10 off $50 is 20%, $20 off $100 is 20% — but that second tier is where I'd actually overspend to optimize, so it's working on me even though I know exactly what they're doing.
- The email itself is cleanly executed — the tiered offer is hero-positioned, the barcode is right there, and the bonus free insulated tote (limited quantities, points-math people will sprint for this) plus the 5X points Saturday hook are genuinely useful signals. The loyalty mechanics are present and visible, which I respect. But nothing in this email tells me I'm Vera Glow, Diamond status, with a documented purchase history. It tells me I'm "Marisol."
- I'd click — I always click Ulta spend-and-save because I will absolutely engineer a $100 basket. But I wouldn't forward it. This is a solo errand, not a hot tip.

## What's working

- **Tiered offer is math-bait, and it works.** The $20 off $100 tier is a 20% effective discount — my Diamond brain clocks this instantly and starts building a mental basket.
- **Barcode is right there in the hero.** No hunting. Screenshot it, walk into the store, done.
- **Free insulated tote is a smart GWP layer.** Seasonal, limited-quantities urgency, and it looks like a cute summer-ready bag — product-adjacent enough to feel on-brand.
- **5X points for BOPIS or Saturday in-store** is exactly the kind of loyalty mechanic I care about. That's Diamond math working in real time.
- **Rewards section personalizes at the bottom** — "Hi, Marisol" with the full account block. At least the backend knows who she is even if the hero doesn't.

## What's weak

- **This is the Rae email with a name swap.** Brand history shows the same tiered offer, same creative, sent to a different persona earlier today. The "personalization" ends at the merge field. Compare this to Charlotte Tilbury's 20% off send (scored 9/10) — CT makes the hero image do real product storytelling even on a discount email. Ulta just makes a coupon.
- **Subject line is 90+ characters.** On mobile this truncates immediately after "Marisol, get $10 off your $50 purchase⁺" — the $20/$100 tier, which is the better offer, gets buried. e.l.f. keeps subjects punchy at under 45 chars. This reads like it was written for desktop first.
- **"Terms & excl. apply" in the subject.** Yes, it's honest — I respect the disclosure — but it also makes my fingers hover. Ulta exclusions can be brutal (prestige brands, gift sets, salon). There's no clarity in the email about what's actually excluded.
- **No product specificity anywhere in the hero.** This is a pure coupon email. There's nothing telling me what to buy, no category curated for me, no "your most-repurchased items are on sale." ILIA's loyalty email (scored 7) at least anchored to a specific product promise.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Cut the subject line in half and lead with the better tier.** The $20 off is the conversion driver — bury the lede no more.
- 2. **Add one curated product row personalized to Marisol's actual category.** "Because you love skincare, here's what counts toward your $100." Drunk Elephant does product-forward storytelling in their hero; Ulta could too.
- 3. **Name the exclusions upfront in one line.** "Prestige brands and gift sets excluded" — just say it. The fine print fear costs more clicks than the disclosure would.
- 4. **Tighten the free tote CTA.** "Grab the tote" with a quantity counter would sharpen the urgency.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Marisol: $20 off your $100 starts now — shop Memorial Day deals`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Your $20-off coupon is here, Marisol. Use it before Monday.`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Free insulated tote with qualifying purchase — limited stock, first come.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `5X points this Saturday + your spend-and-save code inside.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Issue 1 — All links stripped of visible styling (high severity)**

```css
#MessageViewBody a {
  color: inherit !important;
  text-decoration: none !important;
  …
}
```

This rule fires in most webmail clients (Gmail, Outlook.com, Yahoo). Every `<a>` in the email body will render identically to surrounding text — no underline, no color differentiation. This is a WCAG 2.1 SC 1.4.1 failure (use of color alone) and will degrade click rates on text links.

Same pattern repeated in `.appleLinks a`.

**Issue 2 — Google Fonts `<link>` will be silently dropped**

```html
<link href="https://fonts.googleapis.com/css2?family=DM+Sans…" rel="stylesheet">
```

Gmail strips `<link>` tags from `<head>`. Outlook ignores web fonts entirely. The declared fallback in `.helvetica` (`Helvetica, Arial, sans-serif`) is valid, but any element that relies on DM Sans without also having the `.helvetica` class will fall back to the client's default serif, not the intended stack. Audit all font-family declarations in the full source to confirm fallback coverage.

**Issue 3 — `translate="no"` on `<html>` element**

```html
<html lang="en-US" translate="no" …>
```

Disables in-browser translation for all recipients. Intentional for brand-name accuracy, but blocks access for non-English speakers. If this is deliberate policy, no action needed — but it should be a documented decision, not a silent default.

**Issue 4 — Dead CSS shipped to production**

Approximately 100+ lines of hamburger-menu CSS are wrapped in a comment (`/* 91925 Commenting out hamburger menu … END Hamburger Menu */`) but still present in the payload. This adds bytes to every send and provides no function. Strip it at the template level.
