# Brighten Up for Summer ✨
**Score:** 9/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-05**
## Executive summary

- Sally Beauty, again — I literally just got one two days ago. I'm not annoyed yet, but I'm keeping count. "Brighten Up for Summer" in the subject told me absolutely nothing; it could be nail polish, it could be SPF moisturizer, it could be a seasonal home goods sale at Target. It was the preheader that actually made me tap: "$15.99 Wella Lightener Bundle" is a real number attached to a real product from a brand I recognize.
- Inside, it snapped into focus fast. "Lighten up for summer, *no salon required*" — that framing works on me. I have zero hours to sit in a salon chair between school pickups and weekend soccer, and $15.99 for a bundle that does what a colorist would do is the exact pitch I respond to. The product breakdown tiles below the hero are doing real work: I can see what's in the kit (powder lightener, developer, post-color treatment), which removes the "wait, is this actually complete?" suspicion.
- Do I click? Honestly, maybe — depends on where my roots are this week. I would not forward this to a friend without first knowing she's actively lightening her hair. This is a niche send that will feel extremely targeted to maybe a third of Sally's list and completely irrelevant to everyone else. Mark as read for most people, potential purchase for the right subset.

## What's working

- "No salon required" reframes DIY as empowerment, not compromise — that's the right emotional angle for a time-strapped mom.
- $15.99 is right there in the hero, no scrolling required. I know what I'd be paying before I've even processed the rest of the email.
- The three individual product tiles below the hero show me exactly what the bundle contains, which builds trust that I'm getting a complete kit and not just half a solution.
- Sally Rewards nudge ("Earn $5 when you spend $50") is well-placed. I see it, I'm a member, it registers as a reason to spend.
- The "Free hair color advice / Get personalized recommendations" section is a genuine differentiator — Sally acting like a service, not just a store.
- Klarna visible at the bottom. For a beauty purchase, installment payment is a nice low-friction trust signal for the budget-conscious.

## What's weak

- Subject line did the preheader's job and the preheader did the subject's job — they're swapped. "Brighten Up for Summer" is so generic it should be the teaser, not the headline.
- The preheader opens with "Sally Beauty" — that's already my sender name. Those are the most valuable characters in the inbox view and they're wasted on a brand repeat.
- Two emails in two days (June 3 was the summer hair survival kit, June 5 is this). I noticed. I'm starting to file Sally in the "they mail a lot" mental category.
- Zero urgency signals anywhere. No "this week only," no "ends Sunday." A $15.99 bundle with no deadline reads as evergreen — easy to defer, easy to forget.

## Recommendations

- 1. Put the price and product in the subject line — the preheader is currently carrying the entire open weight; don't make subscribers work that hard.
- 2. Add a soft deadline anywhere ("through Sunday," "this week only") to shift "I'll come back to it" into "I should grab this now."
- 3. Move the Licensed Colorist Consultation CTA up, ideally into or just below the hero — it's Sally's most unique offer and it's buried below three product tiles.
- 4. Strip "Sally Beauty" from the start of the preheader and use those characters for the hook.
- 5. Consider a cadence break — a third email in five days is starting to train my thumb toward the archive button.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Malformed `<meta>` tags (confirmed):**

```html
<!-- Missing http-equiv attribute — charset declaration is a no-op -->
<meta content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />

<!-- Missing http-equiv attribute — IE compatibility directive ignored -->
<meta content="IE=edge" />
```

Both tags omit the required `http-equiv` attribute. Correct forms:
```html
<meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />
<meta http-equiv="X-UA-Compatible" content="IE=edge" />
```

**Web font strategy — fallback gap (confirmed):**

`Founders Grotesk` is declared as the primary font in the global `<a>` rule:
```css
a { font-family: 'Founders Grotesk', Helvetica, sans-serif; }
```
No `@font-face` declaration for `Founders Grotesk` exists in the visible `<head>`. The six `@font-face` blocks load Open Sans variants only. All Gmail and Outlook recipients will render link text in Helvetica/sans-serif — acceptable if intentional, but if Founders Grotesk is the brand typeface it is silently missing for the majority of the audience.

**External Google Fonts requests blocked in Gmail (confirmed):**

The `@font-face` rules reference `fonts.gstatic.com`:
```css
url(https://fonts.gstatic.com/s/opensans/v17/...)
```
Gmail strips `<head>` `<style>` blocks and blocks external font fetches via CSP. Outlook has no web font support at all. Open Sans will never load for ~60%+ of typical commercial email audiences; fallback stacks must carry the full visual weight. This is expected behavior but should be validated against the fallback rendering.

**Open Sans version is outdated (minor):**

`v17` is referenced; the current Google Fonts Open Sans release is `v29+`. No functional rendering difference, but version drift can cause cache misses on clients that do honor the fetch.

**`img { white-space: pre; }` — unusual rule (confirmed):**

```css
img { -ms-interpolation-mode: bicubic; white-space: pre; }
```
`white-space: pre` on `<img>` is a non-standard pairing. It has no effect on images themselves but can affect adjacent inline text nodes in some rendering engines. Recommend removing — `bicubic` interpolation is the only intended declaration here.
