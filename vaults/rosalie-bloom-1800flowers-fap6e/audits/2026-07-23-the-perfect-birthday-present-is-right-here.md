# The perfect birthday present is right here 🍬 🎁 ⬇️ 💝 💐
**Score:** 6/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-23**
## Executive summary

- This is 1-800-Flowers running an editorial "My Summer Birthday Faves" roundup — a numbered listicle of five products dressed in a personal-curation frame. The format is a hard pivot from the sharp, single-product double-blooms creative that scored 8–9/10 on July 20–21. Where those sends had one hero, one price, one CTA, this one gives you five products, nine shop-by-category tiles, a Celebration Passport sign-up block, app download links, and a $15.88 credit banner at the top — all competing for your attention simultaneously.
- The $15.88 credit in the green top banner is the only real offer, and it immediately triggers pattern recognition: the brand ran an identical credit-hook on July 14 and July 16, both of which scored 5/10 with notes about bait-and-switch risk. The amount changed by 88 cents but the mechanic is the same. Subscribers who noticed last time will notice again.
- The subject line is the biggest single failure here. Five emojis stacked at the end (🍬 🎁 ⬇️ 💝 💐) lands in spam-signal territory — there is no thematic coherence between a candy emoji and a birthday gift email about flowers — and "The perfect birthday present is right here" is as vague as it gets. There is no product, no price, no deadline. Compare it to "DOUBLE = 💐💐" (July 20, 9/10): three words, one emoji, complete idea. This subject is the opposite.
- **Bottom line:** an unfocused catalogue send with a recycled credit hook and a cluttered subject line. The editorial "faves" concept is interesting in theory but needs a single hero product and a real price to pay it off.

## What's working

- **"Summer Birthday Faves" editorial framing** is a warmer tone than a straight promo blast — the curation angle has potential differentiation from the house cadence.
- **Product variety is actually relevant** — mixing florals (#1, #3, #5), chocolate-dipped strawberries (#2), and a gift basket (#4) acknowledges that not every birthday recipient wants flowers; breadth works here.
- **Celebration Passport block** is cleanly executed at the bottom — the loyalty CTA is visually distinct and doesn't compete aggressively with the product section.
- **No broken images or render bugs** — all five product images load, the layout holds, text and images are not overlapping.

## What's weak

- **No prices visible on any of the five products** — the credit offer means nothing without an anchor price; readers can't calculate savings.
- **$15.88 credit banner is the third iteration of this mechanic since July 14** — subscribers who've been opening these emails are likely fatigued by it; the odd cent ($15.88 vs. $15) feels arbitrary rather than precise.
- **Five emojis in the subject line** (🍬 🎁 ⬇️ 💝 💐) signals inbox noise rather than curation — the candy emoji has no evident connection to birthday flowers.
- **Nine shop-by-category tiles below the product list scatter click intent** entirely — this feels like a homepage link dump bolted onto an editorial send.
- **No CTAs with specific verb + noun copy visible under each product** — it's a visual list with no obvious action to take on each item.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Pick one hero product from the five and build the email around it** — if #1 (Summer Beginnings Bouquet) is the seasonal lead, put it above the fold with a visible price, the credit applied, and a single "Send This" CTA. The listicle can be below-the-fold secondary content.
- 2. **Show the price math** — "$X with your $15.88 credit" removes ambiguity and transforms the banner from noise into signal. Right now the credit floats without an anchor.
- 3. **Cut the subject to one idea, one emoji** — the five-emoji stack is doing harm.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Your $15.88 credit picks the perfect summer birthday gift`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Summer Beginnings Bouquet — $15.88 off today`
- 4. **Rewrite the preheader to deliver the price** — pair it with the subject so the inbox preview reads as a complete offer.
- **Preheader Alt A:** `5 birthday picks — one credit, one checkout. Here's our summer shortlist.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Apply your credit to the Summer Beginnings Bouquet — ships today.`
- 5. **Remove or collapse the shop-by-category grid** — it belongs on the homepage, not in an editorial send. Replace it with a single "Shop Birthday" link in the footer.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**[HIGH] `maximum-scale=1` in viewport meta suppresses pinch-to-zoom**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Prevents users from zooming on mobile. Accessibility failure (WCAG 1.4.4). Remove `maximum-scale=1`.

**[HIGH] `-webkit-text-size-adjust: none` on universal selector**
```css
* { -webkit-text-size-adjust: none; -ms-text-size-adjust: none; ... }
```
Disables OS-level text scaling for all elements. Standard practice uses `100%`, not `none`. Change to `-webkit-text-size-adjust: 100%`.

**[MEDIUM] `line-height: 100%` on universal selector**
```css
* { line-height: 100%; }
```
This collapses relative line-height inheritance throughout the document. Any text element without an explicit `line-height` override will render with collapsed leading. Should be scoped to `td` or removed from `*`.

**[MEDIUM] Non-standard mobile breakpoint: 418px**
```css
@media only screen and (max-width: 418px) { ... }
```
Common device widths are 375px (iPhone SE/standard), 390px (iPhone 14/15), 430px (iPhone 14/15 Pro Max). A 418px breakpoint misses 390–417px devices — a significant share of the iPhone install base — which will render the desktop layout at those widths.

**[LOW] Playfair Display 900-weight uses `.woff` instead of `.woff2`**
```css
src: url(...nuFvD-...fsukDT.woff) format('woff');
```
All other `@font-face` declarations use `.woff2`. The 900-weight file is ~30% larger unnecessarily. Use the `.woff2` variant.

**[LOW] `mso-font-alt` fallback missing on all Lato `@font-face` declarations**
Playfair Display includes `mso-font-alt: 'Times New Roman'` (on some weights). The Lato declarations have no `mso-font-alt`, so Outlook will fall back to its default system font unpredictably rather than a specified fallback like `Arial`.

**[INFO] Google Fonts loaded via external `@font-face` URLs**
`fonts.gstatic.com` requests are blocked by Gmail (strips `<style>` blocks with external URLs), Apple Mail (cached locally), and Outlook desktop. The custom font stack will silently fail in the majority of clients. This is expected/acceptable only if a system-font fallback stack is declared on all text elements — verify in full source.
