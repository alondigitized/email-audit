# Your $15 Credit—Use It for Their Birthday 🎉
**Score:** 5/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-14**
## Executive summary

- This is 1-800-Flowers running the same "We Know Birthdays" creative from the July 7 send — hero headline, editorial bouquet photography, testimonial block, shop-by-category grid — and dressing it in a new subject-line hook: a $15 credit. The problem: the $15 credit is nowhere prominent in the body. The top promo banner is so small it barely registers, and the email's visual story is entirely about brand equity ("We Know Birthdays") and category discovery, not redeeming a credit. That's two consecutive sends now — July 13's $13.80 credit, today's $15 credit — where the subject promises a specific dollar amount and the body doesn't deliver it front and center. Subscribers who notice the pattern stop trusting the subject line.
- What works in the body itself is the testimonial block, which is the most credible thing in the scroll. A real customer quote with a lifestyle photo adds warmth that the July 7 version was missing. The shop-by-category grid is also solid merchandising. But the disconnect between promise and delivery is the dominant story, and it erodes whatever goodwill the stronger creative earns.
- The cadence is exhausting. This is the seventh email in fourteen days. The July 11 "All Favorites. All Celebration. Up to 25% Off" send already felt like a retread; this one recycles the same visual frame yet again. At some point subscribers stop opening not because the subject is bad but because they know what's inside.

## What's working

- **Testimonial block lands.** A real quote with a photo ("My friends and families who have summer birthdays are extra lucky…") is the kind of social proof that was absent in the July 7 birthday reset send — it earns trust.
- **Shop by Category grid is functional.** Multiple product tiles give the subscriber somewhere to go even if the hero doesn't hook them — reduces dead-end exits.
- **"We Know Birthdays" brand anchor is credible.** The hero headline is a genuine positioning statement, not a throwaway promotional line, and the bouquet photography is consistently high quality.
- **Subject length is mobile-friendly** (~44 chars before emoji) and the dollar-amount specificity earns the click better than the generic July 7 subject did.

## What's weak

- **$15 credit is not visible in the body.** The subject promises a specific credit; the email never redeems that promise visibly — same bait-and-switch pattern as July 13's $13.80 email, which scored 4/10 here specifically for this reason.
- **Creative fatigue is real.** The "We Know Birthdays" hero is a direct reprise of the July 7 send. At 7 emails in 14 days, loyal subscribers recognize the template before they read the copy.
- **No explicit expiry on the credit.** Even if the $15 credit were prominent, there's no "expires tonight" or "valid through Sunday" urgency signal visible anywhere in the body.
- **Top promo banner is unreadable at glance.** The yellow strip at the top is too small and low-contrast to communicate whatever offer lives there — most subscribers will skip past it.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Put the $15 credit in the hero, in large type.** "You have $15 to spend — use it on a birthday bouquet" as the H1, with a redemption button directly beneath it. The creative equity of "We Know Birthdays" can live below the fold. Don't make subscribers hunt for what the subject promised.
- 2. **Add an expiry to the credit.** "Expires July 16" creates genuine urgency without manufactured pressure — and it's honest.
- 3. **Break the "We Know Birthdays" creative cycle.** This is the third send with the same hero concept. Swap in a new product angle (a specific arrangement, a new plant line, a "last-minute same-day delivery" hook) to restore novelty.
- 4. **Cut send cadence.** Seven emails in two weeks is the single biggest suppressor of open rates in this audit window. Even one fewer send per week would reduce fatigue unsubscribes.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Your $15 credit expires soon — pick their birthday flowers`
- **Subject Alt B:** `$15 off their birthday bouquet — claim it before it's gone`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Tap to apply your credit at checkout — delivered same day.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Fresh arrangements, $15 off. Offer ends July 16.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Confirmed issue — redundant overlapping `img` max-width rules:**
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img {max-width:640px} }
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img {max-width:640px} }
```
The `min-width: 768px` rule is entirely subsumed by the `min-width: 640px` rule — both produce the same effect. Dead code; remove the 768px block.

**Confirmed issue — `supported-color-schemes` in CSS `:root` is non-standard:**
```css
:root { color-scheme: light dark; supported-color-schemes: light dark; }
```
`supported-color-schemes` is only valid as an HTML `<meta>` tag, not a CSS property. It has no effect here and adds noise. The `<meta>` tags in `<head>` already handle this correctly.

**Confirmed issue — Playfair Display weight-900 falls back to `.woff` instead of `.woff2`:**
```css
src:url(https://fonts.gstatic.com/s/playfairdisplay/v22/...btM.woff) format('woff');
```
All other `@font-face` blocks use `.woff2`. This weight gets a larger, older format file. Fix to `.woff2`.

**Confirmed issue — mobile breakpoint at 418px is below standard:**
```css
@media only screen and (max-width: 418px) { ... }
```
This targets roughly iPhone SE (1st gen) width only. Any viewport between 419px and ~599px receives no responsive styles. Standard practice is `max-width: 600px` or `max-width: 480px`. Content will render unoptimized on most modern phones.

**Confirmed issue — aggressive universal `line-height: 100%`:**
```css
* { line-height: 100%; }
```
This collapses line spacing on all elements. Multi-line text nodes (body copy, disclaimers, nav) will have zero leading. This is a known email hack to override Outlook, but it must be paired with per-element overrides on text containers or readability suffers.

**Note — `@font-face` won't render in Gmail or Outlook:** Fallbacks (`mso-font-alt: 'Times New Roman'`) are present for Outlook, which is correct. Gmail will silently drop all `@font-face` blocks. Verify the fallback stack (`font-family` declarations on actual elements) is acceptable at render.
