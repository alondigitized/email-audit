# Summer pieces everyone's wearing
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-04**
## Executive summary

- Okay so ASOS hit me again — one day after the "New arrivals" send from June 3rd, they're back in my inbox with "Summer pieces everyone's wearing." At this point I've gotten five ASOS emails in about nine days and my thumb is hovering over unsubscribe with increasing enthusiasm. The email itself is actually... fine? The grid of summer dresses is pretty, models are diverse, the content feels on-season. But there is no offer anywhere in this email. No discount. No free shipping. Nothing. It's a pure editorial shop, which is a bold choice when H&M and Zara are both in my inbox this week with actual hook copy and collab energy.
- The South Beach x Sasha Corey collab section is the only thing that snaps me out of scroll mode — a named collab is trend-signaling, and it's the kind of specific thing I'd screenshot for my group chat. But that section is buried below four other categories and feels like an afterthought instead of the lead.
- I'd mark this read and scroll past. I wouldn't forward. If they'd led with the collab and offered even 20% off, I'd probably have clicked.

## What's working

- **Hero grid is visually strong.** The mosaic of summer dresses — sage green maxi, chocolate brown column, the yellow skirt set — actually looks curated and on-trend, not just catalog filler.
- **Model diversity is real.** Different skin tones, different body types across the grid. ASOS consistently does this better than H&M, whose "Your summer update is here" email from this week used a much more homogenous casting.
- **Category architecture is clear.** Oceanswear → Graphic Tees → Edit Shoes → Collab — the scroll has a logic to it, even if none of those sections have offers.
- **South Beach x Sasha Corey name-drop.** A branded collab section, even if understated, is the kind of trend-credibility signal I'm actually looking for. Zara's "CARAMEL LONDON x ZARA" did this harder and better in the subject line, but at least ASOS got it in the body.
- **Clean render.** No broken images, no layout blowouts, readable text throughout.

## What's weak

- **Zero offer anywhere.** Not a single price, discount, or free-shipping mention. I'm literally doing three-tab compares every weekend — if there's nothing to compare on price, I'm just looking at vibes.
- **Back-to-back sends with no escalation.** Yesterday was "New arrivals." Today is "Summer pieces everyone's wearing." These feel like the same email wearing different subject-line clothes. Compare this to Zara, which at least pivoted from their Men send to a new collab drop — that's narrative. This is just ASOS existing in my inbox again.
- **South Beach x Sasha Corey buried at position five.** If that collab is the freshest thing in this email, it should be the hero, not the fifth scroll.
- **"SHOP ALL" and "SHOP NOW" CTAs everywhere.** Every section has the same generic button. Even "Shop the Collab" would've added intent. Zara's collab emails at least name the specific thing you're clicking into.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Lead with the collab.** Flip the email so South Beach x Sasha Corey is the hero — a named collab drop is the most trend-differentiated thing in here. Move the generic category grid below.
- 2. **Add one actual offer.** Even "Free next-day delivery this weekend" changes the calculus. Without any offer, this email is editorial content, not email marketing.
- 3. **Cool the send cadence.** Five emails in nine days with largely similar content is how you train subscribers to ignore you. The June 3 "New arrivals" and this June 4 send should have been one email.
- 4. **Personalize at least one section.** Show me categories based on my browse history — even "based on what you've been looking at" copy would feel more targeted than a broadcast grid.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Empty preheader div** — The hidden preheader element contains no text:
```html
<div style="display:none !important;visibility:hidden;...overflow:hidden;"></div>
```
Email clients (Gmail, Outlook, Apple Mail) will fall back to pulling the first visible body text as preview copy. This is a content-delivery defect regardless of intent.

**`-webkit-text-size-adjust: none` on `body`** — Set to `none` rather than `100%`. Although `* { -webkit-text-size-adjust: 100% }` later overrides it for child elements, the `body` rule has non-zero specificity risk in some parsing environments and contradicts WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text). Prefer `100%` uniformly.

**`font-display: swap` in `@font-face`** — This property has no effect in any email client (it is a browser-only CSS descriptor). Harmless but indicates CSS copied from a web stylesheet without email-specific review.

**`@keyframes ticker` animation** — Only rendered in Apple Mail and a small subset of modern clients. The `@supports` guard is present and correct:
```css
@supports (animation: ticker 5s linear infinite) { .fallback { display: none !important; } }
```
Progressive enhancement is handled properly; no action required.

**Mobile breakpoint at 460px** — Flagship viewport widths (iPhone 16 Pro Max: 430px, Galaxy S24+: 412px) fall below this threshold and will receive the mobile layout. However, devices with viewports between 461–600px (some tablets, landscape phablets) receive the desktop layout with no intermediate adaptation. Flag for QA on those device classes.

**Duplicate charset declaration** — Both `<meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=UTF-8">` and `<meta charset="utf-8">` are present. The redundancy is intentional (the `http-equiv` form targets legacy Outlook) and is not a bug.

**Image `alt` attributes and table `role="presentation"`** — Cannot verify from truncated source. Both must be present in production HTML for accessibility compliance.
