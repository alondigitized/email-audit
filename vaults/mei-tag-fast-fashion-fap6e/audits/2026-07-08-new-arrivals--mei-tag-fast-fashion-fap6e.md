# New arrivals
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-08**
## Executive summary

- So ASOS just landed in my inbox again — that is number eight if I'm counting, which I am, because at this point my inbox feels like their personal marketing test lab. After yesterday's palette editorial actually got me genuinely excited about the brand again, today they're back to "New arrivals" with exactly zero concept and zero offer. The subject line is twelve characters and says literally nothing I didn't already know. I am on your list. I know you have new arrivals.
- Here's the thing though — the actual products aren't bad at all. The sage green one-shoulder cami leading the grid is pure TikTok coastal-girl energy, the yellow textured mini is peak dopamine dressing for right now, and the dark floral midi at the bottom right is the screenshot-and-send-to-friends type of piece. The merch team showed up. The email team just handed them a blank brief and called it a day.
- Would I click? Maybe — but only because I already had the app open on my phone. Would I forward this to a friend? Hard no. There is nothing to forward. This is a passive scroll-past on a Tuesday morning, and after the creative bar they set yesterday, the drop feels worse than it should.

## What's working

- The sage green asymmetric cami leading the hero grid is a legitimate trend moment — if I saw that piece on my FYP I would come looking for it and ASOS surfacing it first matters
- Model diversity across the grid is solid — different skin tones, different body types, all young-presenting, all looking like actual people from my campus
- The yellow lime mini in the bottom row is on-mood for summer 2026 dopamine color energy and it earns its spot
- Zero render bugs — clean layout, images load, text is readable at mobile size without zooming, ASOS consistently gets the technical stuff right
- The "Shop Our Top Brands" row quietly featuring New Balance and Levi's is doing work — both are genuinely having a massive cultural moment and I'd actually click those logos

## What's weak

- "New arrivals" as a subject line after eight emails in nine days is genuinely the blandest possible move — give me a trend name, a vibe, a colorway, literally anything with a pulse
- No offer anywhere — not even a free-shipping hook — and after "30% extra off Sale" just a few days ago, dropping to full-price new-in with zero reason to act now is a conversion cliff
- The "Download the App" yellow bar at the bottom is aggressively optimistic for an email I barely opened; nobody is converting to app installs from a new-arrivals send
- The top-right product shot (white ribbed top + silver mules, no model, no context) breaks the editorial energy of every other image in the grid — it feels like a product-page placeholder that snuck into the lookbook
- Yesterday's palette email had an actual idea behind it; today's email has a grid and a button — the tonal whiplash within 24 hours makes ASOS feel like two different teams who don't talk

## Recommendations

- 1. **Name the trend, not the category.** "New arrivals" is a placeholder, not a hook. Lead with what these specific pieces *are*. The sage green asymmetric top has a TikTok name — use it. Make me feel like ASOS is inside the same algorithm I'm living in, not just pointing at a category page.
- 2. **Pull one hero product out of the grid and tell its story.** H&M's campus lookbook email last week had a premise. Even Zara — as wrong-demo as their Home email was — commits to a concept. A single hero item with a sentence of copy beats six equal-weight thumbnails with no hierarchy.
- 3. **Put the Maia collab / creator peg in the subject line.** If the subtext is referencing a specific creator, that name should be leading — creator affiliation is the single highest-trust signal for someone shopping my category. Burying it in the preheader is leaving conversion on the table.
- 4. **Kill or replace the app download bar.** Use that strip for a Premier callout, a "now in your size" nudge, or anything that rewards me for being a known subscriber rather than asking me for something.
- 5. **Space the sends.** Send fatigue is real. Even a 9/10 creative is fighting uphill as the eighth email in nine days.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Critical — Missing `<head>` element:**
```html
<!DOCTYPE html> <html><body>
  <div style="display:none !important;..."></div>
  <p>
    <meta http-equiv="Content-Type" ...>   ← inside <body><p>
    <meta charset="utf-8">
    <style>...</style>
    <title>New arrivals</title>
```
All `<meta>` and `<style>` declarations are nested inside `<body>` inside a `<p>` tag. Browsers and most email clients are tolerant of this (they hoist `<meta>`/`<style>` via their error-recovery parser), but Outlook's Word rendering engine and some older mobile clients do not. This can cause charset misdetection, broken responsive CSS, and missing font declarations in affected clients.

**Preheader div is empty:**
```html
<div style="display:none !important;visibility:hidden;..."></div>
```
No preheader text is present. Most email clients will pull the first visible body text as inbox preview copy — which in this case is whatever comes immediately after the empty div. This is a content gap but creates a technical risk of rendering boilerplate (e.g., "View in browser" link text) as the snippet.

**Outdated Outlook conditional comment target:**
```html
<!--[if gt mso 15]>
```
`mso 15` = Outlook 2003. This conditional fires for all Outlook versions above 2003. The correct modern target for all Outlook desktop is `[if mso]` or `[if gte mso 9]`. The current selector works but is semantically stale and could silently mis-target if the logic is ever extended.

**Duplicate charset declarations:**
Both `<meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=UTF-8">` and `<meta charset="utf-8">` are present. Redundant but harmless.

**`font-display: swap` in @font-face:**
This CSS property is web-browser-only; it is silently ignored by every email client. Including it adds noise but causes no harm.

**`font-size: 0em` on all table elements:**
```css
table, tr, td, th { font-size: 0em; ... }
```
Standard gap-suppression technique for email, but requires explicit `font-size` overrides on every content cell. If any `<td>` containing text is missing a font-size reset, text renders invisibly. Cannot verify coverage from truncated source.

**SVG font format in @font-face stack:**
```css
url("...FuturaPT-Book.svg") format("svg")
```
SVG fonts are unsupported in all modern email clients and browsers. Dead weight; minor.

**Responsive breakpoint at 460px:**
```css
@media screen and (max-width:460px)
```
Standard industry breakpoint is 480px–600px. At 460px, narrow-viewport Android devices (commonly 360–414px wide) are covered, but devices in the 461–479px range get desktop layout on a mobile screen. Risk depends on product image grid — cannot verify from truncated source.

**Alt text on images:** Cannot audit — `<img>` tags not present in truncated excerpt. Required for accessibility and clients with images blocked.
