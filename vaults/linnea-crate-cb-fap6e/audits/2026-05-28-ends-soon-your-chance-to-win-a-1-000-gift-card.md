# ENDS SOON! Your chance to win a $1,000 Gift Card
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-05-28**
## Executive summary

- This is Crate & Kids' second email on 5/28 — landing the same day as their "Love them now & forever" send (scored 7.00) — and it's a jarring pivot: a sweepstakes hook ("win a $1,000 gift card") grafted onto a standard product-editorial template. The body beneath the hero is actually good: personalized product recommendations with visible prices, a summer category hook, the CrateKid$ loyalty play, and a design consultation offer. But the sweepstakes hero is dead weight — it doesn't drive immediate purchase intent, the "ENDS SOON" urgency is undated and therefore hollow, and it adds a spammy ALL CAPS tone that undercuts the brand's earned credibility.
- The structural problem is a mismatched promise: the subject says "win," but the body is really selling kids' furniture and summer gear. If someone opens to enter a contest, they don't want to shop. If someone opens to shop, the contest preamble is a distraction. Neither audience gets a clean experience.
- The cadence call is the bigger issue. Three emails from the C&B/C&K family in a single day — after five days of Memorial Day urgency blasts — is subscriber fatigue territory. This send would've worked better as a standalone, cadence-isolated sweep or dropped from the calendar entirely in favor of letting the morning "Love them now" email breathe.

## What's working

- **Product recommendation grid ("we love these for you")** is the strongest section — personalized, price-tagged, and visually clean; this is what drives clicks.
- **Summer category framing ("gear up for summer")** is timely and gives the mid-email content a coherent seasonal anchor.
- **CrateKid$ loyalty section** is a smart inclusion — reinforces membership value and cross-sells the credit product without interrupting the shopping flow.
- **Visual render is clean** — no broken images, no overlapping text, consistent typography throughout.
- **$1,000 specificity in the subject** is the one concrete, scannable signal in the inbox preview.

## What's weak

- **Sweepstakes hero displaces a purchase offer** — the top fold sells a chance to win, not a reason to buy; that's the wrong call for a promotional calendar already saturated with urgency.
- **"ENDS SOON!" with no visible deadline date** — vague urgency is worse than no urgency; it reads as boilerplate and erodes trust.
- **ALL CAPS "ENDS SOON!" in subject** is a spam trigger and clashes with C&K's otherwise warm, editorial tone.
- **Two C&K emails on the same day** ("Love them now & forever" + this one) is overexposure — the combined effect hurts deliverability reputation and trains subscribers to tune out.
- **The hero-to-body disconnect** — a subscriber who clicks to "enter to win" is exiting the email, not browsing the product sections below; the CTA structure works against the email's own monetization.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Kill the sweepstakes hero or demote it to a sidebar module.** Lead with the summer product story ("finishing touches for their dream room") or the personalized product grid. The $1,000 sweepstakes, if it must exist, works better as a module below the fold — it's a loyalty perk, not a hero offer.
- 2. **Add a concrete deadline to any urgency claim.** "Ends June 1" converts orders of magnitude better than "ENDS SOON."
- 3. **Drop one of today's C&K sends.** This email and "Love them now & forever" are competing for the same subscriber on the same day. Pick one.
- 4. **Rewrite "Shop Now" CTAs to category-specific copy** — "Shop Summer Gear," "Shop Cribs & Beds," etc. — to match the section context and drive more intentional clicks.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Enter to win $1,000 — plus summer picks we love`
- **Subject Alt B:** `$1,000 gift card: enter before it closes + shop summer`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `New arrivals for their room, gear for the season, and one big giveaway.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Summer picks, finishing touches — and a chance to win $1K.`

**[CONFIRMED] Conflicting `img` max-width breakpoints**
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img { max-width: 600px } }
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img { max-width: 640px } }
```
Both rules are present. On viewports ≥ 768px both fire; the 768px rule is declared later, so it wins and clamps images to 600px. On 640–767px viewports only the second rule fires (640px). The ordering is inverted from intent — the 640px rule should come first so the 768px rule properly overrides it. This is consistent behavior, but the narrower desktop cap (600px on a 768px+ screen) may cause blurring of any image intended to fill a 640px container.

**[CONFIRMED] Duplicate meta `format-detection` declarations**

<meta name="format-detection" content="address=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="telephone=no">

The first declaration covers all five. The three individual tags below it are redundant. No functional breakage, but bloats `<head>` unnecessarily.
