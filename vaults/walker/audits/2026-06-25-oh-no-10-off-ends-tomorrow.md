# Oh no, $10 off ends tomorrow!
**Score:** 9/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-20**
## Executive summary

- DSW in the sender column, my thumb was already moving. That muscle memory is real — I've opened almost every DSW email for the past two weeks straight. "Oh no, $10 off ends tomorrow" is trying to sound casual and friendly, like a buddy nudging me before a deal slips. I'll give it that — it's more human than the "HAPPENING NOW: $10 OFF!!" version from June 19. But here's my problem: I literally just saw "$10 off ends tomorrow" from DSW on June 20. That was five days ago. If tomorrow arrived on June 20 and the same deal is back "ending tomorrow" on June 25, I start thinking DSW is crying wolf. The urgency is running out of gas.
- Then the email opened. The big product splash — "BRAND NEW...AND SO YOU" — is a grid of women's pumps, heels, stilettos, and strappy sandals. Not one comfort walker or men's slip-on in sight at the top. I had to scroll past that whole section before I found the Deal Savers area with Crocs and New Balance. That's the part that almost got me.
- I'd circle back to the Deal Savers link later, maybe. I wouldn't forward this to a friend — there's nothing here that says "Dave, look at this."

## What's working

- The tiered BUY MORE SAVE MORE structure ($10/$20/$60 off) is actually smarter than the flat $10 I've seen recycled lately — rewards a bigger basket, which is exactly how I shop when I finally find the right shoe.
- Crocs and New Balance showing up in the Deal Savers section are names I cross-shop regularly — that section is doing the right work.
- The "SHOP WIDE WIDTH" button in the Shop by Size section is the most genuinely useful thing I've seen in a DSW email in weeks — wide feet are a real thing and DSW knows it.
- The HELLOSUMMER code is printed plainly in the hero — no clicking to reveal, no mystery. I respect that.
- Clean mobile render: no broken images, no garbled text, text is readable at arm's length without pinching.

## What's weak

- The "BRAND NEW...AND SO YOU" product grid is wall-to-wall women's fashion shoes — heels, pumps, stilettos, ankle boots with pointed toes. None of that is for me and it takes over the entire upper body of the email.
- The "ends tomorrow" clock has been rung too many times. June 20 was also "ends tomorrow" for $10 off. You can't cry urgent twice in five days on the same deal and expect me to believe either one.
- Not one word about comfort, cushioning, arch support, or walkability anywhere in the first two scrolls. That language is what connects DSW to why I shop there.
- The "Oh no" opener reads slightly younger and more social-media-native than my age bracket — not offensive, just a small mis-key.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Lead with men's comfort brands, not women's fashion.** Put the Crocs, New Balance, and Skechers GO Walk product grid first — or at minimum show a gender-split hero. A 62-year-old man seeing stilettos in the hero slot navigates away before scrolling.
- 2. **Add comfort language to the discount copy.** Something like: "Cushioned, supportive, and $10 off before tonight." That one phrase tells me this sale is for me.
- 3. **Retire or refresh the "ends tomorrow" hook.** If you're going to recycle deadline urgency, at least vary the framing. "Tonight only" or an actual clock graphic is more believable than the same expiring deadline that somehow resets every five days.
- 4. **Move "SHOP WIDE WIDTH" up.** That button is below three full sections but it's one of the most practical differentiators DSW has. Men my age care about this. Put it in the hero or right below the offer banner.
- **Subject Alt A:** `$10 off ends tonight — Crocs, New Balance, and more`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Cushioned comfort + $10 off: last chance today`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Wide widths, comfort brands — grab it before midnight`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `New Balance, Crocs, Brooks — shop now, save before it's gone`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Confirmed issues:**

**a. Invalid `Src:` property in all `@font-face` blocks (12 instances)**
The CSS property name is `src` (lowercase). All 12 `@font-face` declarations use `Src:` with a capital S:
```css
@font-face {
  font-family: 'Futura_PT_Regular';
  Src: url("https://braze-images.com/...") format('woff');  /* invalid */
}
```
Most email clients are lenient, but this is a CSS spec violation that can cause font-load failures in strict renderers (some Outlook WebView builds, Samsung Mail). All font families are affected.

**b. Missing `http-equiv` attributes on two meta tags**
```html
<meta content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />           <!-- missing http-equiv="Content-Type" -->
<meta content="IE=Edge" />                             <!-- missing http-equiv="X-UA-Compatible" -->
```
Without `http-equiv`, these tags have no effect. The charset declaration is especially important — without it, character encoding is inferred and could misparse special characters (the `$` in the subject line, accented characters in product names, etc.).

**c. `text-size-adjust` contradiction**
The global reset sets vendor-prefixed properties to `100%` then overrides the standard property to `none`:
```css
*{
  -ms-text-size-adjust: 100%;
  -webkit-text-size-adjust: 100%;
  text-size-adjust: none;   /* contradicts above; could suppress correct behavior as spec matures */
}
```

**d. Mixed and incomplete font format coverage**
- Futura PT family: `.woff` only — no `.woff2` (20–30% smaller), no TTF fallback
- HelveticaNow family: `.ttf` only — no `.woff` or `.woff2`
- All fonts hosted on `braze-images.com` (Braze CDN); a CDN availability issue silently falls back to whatever the system fallback stack is (not declared in the source visible here)
