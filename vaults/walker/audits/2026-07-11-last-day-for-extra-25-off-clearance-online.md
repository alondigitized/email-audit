# LAST DAY for EXTRA 25% off Clearance online ⏰
**Score:** 6/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-11**
## Executive summary

- I knew what this email was before I even tapped it open — "LAST DAY" from Famous Footwear, another clearance push. By my count this is the eighth email in seven days from these folks, and they are really leaning on that 25% off clearance angle again. That's the same play they ran on the 8th with the BOGO last-call, just dressed up differently. I've gone from mildly interested to starting to feel like I'm being worked over.
- Once I got inside, the hero is fine — clear red-bordered box, "FINAL DAY" badge, 25% off clearance is easy to read. I can see Birkenstock, Nike, a Brooks shoe at $109.99, and then — here's where it fell apart for me — two full sections for kids' sandals and women's sandals. I'm 62. I'm shopping for my own feet. I scrolled past stuff that had nothing to do with me before I found the one shoe I might actually care about.
- I would not click through. I'd mark it read and wait to see if the next email is actually aimed at a guy my age who needs arch support, not a mom buying Back-to-School sandals for the kids.

## What's working

- **Hero offer is unmissable.** The "Take an extra 25% off CLEARANCE — FINAL DAY" block with that red border stops the eye immediately. Clear, no guesswork.
- **Brooks at $109.99 is the right product for me.** That's a walking shoe I recognize and trust. Showing a specific price is what I need — not a vague "shop now."
- **Birkenstock section has some comfort credibility.** I know Birkenstocks for arch support; seeing that brand name at a sale price is relevant even if the styling skews sandal.
- **No broken images or layout crashes.** Everything loaded clean, text is readable without zooming, tap targets look reasonable on mobile.
- **"Final Day" urgency is believable this time.** After the July 8th last-call email I was skeptical, but pairing it with the 25% off number makes it feel like a real deadline rather than a trick.

## What's weak

- **The preheader is a raw tracking URL.** Before I even opened this, my inbox showed a `https://click.email.famousfootwear.com/?qs=ABB7…` string instead of any actual copy. That looks broken and sketchy — exactly the kind of thing that makes a 62-year-old wonder if an email is a phishing attempt.
- **Two full sections aimed at people who aren't me.** Kids' sandals at 50% off. Women's sandals at 60% off. I had to scroll past both before reaching Brooks. That's not how you make a 62-year-old guy feel like a known customer.
- **Eight emails in seven days is too many.** I said this on the 9th and the 10th. The volume is wearing down any goodwill the offer generates.
- **Nike section shows athletic performance shoes, not comfort walkers.** "30% off Nike" with running shoe imagery doesn't speak to me. If it's a walking or lifestyle shoe, show me that; right now it reads like a teen sneaker drop.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Fix the preheader immediately — it's leaking a tracking URL.** Before anything else, this is a trust problem. In the inbox it looks like spam or phishing. Write actual preview copy that reinforces the "FINAL DAY" message.
- 2. **Swap or move the kids' and women's sandal blocks** for men's comfort/walking content. If they have men's Birkenstocks or men's wide-width clearance, that's the section that belongs in my email. Filter the content, don't batch-send the full catalog.
- 3. **Give the Brooks section a comfort callout.** "$109.99" is good, but "cushioned, wide-width available" or "arch support built in" would close the deal for me. That's the one piece of product detail I want to see.
- 4. **Show the rewards multiplier explicitly** if there's a loyalty bonus tied to the clearance event. I saw a "3X" reference in the Brooks area but it wasn't clear enough to act on.
- 5. **Cool the send cadence.** After daily emails all week, even a genuinely good offer gets tuned out. Give me two days off and the next one hits harder.

- **Preheader Alt A:** `Men's comfort shoes marked down — today only.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Brooks, Birkenstock, and more — final hours on extra 25% off.`

### 1. Technical Summary

One CSS property name is misspelled in the HTML source.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Critical — 25 images missing `alt` text** (confirmed by QA). All product and hero images render as blank blocks when images are disabled, which is the default in many enterprise mail clients (Outlook, Gmail on mobile data-saver). Affected images include the logo wrapper image and every product/promotional image. Two examples:
```
src: .../3b6a7dc1-6955-49f8-868a-bf62a15122ee.png  (logo area)
src: .../4ccff4ad-f402-43de-a027-5eb147b6f238.jpg  (appears 4× in QA output — likely a divider reused)
```
The logo `<img>` has `alt=""` — acceptable for purely decorative images, but the logo is a functional link to the homepage and should carry `alt="Famous Footwear"`.

**CSS typo** in inline style on a content cell:
```html
style="...lineapacing:20px;..."
```
`lineapacing` is not a valid CSS property. Likely intended `line-height` or `letter-spacing`. The rule silently fails in all clients.

**Zoom blocked on mobile** — viewport meta sets `user-scalable=0`:
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1, user-scalable=0" />
```
This prevents pinch-to-zoom, which WCAG 1.4.4 flags as an accessibility violation and iOS 10+ partially ignores anyway.

**`<meta>` charset malformed** — the charset declaration is missing `http-equiv`:
```html
<meta content="text/html; charset=UTF-8" />
```
Should be:
```html
<meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=UTF-8" />
```
Without `http-equiv`, the charset hint is ignored by conformant parsers. Older Outlook versions may default to system encoding.

**DOCTYPE** is HTML 4.01 Transitional — legacy but intentional and widely used in ESP templates. Not a defect.
