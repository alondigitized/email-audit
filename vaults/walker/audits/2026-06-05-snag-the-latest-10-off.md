# Snag the latest & $10 off
**Score:** 9/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-05**
## Executive summary

- Seventh DSW email in three days, and I'll be straight with you — by the time this one hit my inbox the "$10 off" hook barely registered the way that "OOOO WEEE" tiered savings send did back on June 3rd. I opened it anyway. Force of habit, and because the subject was clean and short. Then I scrolled down and the first thing I saw was a big "SHOP WOMEN" button sitting right below the hero. I'm 62, I wear a men's 11 wide. The product grid under it was heels, mules, strappy sandals. The category section below that: HEELS, ATHLETIC & SNEAKERS, SANDALS, WOMEN'S SHOES. DSW has my purchase history. They know exactly who I am. And they still sent me this.
- I'll give credit where it's due — the email renders clean, the 30% off badges on the product images are visible, and I spotted a "SHOP WIDE WIDTH" button buried somewhere below the fold, which is the first copy in this entire send that talked to my feet. Wide fit matters more than any logo at my age. But one button below four rows of women's shoes is not a strategy.
- Mark as read. No click, no forward to a friend.

## What's working

- **Recognized brand, clean execution** — DSW logo renders sharp, no broken images, no overlapping text; builds baseline trust immediately.
- **30% off discount badges on the product images** — concrete number right on the product, no hunting required; I like seeing a real discount, not "great value."
- **"SHOP WIDE WIDTH" button** — only thing in this email aimed at someone like me; wide-fit comfort is exactly what I'm shopping for.
- **VIP rewards block at the bottom** — confirms I should be getting something back for being a regular; reinforces loyalty even at the end of the scroll.
- **Subject line is short and direct** — 25 characters, no exclamation stacking, promise of a real dollar amount.

## What's weak

- **"SHOP WOMEN" as the hero CTA** — I'm a man; that button tells me in two words that DSW doesn't know who they're talking to.
- **Every product visible is women's** — heels, strappy mules, ankle-strap sandals; none of those are what I opened for.
- **No comfort language anywhere in the body** — no cushioning, no arch support, no slip-on, no easy-on language; the things that make me actually buy a shoe are completely absent.
- **$10 off promised in the subject, not surfaced in the body** — the subject made a specific promise; the body serves 30% off product badges and doesn't honor that promise with a visible banner or code.
- **Cadence fatigue** — this is send number seven or more this week; the urgency hook has worn thin and the email feels like noise.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Segment male subscribers out of the women's hero** — flip the primary CTA to "SHOP MEN" for men; this is a database filter, not a design project, and it's the single change that would rescue this email's relevance for half the list.
- 2. **Move "SHOP WIDE WIDTH" above the fold** — it's the most Walker-relevant thing in this send; it belongs in the hero row, not buried below rows of women's product.
- 3. **Lead the hero copy with comfort language** — "Cushioned. Easy on. Up to 30% off." beats "Hey, what's new?" for someone shopping by fit, not fashion.
- 4. **Surface the $10 off in the body** — the subject promised it; put a banner or promo code block right under the hero so the promise lands.
- 5. **Back off the send cadence** — a rest day or two makes the next email feel like news; right now DSW's emails feel like a faucet that won't shut off.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**[HIGH] `Src:` (capital S) in all 10 `@font-face` declarations**
CSS spec requires lowercase `src:`. All Futura PT and HelveticaNow faces are affected:
```css
/* Observed (invalid) */
Src: url("https://braze-images.com/appboy/communication/assets/font_assets/files/67dd7a3bf1de1d0066403c87/original.woff") format('woff');
```
Most modern clients silently accept this, but it is a spec violation that can cause unpredictable failures in stricter CSS parsers (older Outlook WebView, some Samsung Mail builds).

**[MEDIUM] `text-size-adjust: none` overrides prefixed `100%` declarations**
In the universal selector reset:
```css
* {
  -ms-text-size-adjust: 100%;
  -webkit-text-size-adjust: 100%;
  text-size-adjust: none;   /* ← unprefixed shorthand clobbers the 100% above */
}
```
The unprefixed `none` takes precedence in clients that support the unprefixed property, disabling automatic text-size adjustment entirely rather than capping it at 100%. This can produce illegibly small text on narrow viewports.

**[MEDIUM] `maximum-scale=1` in viewport meta**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Blocks user pinch-to-zoom. Fails WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text, Level AA). iOS 10+ ignores this in Safari, but it remains a compliance flag.

**[LOW] No `.woff2` format for any web font**
Futura PT faces are `.woff`-only; HelveticaNow faces are `.ttf`-only. No `.woff2` (30–40% smaller) is provided for any variant. Increases load time, particularly on mobile.

**[LOW] Redundant `color-scheme` declaration**
Declared in both a `<meta>` tag and `:root` CSS:
```html
<meta name="color-scheme" content="light dark" />
```
```css
:root { color-scheme: light dark; }
```
The meta tag is the correct canonical location for email clients; the `:root` rule is harmless but redundant noise.

**[INFO] `#000001` used as "black" in dark mode overrides**
Classes like `.dm-font-black` and `.dm-bg-black` use `#000001` / `#00000F` instead of `#000000`. This appears intentional (some clients treat pure `#000000` as transparent in dark mode contexts) — flagging for awareness, not as a defect.
