# Gifts for every dad
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-05-27**
## Executive summary

- Well, I'll give HOKA this — after two weeks of "Final Hours" and "Sale Not Over Yet" landing in my inbox every other day, switching to a Father's Day gift guide at least feels like a different creative gear. The "For Every Father" hero with the guy jumping and celebrating is energetic, and I noticed it immediately. It doesn't look broken, the layout is clean, and the button is easy to find. So on the surface, fine.
- But here's where it falls apart for me: I'm 62, and when I open an email that says "gifts for every dad," I'm not thinking, "oh good, someone can buy me shoes." I'm thinking — do I need to buy something for another guy? And that is not how I shop. I shop for myself, for my feet, for comfort. This email is written for a gift-giver, not for the guy who is actually going to wear the shoe. None of those five product categories I can make out — "For the Off-Road Legend," "For the Style Icon," whatever the others say — mentions cushioning, arch support, or ease of slip-on. The things I actually care about. The product copy under each shoe is so small on my phone I'd have to pinch the screen, and I'm not doing that.
- After eight or nine HOKA emails in the last two weeks, I'd scroll this one and put it down. I would not click. I would not forward it. I'm honestly starting to wonder whether I want to stay on this list.

## What's working

- The creative pivot away from another countdown sale is genuinely noticeable — "For Every Father" breaks the pattern after all that "final hours" noise
- Male-targeted hero imagery fits my demographic — active man, arms up, energy that says HOKA without being clinical
- Visual hierarchy is clean: big hero first, clear CTA button, product cards in a logical scroll — my eye knew where to go
- "Shop Father's Day" button is large enough to tap on a phone without hitting the wrong thing
- Father's Day timing is seasonally sharp — nothing stale, this is timely

## What's weak

- The framing treats me as a gift-buyer, not the gift-recipient — I am the dad, and the email never invites me to treat myself
- Not one of the visible product sections references cushioning, arch support, or comfort fit — the whole reason I'm on this list
- No offer anywhere — no percentage off, no free shipping, nothing — after a two-week sale marathon, switching to full-price gift guide with no incentive is a cold shower
- The product description copy under each shoe is too small to read without zooming on my phone; at 62 that's a dealbreaker
- Nine HOKA emails in two weeks means I'm opening this with my hand already halfway to "mark as read"

## Recommendations

- 1. **Reframe from gift-giver to self-treat** — "Dad's been on his feet all year. Time to let them rest." Plenty of dads buy their own Father's Day shoes; don't leave that money on the table.
- 2. **Add one comfort callout per product card** — "Plush midsole," "Slip-on ease," "Extra-wide available" — something scannable that tells me in two words whether any of these are for someone with my feet.
- 3. **Pair the guide with an incentive** — "Free shipping on all Father's Day orders" or a flat 15% off doesn't require a sale event and gives me a concrete reason to click rather than just browse.
- 4. **Increase the product sub-copy font size** — unreadable body text on mobile is a real barrier for the 60+ segment HOKA wants to keep.
- 5. **Space the cadence** — dropping a gift guide the morning after a nine-email sale run means it lands in a pile. Two or three days of quiet before this send would have made it land harder.
- **Subject Alt A:** `New shoes for the dad who picks his own`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Father's Day starts with the right pair`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Comfort-first picks — because dad knows what his feet need`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `From trail-ready to slip-on easy — find his fit`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Confirmed issues:**

**3a. Duplicate, conflicting `<meta name="viewport">` tags**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
<meta content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1, user-scalable=yes" name="viewport" />
```
The first locks scale (`maximum-scale=1`); the second re-enables it (`user-scalable=yes`). Only the last declaration wins in most parsers — keep one canonical tag.

**3b. Malformed `charset` meta — missing `http-equiv`**
```html
<meta content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />
```
Outlook's Word rendering engine requires `http-equiv="Content-Type"` to parse charset correctly. Should be:
```html
<meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />
```

**3c. Malformed CSS — missing values in `.nav_padding` and `.nav_border`**
```css
.nav_padding { padding-top:px !important; padding-bottom:px !important; }
.nav_border  { border-top:px solid ; }
```
Both rules have empty numeric values before `px` and `.nav_border` has no color. These declarations are invalid and will be silently ignored by all clients — the mobile nav spacing and border will not render as intended.

**3d. Empty `<title>` tag**
```html
<title></title>
```
Screen readers surface the `<title>` value when announcing the document. An empty title is an accessibility gap; it also omits a secondary display surface in some webmail clients.

**3e. `@font-face` web fonts unsupported in major clients**
Five `@font-face` declarations load `TheFutureHOKA` variants from `hoka.com/on/demandware.static/…`. Gmail (all surfaces), Outlook (Windows), and Samsung Mail strip or ignore `@font-face` — no fallback `font-family` stack is visible in the declared rules. If usage elsewhere in the template doesn't specify a system-font fallback, affected clients will apply the UA default (typically Times New Roman or Arial).

**3f. `font-display: swap` in `@font-face`**
```css
font-display: swap;
```
This CSS property is not honored by any email client rendering engine. Harmless, but adds unnecessary bytes across five declarations.
