# Sandals starting at $24.98 + 10% off ALL the BEST deals
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-05-17**
## Executive summary

- Shoe Carnival has been hitting my inbox every single day this week, and I'll admit the subject line caught me — $24.98 for sandals is a real number, and I've been half-shopping for a replacement pair since last summer. When I opened it, the layout is tidy enough: "Inflation Relief" banner up top (which, by the way, I just saw in their May 15th email — recycled framing already), a 10% off coupon box, then a Footbed Sandal section at $24.98. That "footbed" word matters to me. It's not arch support or cushioning by name, but I know what a footbed means for a walking shoe.
- Here's where it falls apart for me: that coupon is in-store only. I'm browsing on my phone at 9pm. I'm not driving to a store. So the headline offer immediately becomes irrelevant to how I actually shop. Then I scroll and there's a whole "Long Weekend Ready" section with athletic sneakers, followed by a Rocket Dog flip-flop section that looks like it's aimed at someone's 30-year-old son, not me. The email can't decide who it's talking to.
- I'd click "Shop Sandals" because the $24.98 price is real and the timing is right. But I'm not forwarding this to anyone — it feels like a broadcast to the whole mailing list, not anything tuned to a guy my age who walks three miles a day and needs foot support.

## What's working

- The **$24.98 "Footbed Sandal" headline** hits right — "footbed" signals that someone thought about comfort, and the price is specific and competitive.
- **Crocs at $34.98** is a genuinely useful data point; I cross-shop Crocs for recovery/around-the-house wear and that's a price worth clicking on.
- **"Long Weekend Ready" framing** is seasonally smart — Memorial Day is days away and it earns the email a reason to exist.
- **Shoe Perks $10 reward with barcode** at the bottom is clear and scannable; if I'm walking into a store I know exactly what to pull up.
- **Clean layout, no broken images**, no overlapping text — at my screen size on mobile the text was readable without pinching.

## What's weak

- **"In-store only" on the 10% coupon** — that's the top offer in the subject line, and it's not for me after dinner on my couch. That's a bait-and-switch feeling even if the fine print is technically there.
- **"Inflation Relief" hero is recycled** — I saw the exact same yellow banner and identical framing in their May 15th "Inflation Relief Is Here!" email two days ago. Creative fatigue is real after seven consecutive days of Shoe Carnival.
- **Rocket Dog "Kick Back" section** — flip-flop brand aimed at beachgoers in their 30s. Nothing about that section spoke to me.
- **No comfort specifics anywhere** — cushioning, arch support, slip-on convenience, wide width availability. I'm buying on fit, not just price. The email gives me price and nothing else to evaluate.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Kill the in-store-only restriction or make a separate online offer.** A phone-first shopper gets burned by the top offer before he even scrolls. Lead with the online-valid offer instead.
- 2. **Pull the Rocket Dog and generic athletic sneaker sections** for a comfort/walking-focused subscriber segment. Replace with Skechers or Hoka sandal callouts that speak to arch support, extra cushioning, or slip-on convenience — words that close a sale for me.
- 3. **Fix the preheader.** A raw tracking URL leaked into the inbox preview slot. That's the single highest-leverage technical fix — a real preheader that mentions $24.98 sandals would lift opens meaningfully.
- 4. **Retire the Inflation Relief creative** — it's been two days and feels stale. "Long Weekend" is the right seasonal hook; lead with that.
- 5. **Add one fit/comfort detail** to the footbed sandal section — even a single line like "wide widths available" or "memory foam footbed" would move me from browse to click.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Confirmed issues:**

**`<meta http-equiv>` attribute missing** — Line reads:
```html
<meta content="IE=edge" />
```
The `http-equiv="X-UA-Compatible"` attribute is absent. Without it, the directive is ignored by Outlook rendering engine. Fix:
```html
<meta http-equiv="X-UA-Compatible" content="IE=edge" />
```

**`<style>` block before `<meta charset>`** — The first `<style>` block appears in `<head>` before `<meta charset="utf-8">`. Per HTML spec, charset declaration must precede any content that could require encoding interpretation. Move `<meta charset>` to the first position in `<head>`.

**No dark mode support** — Both color-scheme declarations are light-only:
```html
<meta name="color-scheme" content="light" />
<meta name="supported-color-schemes" content="light" />
```
This forces light rendering in Apple Mail dark mode but provides no fallback styles. If dark mode is intentionally unsupported, this is acceptable, but should be a documented decision.

**Empty breakpoint block** — `@media only screen and (max-width: 320px)` contains no rules. Dead code; remove or populate.

**`pointer-events: none` on `tel:`/`sms:` links** — The global anchor reset includes:
```css
a[href^="tel"], a[href^="sms"] { pointer-events: none; cursor: default !important; }
```
This disables tap-to-call on mobile. Intentional or not, it's a UX regression on iOS/Android. If no phone numbers appear in this send, it's harmless dead CSS; if they do, remove this rule.

**Duplicate `color: inherit` in anchor reset:**
```css
color:inherit !important; color:inherit;
```
The second declaration is redundant and may confuse specificity in edge clients. Remove the non-`!important` duplicate.

**Inconsistent responsive breakpoints** — Three separate breakpoints defined: `600px`, `660px`, `450px`. The 600px block only targets `.responsive-text` while the 660px block handles layout. No structural issue, but the cascade is fragile — a viewport between 601–659px gets `responsive-text` font rules but not layout rules.
