# Clifton 10: Now on Markdown
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-05-21**
## Executive summary

- Now we're talking a little more like it. I open this one and the first thing I see is a clean shot of the Clifton 10 — a shoe I actually know by name — with a big "Now on Sale" right across the top. For a guy like me who's been half-watching the Clifton line because my feet need real cushioning, that headline does what it needs to do. No mountain runners, no neon-colored performance gear — just a neutral, comfortable-looking walking shoe at a reduced price. That's the pitch.
- But here's my gripe, and it's not a small one: they never show me the actual price or the discount. "Markdowns" is nice talk. Tell me it's $40 off or 25% down, and I click in ten seconds. Instead they want me to go fish for the number on their website. That friction costs them. Just yesterday I got another HOKA email about "deeper markdowns" with the same vague language. I'm starting to wonder if this is a brand that never puts a real number in an email.
- Honest verdict: I'd click — barely — because the Clifton 10 has enough word-of-mouth for me to want to see what the markdown actually is. I wouldn't forward this to anyone because there's nothing here a friend couldn't find himself by typing "Hoka sale" into Google.

## What's working

- "Now on Sale" paired with the specific model name (Clifton 10) is the clearest, most walker-friendly headline HOKA has sent me in weeks — no jargon, no performance nonsense.
- "Famously plush and smooth" in the body paragraph speaks directly to what I'm shopping for: cushion and comfort on a daily walk. First time in a while they said the comfort word without burying it.
- The hero shoe is a neutral colorway — no loud athletic colors — which feels like it's dressed for someone my age, not a 30-year-old sprinter.
- The 2×2 grid of men's and women's colorways gives me options and confirms there are real men's styles in the sale, not just leftovers.
- Clean layout, nothing broken, text is readable without pinching the screen.

## What's weak

- No price or discount percentage anywhere in the email. "Markdowns" without a number is the email equivalent of a car dealership saying "reduced!" with no sticker.
- The CTA says "Shop Summer Sale Styles" — that's a category, not the Clifton 10 I just got excited about. I was primed to tap "Shop Clifton 10" and they sent me somewhere broader.
- This is the second HOKA email in two days (yesterday was "Deeper markdowns") and both use the same vague-pricing approach. The frequency is getting close to annoying, and the message isn't different enough to feel like a new reason to look.
- No size or availability signal — for a guy who's learned the hard way that sale shoes run out in wide widths fast, I'd like some urgency beyond "the sale is moving fast."

## Recommendations

- 1. **Show a real number in the hero.** "Now $109 (was $145)" or "Save $36 today" turns this from a browse into a buy. One line. Huge lift.
- 2. **Change the CTA to "Shop Clifton 10 Sale" or "See My Size"** — keep me focused on the product you just sold me on, not the whole summer catalog.
- 3. **Add one line of size/availability urgency** — something like "Popular widths going fast" lands differently than "the sale is moving fast," because it's personal.
- 4. **Pull back the email cadence.** If yesterday was "deeper markdowns" and today is "Clifton 10: Now on Markdown," I start tuning out. Space these out so each send still feels like news.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Clifton 10 is $40 off — today only`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Your Clifton 10 just got cheaper`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `The plush cushioning you've been eyeing — now marked down in your size`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Summer Kickoff Sale: Clifton 10 is finally on markdown`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Duplicate conflicting viewport meta tags.**
Two `<meta name="viewport">` tags are present with contradictory values:

```html
<!-- Tag 1 -->
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
<!-- Tag 2 -->
<meta content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1, user-scalable=yes" name="viewport" />
```

`maximum-scale=1` without `user-scalable=yes` suppresses pinch-zoom; the second tag attempts to re-enable it. Browser behavior on duplicates is undefined — whichever tag wins, the intent is unclear. WCAG 1.4.4 requires zoom not be blocked; `maximum-scale=1` alone fails this. **Remove the first tag; keep only the second.**

**Malformed CSS — two properties with missing numeric values.**
In the `@media only screen and (max-width:480px)` block:

```css
.nav_padding { padding-top:px !important; padding-bottom:px !important; }
.nav_border  { border-top:px solid ; }
```

Both rules have unit values without numbers (`px` instead of e.g. `10px`; `solid` without a color). These will be discarded by CSS parsers, silently breaking nav spacing and border on mobile.

**Empty `<title>` tag.**

```html
<title></title>
```

Some email clients (notably older Samsung Mail) use `<title>` as the preview subject. An empty title may cause a blank or garbled subject preview in those clients.

**`<meta charset>` missing `http-equiv`.**

```html
<meta content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />
```

The `http-equiv="Content-Type"` attribute is absent. While most modern clients infer encoding from the MIME part, the canonical form for HTML email is:

```html
<meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />
```

**Near-black/near-white color values on links.**

```css
a { color: #000001; }
.footer_text a { color: #fffffe; }
```

Values are one digit off from pure black/white. If intentional (spam-filter evasion), note this technique is ineffective against modern filters and adds confusion. If unintentional, these should be `#000000` / `#ffffff`.

**Web fonts unsupported in majority of clients.**
Five `@font-face` declarations load `TheFutureHOKA` from `hoka.com/on/demandware.static/…`. These render only in Apple Mail, iOS Mail, and a handful of modern clients. Gmail, Outlook (Windows), and Yahoo ignore them and fall back to the CSS `font-family` stack. No fallback stack is visible in the `<head>`; confirm body-level `td` or `p` elements specify safe system fonts (Arial, Helvetica, sans-serif) as fallback.
