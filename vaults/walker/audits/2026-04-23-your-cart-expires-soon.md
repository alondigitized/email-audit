# Your cart expires soon!
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-04-23**
## Executive summary

- Well, this one actually got my attention right away. I had that GO WALK Slip-in sitting in my cart — I looked at it, liked it, then got distracted by something on TV and forgot about it. Skechers caught me. The headline "You Liked These and You Weren't Wrong" made me chuckle a little. That's not nothing. The 20% off banner is front and center, which is exactly what I need to stop hemming and hawing. But then I keep scrolling and I'm looking at a bunch of pink and tan women's shoes in that grid section, and I'm thinking — who is this for? They had me, then they lost me a little. The "Complete Your Order" button is big and obvious, which I appreciate. At my age, I don't want to hunt around for what I'm supposed to do next.
- Cart abandonment email with a real discount is a smart play. Loses points because the product grid beneath the hero is clearly not curated for me — a lot of women's colorways — and it dilutes what could've been a clean, focused nudge to close the sale.

## What's working

- **The hero shoe is the right shoe.** The GO WALK Glide-Step 2.0 in that orange colorway is a Slip-in, it's a men's walking shoe, and it's exactly what I had in my cart. That's real personalization.
- **"You Liked These and You Weren't Wrong."** That headline is casual, confident, not pushy. Feels like a friend saying "go back and buy the thing." My generation responds to that better than hard-sell pressure.
- **"Complete Your Order" button is big and obvious.** I can tap it without squinting. That matters on a phone screen.
- **20% off a single pair** is prominently displayed in a high-contrast dark banner. I noticed it immediately. That could be the difference between me buying and walking away again.
- **Clean visual layout.** Not cluttered at the top. Easy to read without zooming.

## What's weak

- **That product grid below the hero is mostly women's shoes.** Pink sneakers, pastel colorways — not for me. If Skechers knows I'm a 62-year-old guy who was looking at men's walking shoes, why am I seeing that? It breaks the spell right when the email was doing its job.
- **"Shop All Best Sellers" as a CTA doesn't match the urgency of the email.** This email told me my cart expires soon, and then sends me to browse everything? That's contradictory.
- **The category navigation at the bottom** (Women, Men, Kids, Clothing, Sale) is just text links. That's basically a website menu. It adds nothing at this point in the funnel — I'm here to finish buying one thing, not to start a new browsing session.
- **The texting sign-up, the app download, and the "Shop Now Pay Later" all pile up at the bottom.** Too much going on. That's three separate asks after I've already been asked to complete my order.
- **No urgency specifics on the cart expiry.** The subject says "expires soon" — when? Today? Tomorrow? Three days? I'd respond better if it said "expires in 24 hours" right in the email body.

## Recommendations

- 1. Replace the women's shoe grid with men's Slip-ins or GO WALK styles — something I'd actually browse if I wanted to swap out what's in my cart.
- 2. Add a specific deadline next to the "Complete Your Order" button. "Your cart expires in 24 hours" is a lot more motivating than vague.
- 3. Cut the bottom clutter by half. Keep the 20% off, keep the store finder, lose the texting signup and app pitch in this particular email.
- 4. Make the 20% off discount code or terms clearer — is it already applied to my cart? Do I need a code? I want to know before I click.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**[FAIL] `<title>` is empty**
```html

```
Some clients (Apple Mail, Outlook on macOS) surface the title as a tooltip or in accessibility trees. An empty title is a minor but unnecessary gap.

**[WARN] `#MessageViewBody a { color: inherit; text-decoration: none }`**
This rule suppresses all visual link cues inside Gmail's message view body. Combined with custom link colors defined per-element, any link that inherits its parent's text color becomes visually indistinguishable from body copy.

The preheader filler uses both U+034F (Combining Grapheme Joiner, `͏`) and soft-hyphen (`­`). This is an intentional technique to prevent inbox clients from pulling body copy into the preview snippet, and it works — no functional issue, but worth noting if character encoding is ever changed.
