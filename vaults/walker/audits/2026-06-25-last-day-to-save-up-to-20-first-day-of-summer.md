# Last day to save up to $20 + First day of summer ☀️
**Score:** 9/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-21**
## Executive summary

- I've been opening Famous Footwear emails for years now, and that preview text greeting me in my inbox — the same raw tracking URL garbage I've been staring at since June 8 — is starting to feel like Famous Footwear just doesn't care. That's now six times, by my count, that I've tapped open a Famous Footwear email hoping the preview text issue had been fixed. It hasn't. I flagged it on the June 15th send, then the 16th, then the 16th again, then the June 17th Spend & Save, then the June 21st Samba send. And here we are again on June 25th with the same wall of URL noise followed by a lonely word "Want." I almost didn't open this one on principle.
- Once I got inside, the bones are solid enough. Tiered discounts — $10 off $50, $15 off $75, $20 off $100 — that's the kind of math I actually run in my head before I buy. The "Last day" urgency is real. The "First day of summer" hook is timely. The email is clean, nothing's broken, and there's a "Bonus Cash with Story Pickup" incentive that rewards doing it in-store rather than shipping to my door. For a man who doesn't love waiting on a UPS box, that's a small but genuine win.
- What's missing for me is any reason to believe Famous Footwear understands what I'm shopping for. I'm looking for walking shoes with cushion and arch support — something I can slip on in the morning without fighting laces. I don't see that anywhere in this email. What I see is sandals, summery slides, and lifestyle photography that looks aimed at a 30-year-old woman. I'll probably click through because the Spend & Save offer is real money, but I'm digging through a generic sale rather than being shown the three shoes that would actually fit my foot. Mark as read, then maybe click. Would not forward to a friend.

## What's working

- **Tiered savings are crystal clear.** $10/$15/$20 off at specific spend thresholds — I don't have to squint, do math, or read fine print to understand what I'm getting.
- **"Last day" urgency is credible.** It matches the subject, it matches the season, and it gives me a reason to act today rather than tomorrow.
- **"First day of summer" timing is on point.** This email landed on the actual first day of summer. That kind of relevance earns a little goodwill.
- **Bonus Cash with Story Pickup is a genuine differentiator.** An in-store incentive works on someone like me who wants to try the shoe before I buy it anyway.
- **No visual clutter or broken layout.** The email renders cleanly; hierarchy flows top to bottom without anything fighting for attention.

## What's weak

- **That preview text. Again. STILL.** A raw tracking URL followed by "Want" — six sends in a row now. At this point it isn't a bug, it's a policy, and the policy is to disrespect the inbox.
- **Nothing in here is aimed at a 62-year-old man shopping for walking comfort.** The models, the vibe, the featured products all skew younger and more female. I'm browsing past merchandise that isn't for me.
- **"Shop Now" is the laziest CTA on earth.** When every section says "Shop Now" I stop reading the buttons entirely. Tell me what I'm shopping — "Shop Men's Walking" would've made me tap.
- **No comfort, fit, or support language anywhere.** Cushioning, arch support, slip-on, wide width — the words that actually move my feet don't appear once.
- **One email carrying two messages is one too many.** "Last day to save" and "First day of summer" are both reasonable hooks. Stapling them together dilutes both.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Fix the preheader. Today. Not next send.** Six in a row is not a coincidence — this is a template-level bug. A blank preheader is better than a tracking URL. A broken inbox experience is costing opens.
- 2. **Split the two messages across two sends or give summer its own standalone moment.** "Last day to save" is urgent commerce. "First day of summer" is brand storytelling. They're fighting each other in the subject line and in the email body.
- 3. **Add one men's walking/comfort section or surface one comfort SKU as a hero product.** A "Built for walking all summer" callout with a Skechers Go Walk or a New Balance 928 with actual fit copy would have made me stop scrolling.
- 4. **Make the CTA copy do work.** "Shop Men's Comfort," "See Walking Shoes on Sale," "Find Your Fit" — anything with a noun attached to the verb.
- 5. **Pull back on email frequency or segment harder.** Seven Famous Footwear emails in ten days means I'm going numb to the brand. If they segmented by shoe type — comfort/walking vs. fashion/trend — I'd open every single walking-focused one. Right now it's a firehose.

## Technical Audit — Famous Footwear "Last day to save up to $20" (2026-06-25)

### 1. Technical Summary

The email is a standard ESP-rendered table layout with no critical send-blockers, but it carries a mixed-content HTTP asset, pervasive missing alt text across ~18 product images, a malformed `<meta>` tag, and an invalid CSS property. The QA tool also misreports its own output (see §7).

---

### 2. Link & Tracking Issues

**No broken links detected.** All 28 CTAs route through `click.email.famousfootwear.com` (Salesforce Marketing Cloud click-redirect). The QA prober correctly skipped these — redirect chains cannot be probed without live session cookies.

**Open pixel** at `click.email.famousfootwear.com/open.aspx?5DJNDQJ2N26E7I5H457KFN7MQI.70259` is standard SFMC open tracking. No issue.

**UTM parameters are not visible in source.** All destination URLs are obscured behind the click-redirect. UTM injection is presumably handled server-side by SFMC at redirect time; this cannot be confirmed from HTML alone. See §6.

---

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**[HIGH] Mixed-content HTTP asset**
One image is served over HTTP:
```
src="http://cl.S7.exct.net/LiveContent.aspx?qs=..."
```
This is a LiveContent (dynamic/personalized) image from Salesforce's `S7.exct.net`. Gmail, Outlook 365, and Apple Mail enforce HTTPS; this asset will be blocked or proxied with a broken-image fallback on those clients. The HTTPS equivalent (`https://cl.S7.exct.net/...`) should be used.

**[HIGH] 18 images missing `alt` text**
Every product/hero/divider image has `alt=""`. In image-blocked environments (Outlook desktop default, corporate proxies), the email renders as a blank column of empty boxes with no content fallback. Affected images include all primary product shots (e.g., `ac216ca0`, `73bc3661`, `1d62dde4`, and 15 others per QA log).

Exception: the open-tracking pixel `alt=""` is correct — intentionally invisible.

**[MEDIUM] Malformed `<meta charset>` tag**
```html
<meta content="text/html; charset=UTF-8" />
```
The `http-equiv` attribute is missing. The correct form is:
```html
<meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=UTF-8" />
```
Without it, charset declaration is inert. Most clients will still default to UTF-8, but the ☀️ emoji in the subject line and any non-ASCII characters in body copy are at risk of mojibake in edge cases (older Outlook, some webmail).

**[MEDIUM] `user-scalable=0` in viewport**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1, user-scalable=0" />
```
Disables pinch-to-zoom on mobile. This is an accessibility violation (WCAG 1.4.4) and is increasingly overridden by iOS/Android anyway, so it provides no real layout protection.

**[LOW] Invalid CSS property**
```css
lineapacing:20px
```
on `td.darkmode.padl10m`. Likely a typo for `line-height` or `letter-spacing`. Ignored by all parsers; the intended spacing is silently lost.

**[LOW] HTML 4.01 Transitional DOCTYPE**
Intentional for email client compatibility. No action needed.
