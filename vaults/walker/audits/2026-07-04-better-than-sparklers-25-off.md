# Better than sparklers: 25% OFF
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-04**
## Executive summary

- Here we go again — DSW number nine, more or less, this week. I recognized the sender before I even read the subject line. "Better than sparklers" got a half-smile from me, I'll give it that. It's at least trying to be clever for the Fourth, instead of just shouting "SALE" in capital letters like a few of the earlier ones did.
- But the moment I opened it, I was staring at a young woman in a white dress holding a pair of sandals. "Natural beauties." "Whoaaa, cute & comfy." That's not me they're talking to. This email was built for my daughter, maybe my granddaughter, not a 62-year-old guy who just wants a cushioned slip-on that won't kill his heels by noon. The 25% off is real and the $39.99 price point is printed big enough to read without my reading glasses — I'll give them that — but the entire scroll is sandals aimed at women. By the time I hit the "Shop by Size" grid near the bottom, I'd already mentally checked out.
- I'd mark it read and maybe circle back to the DSW app on my own. I would not forward this to anyone. Wouldn't click either, unless I happened to notice the "MEN" link up in the nav bar and remembered I need something.

## What's working

- The "STARTING AT $39.99" text is big, bold, and impossible to miss — I didn't have to squint or zoom to catch the price anchor.
- "25% OFF" is stated plainly at the top; no mental math required, which I appreciate.
- The "SHOP BY SIZE" grid is a genuinely useful touch — if I'm browsing on my phone, jumping straight to my size is the right idea.
- "SALE!" badge mid-email gives a second visual cue that this isn't just browsing content, there's a real deal happening.
- No broken images, no garbled text — the layout held together cleanly on mobile.

## What's weak

- The hero image is a young woman in a summery dress — I am not that person, and nothing in the first visible screen tells me there's anything here for a man.
- "Whoaaa, cute & comfy sandals" in the preview — that's not how I shop or talk, and it signals immediately this email wasn't written with me in mind.
- "Natural beauties" and "Keep it simple" copy are fine phrases, but they're styled for a female-skewing audience; nothing about arch support, cushioning, or walking comfort, which is what would actually get me to click.
- This is the third or fourth email this week leaning on the same 25% off. The offer hasn't changed; the creative barely has. The fatigue is real.
- No social proof anywhere I could see — a star rating or a "Best Seller" badge on even one product would've given me a reason to look closer.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Segment the men's list.** If DSW knows I've been browsing men's walking shoes, send me a men's email. Route female subscribers to a different version. This is table stakes at this point.
- 2. **Lead with a comfort hook in the hero.** Even one line — "All-day comfort, 25% off" — would've told me this email had something for me before I scrolled past the woman in a sundress.
- 3. **Dial back the send cadence.** Nine emails in a week deadens every offer. Consolidate or at least vary the hook enough that I don't feel like I'm seeing the same poster reprinted.
- 4. **Swap "cute & comfy" preview language for something gender-neutral.** "Sandals from $39.99, 25% off this weekend" works for everyone and doesn't push men away in the inbox.
- 5. **Add one product callout with a fit or comfort detail** — "memory foam footbed," "wide width available," something tactile that makes the offer feel concrete for a comfort shopper.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Malformed `<meta>` tags (missing `http-equiv`):**
```html
<!-- found -->
<meta content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />
<meta content="IE=Edge" />

<!-- correct -->
<meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />
<meta http-equiv="X-UA-Compatible" content="IE=Edge" />
```
Without `http-equiv`, these tags are inert. Outlook/IE rendering hints won't apply.

**Capitalized `Src:` in all `@font-face` blocks:**
```css
Src: url("https://braze-images.com/.../original.woff") format('woff');
```
CSS property names are case-insensitive per spec, but several email preprocessors and Outlook's rendering engine are not fully spec-compliant. Lowercase `src:` is the safe form.

**Conflicting `-ms-text-size-adjust` values in `*` reset:**
```css
* {
  -ms-text-size-adjust: 100%;   /* set */
  -ms-text-size-adjust: none;   /* immediately overrides above */
}
```
The `none` value disables text-size adjustment globally in Windows Mail / legacy Outlook WebView, which can suppress user-controlled font scaling and break accessibility on small viewports.

**`supported-color-schemes` as a CSS property is a no-op:**
```css
:root {
  color-scheme: light dark;
  supported-color-schemes: light dark;  /* not a valid CSS property */
}
```
`supported-color-schemes` is only valid as a `<meta>` tag (already correctly present in `<head>`). The CSS declaration does nothing and should be removed to avoid confusion.

**Font format coverage — no `woff2`:**
- Futura PT: `.woff` only
- HelveticaNow: `.ttf` only

`woff2` is ~30% smaller and supported by every email client that supports web fonts at all. Missing it inflates payload for no gain.

**`alt` text coverage:** Not auditable — `<img>` tags not present in the truncated source.
