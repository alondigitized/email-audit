# Full speed, full-color
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-01**
## Executive summary

- "Full speed, full-color" — that subject line told me everything I needed to know before I even opened it. This isn't about comfort or fit, it's about making a color statement on the trail. I open it and sure enough: bright yellow shoes, a young woman sprinting in neon gear, and three reasons to care about "vibrant expressive palettes." I'm 62 years old. The only statement I'm trying to make is that my feet don't hurt by 2 p.m.
- This is back-to-back with the May 30th Berry Jam email that also left me cold — HOKA keeps swinging at the fashion-first runner, and I keep not being that guy. The May 28th "Comfort you can count on" email with the APMA seal? That one stopped me. This one doesn't. No pricing, no cushioning callouts, no arch support, no fit details. Just a colorway launch dressed up with a Strava Challenge tie-in I don't use.
- I'd mark this as read without clicking. Wouldn't forward it — none of my walking buddies are chasing neon yellow shoes either.

## What's working

- **CTA is clean and unambiguous** — "Shop Motion Party" and "Shop Motion Party Pack" are clear verb-noun buttons. No guessing where they go.
- **Visual hierarchy lands fast** — hero image, headline, and button are stacked logically. On a phone screen I can parse this in about three seconds.
- **No render bugs** — nothing broken, no overlapping text, images all loaded cleanly.
- **"A Versatile Range — To run fast on the trail or road"** is at least a functional claim, even if it doesn't say anything about how the shoe actually feels underfoot.
- **Honest campaign** — no fake countdown, no buried exclusions, no bait-and-switch. It's straightforwardly a new colorway launch.

## What's weak

- **Zero comfort language** — not one mention of cushioning, arch support, or how this shoe actually feels. After the podiatrist-approved May 28th send, this feels like a different brand entirely.
- **Wrong demographic in every photo** — young women running hard. I don't see myself anywhere in this email, not even a walking-trail shot with a broader age range.
- **No offer** — no price, no discount, no free shipping. It's a pure product launch with nothing to sweeten the deal for someone who's comparison-shopping against Brooks and New Balance at the same time.
- **Strava Challenge section** — I don't use Strava. This whole block is dead real estate for me. It reads like influencer bait, not a customer benefit.
- **HOKA cadence fatigue is real** — I've gotten six-plus emails in the past two weeks. Another fashion-first launch with no price hook barely registers anymore.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Lead with one comfort proof point** — even one sentence like "Clifton cushioning. Now in bold colorways." ties the style pitch back to why I trust HOKA at all. Right now comfort and color are completely disconnected.
- 2. **Add a price anchor or introductory offer** — even "Starting at $X" or "Members get early access" gives me a reason to click beyond curiosity.
- 3. **Show one men's colorway front-and-center** — the shoes are technically gender-neutral but every photo pairs them with a young woman. One clean men's walking shot changes the demographic read entirely.
- 4. **Cut the Strava block or bury it** — if it stays, move it to the bottom. It's a niche ask that means nothing to the casual comfort shopper.
- 5. **Subject Alt A:** `New colorways: Clifton & Bondi in bold`
- 6. **Subject Alt B:** `Motion Party Pack — now in men's`
- 7. **Preheader Alt A:** `Fully cushioned, fully flooded — see the new palette`
- 8. **Preheader Alt B:** `Same comfort. Louder color. Shop the Motion Party Pack.`

## 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Duplicate, conflicting `<meta viewport>` tags:**
```html
<!-- Tag 1 -->
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
<!-- Tag 2 (duplicate) -->
<meta content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1, user-scalable=yes" name="viewport" />
```
The two tags carry conflicting directives — the first omits `user-scalable`, the second adds `user-scalable=yes`. Browsers use the last-declared tag; email clients may behave unpredictably. The duplicate should be removed.

**Malformed `<meta charset>` — missing `http-equiv` attribute:**
```html
<meta content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />
```
Should be:
```html
<meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />
```
Without `http-equiv`, some legacy clients (Outlook 2007–2016) may not honor the charset declaration.

**Empty `<title>` tag:**
```html
<title></title>
```
Some clients (notably Apple Mail and Samsung Email) surface the `<title>` as a preview label or document name. An empty value is a missed fallback opportunity and can appear as a blank subject line in certain preview contexts.

**Malformed CSS property values — missing numeric tokens:**
```css
/* In @media only screen and (max-width:480px) */
.nav_padding { padding-top:px !important; padding-bottom:px !important; }
.nav_border  { border-top:px solid ; }
```
Both rules have zero-length or zero-color values (e.g., `padding-top:px` — the number is absent). These declarations will be silently dropped by CSS parsers. If `.nav_padding` is applied to nav elements at mobile widths, those elements will retain their non-mobile padding. `.nav_border` will render no border. These are likely template variable substitution failures (e.g., a CMS variable that should have resolved to `10px` or `1px solid #000`).

**Aggressive universal selector reset:**
```css
* { border:none; }
```
`border:none` on `*` will strip borders from `<hr>`, `<input>`, and any decorative bordered elements. In practice, most such elements are not used in email HTML, but this is a latent risk if components are reused across templates.

**Web fonts will not render in major clients:** Gmail, Outlook (all versions on Windows), and Yahoo Mail block external `@font-face` entirely. The declared fallback stack needs to be verified once the full body is accessible — if body copy relies on `TheFutureHOKA` without a safe-web-font fallback, text will render in the client default (usually Times New Roman on Outlook).
