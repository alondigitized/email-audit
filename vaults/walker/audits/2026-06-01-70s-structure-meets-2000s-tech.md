# '70s structure meets 2000s tech.
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-01**
## Executive summary

- New Balance pivots from the sale drumbeat — and honestly, the change of pace is welcome. Five emails in the last two weeks have all been variations of "25% off select shoes," so something with a story behind it at least makes me pause before I hit delete. The subject line grabbed me enough to open: I was alive in the '70s and I appreciate when a brand nods to the era without being corny about it.
- But once I'm inside, this email isn't really for me. The hero model is a young woman in athleisure — fine for her, but not who I am. Then lower down there's a second young woman sitting with the shoe. Where's the guy in his sixties who wants to know if his feet won't bark at him after a few hours of walking around a hardware store? The "Available for all ages" section is the closest they get to waving at me, and even that's a vague gesture rather than a clear pitch. I can't tell from anything in this email whether the 204L has cushioning that matters to me, whether it comes in wide widths, or whether I can slip in and out without bending down. Those questions go unanswered.
- I'll probably scroll the 204L colorways, maybe click — the 25% off banner at top is doing its usual thing — but I won't forward this to anyone and I won't feel like New Balance looked me in the eye. Mark as read and move on.

## What's working

- **The subject line earned the open.** "'70s structure meets 2000s tech." is the most interesting thing NB has put in my inbox since the Members offer on May 18. It's short, it has a hook, and it made me curious enough to tap.
- **The 204L colorway grid does the job.** Multiple neutral tones — the grey and the sandy beige both catch my eye as shoes I could actually wear somewhere.
- **"Available for all ages" is a smart line.** It's the one moment the email reaches across the demographic gap and says I might belong here.
- **Clean layout, no broken images.** Nothing confusing or truncated; the visual hierarchy from hero shot down to product grid is easy to follow on a phone screen.
- **The 25% off banner at the top carries over from ongoing sale messaging** — I at least know there's a financial incentive waiting if I click.

## What's weak

- **Both models are young women.** I'm a 62-year-old man. I can look past that, but it signals to me that this shoe is not being marketed to me specifically, even after years of being on this list.
- **Zero comfort language.** Nothing about cushioning, arch support, or how this shoe actually feels after a few miles. "2000s tech" is marketing speak that tells me nothing useful.
- **The preview text is a wasted slot.** "The 204L" and then invisible padding characters. In my inbox it just trails off — they could have put something like "Retro look, modern cushion. Men's sizes available." and actually reeled me in harder.
- **The sale banner at top is getting stale.** I've now seen "up to 25% off select shoes" in at least four consecutive emails. It no longer feels like urgency — it feels like wallpaper.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Add one comfort signal to the hero copy.** Even a single line — "Fresh Air cushioning underneath. Retro everything else." — would give me a reason to care beyond the aesthetics.
- 2. **Show a male model, preferably not 25 years old.** The "Available for all ages" line is doing the heavy lifting but the visuals contradict it. One shot of a man wearing the 204L on a sidewalk changes the whole read for me.
- 3. **Use the preview text.** Right now it's just the model name and then nothing. Tell me one more thing — the offer, the silhouette, whether it comes in wide.
- 4. **Retire the 25% off banner or make it the headline.** Either lean into the sale or lean into the story. Both at half-volume makes neither work.

- **Preheader Alt A:** `Fresh colorways, 25% off select styles — your size is in there`

### 1. Technical Summary

The email template has three confirmed code-level defects in the visible HTML: a duplicate CSS class definition that causes a color collision, a set of empty `@font-face` declarations that will silently fall through to system fonts, and an unfilled placeholder URL using unencrypted HTTP. The HTML source is truncated so link, UTM, and compliance sections are partially assessable.

---

### 2. Link & Tracking Issues

**Unfilled placeholder in background image URL (confirmed defect)**
```css
.bgwhite {
  background-image: url('http://image.XXXXX.png');
}
```
`XXXXX` is an unresolved template variable — this URL will 404. The HTTP scheme (not HTTPS) will also trigger mixed-content warnings in secure clients (Gmail, Apple Mail on iOS). Any element using `.bgwhite` will render without its intended background.

**Remaining links not assessable** — The HTML is truncated before the body content. UTM parameter presence on anchor `href` values cannot be confirmed.

---

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**`.bg1` class defined twice with conflicting values (confirmed defect)**
```css
/* First definition (line ~14) */
.bg1 { background-color: #ffffff; }

/* Second definition (later in same <style> block) */
.bg1 { background: #58546d; }
```
The second rule wins via cascade. Any element assigned `.bg1` expecting white (`#ffffff`) will instead render with a dark muted-purple (`#58546d`). This is likely an authoring error — one of these definitions is wrong.

**All `@font-face` declarations are empty — no `src:` property on any (confirmed defect)**
```css
@font-face { font-family: linotype-didot, serif; font-weight: 400; font-style: normal; }
@font-face { font-family: proxima-nova, sans-serif; font-weight: 700; font-style: normal; }
/* ... 7 total, all missing src: */
```
Without a `src:` URL, none of these font faces will load. All text will fall back to `Arial` / `Times New Roman`. If the intent was to load from Adobe Fonts/Typekit via `<link>` in `<head>`, that tag is not present in the supplied source. Email clients that block external CSS will also silently fall back, so this is only recoverable if a `<link rel="stylesheet">` tag exists elsewhere.

**Proxima Nova bold-italic `@font-face` has a duplicate `font-family` property**
```css
@font-face {
  font-family: proxima-nova, sans-serif;
  font-weight: 700;
  font-style: italic;
  font-family: proxima-nova, sans-serif; /* duplicate, ignored */
}
```
Minor authoring noise; no functional impact beyond the already-broken `src:` issue.

**`<meta>` tag missing `http-equiv` attribute**
```html
<meta content="IE=edge" />
```
Should be:
```html
<meta http-equiv="X-UA-Compatible" content="IE=edge" />
```
Without `http-equiv`, the tag is invalid and ignored. Low real-world impact (IE is end-of-life), but indicates template hygiene issues.

**DOCTYPE / namespace mismatch**
The document declares `HTML 4.01 Transitional` but simultaneously uses `xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml"`. While common in legacy email templates, this is technically inconsistent. No confirmed rendering failures from this alone.

**Alt text on images** — Cannot assess; `<img>` tags not present in truncated source.
