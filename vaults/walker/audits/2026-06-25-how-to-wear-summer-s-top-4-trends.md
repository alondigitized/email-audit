# How to wear summer’s top 4 trends
**Score:** 6/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-19**
## Executive summary

- DSW in the sender column, my thumb opened it before I even read the subject. That's just muscle memory at this point — I've opened almost every DSW email they've sent me this month. Then the email loaded, and once again: trends. Four of them. "Minimalist," "Jelly," "Retro," "Natural" — all shown on women in strappy sandals and slide-on heels. The one big button I could find said "SHOP WOMEN." I kept scrolling hoping for a men's walking shoe or a Brooks or a Skechers slip-on, and I never found it.
- There's a buy-more-save-more deal up top — spend X, get $10 off, spend more, get $20 or $60 — and that's real money if I'm buying something I actually want. But the whole rest of the email is a fashion editorial that could have run in a women's magazine. I didn't see a single men's shoe, a single comfort callout, nothing about arch support or cushioning. It read like they grabbed the wrong segment list.
- I'm marking it read and moving on. Won't forward it — nobody I know needs jelly sandal advice.

## What's working

- **The buy-more-save-more tier is front and center.** $10/$20/$60 at the very top — I spotted it immediately and the offer math is simple enough that I didn't have to squint.
- **No render issues.** Clean layout, images loaded, text was readable without zooming. After two weeks of opening DSW emails, at least the tech is reliable.
- **The "Shop by Size" block at the bottom** is a nice practical touch — if I ever made it that far and found men's shoes, that's how I'd want to shop.
- **Preview text earned the open.** "Try 'em now & get $10 off" is concrete, doesn't repeat the subject, and that $10 kept me curious enough to tap.

## What's weak

- **100% women's content.** Every model, every trend name, every CTA — nothing here was aimed at me. "SHOP WOMEN" as the primary button told me everything I needed to know about who built this list.
- **"How to wear trends" is not my language.** I'm not shopping for a look. I'm shopping for a shoe that doesn't wreck my feet by 2pm. Trend styling advice lands like a foreign language.
- **Jelly shoes.** I'm 62. I don't need a rubber shoe trend report.
- **No comfort or fit language anywhere.** Not one mention of cushioning, arch support, wide widths, or easy on/off. Those are my buying triggers and this email has zero of them.
- **DSW is running this almost daily now.** Between June 16 and today I've opened at least six of these. The cadence is starting to feel like noise — and when each one turns out to be women's trend content, the muscle memory that once served them starts costing them.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Segment the list by gender before you send trend content.** This email might have worked on someone else. On me it's wasted. If DSW has my purchase history, they know I haven't bought women's shoes — use it.
- 2. **Lead with comfort, not trends.** Swap the trend editorial for a hero that speaks to men: "Walking all day? These hold up." Something that earns the click from someone who doesn't speak fashion.
- 3. **Give the $10 off a home in the men's section.** The offer is fine, but it needs to point somewhere I can spend it. Right now it's a coupon with no shelf I want to shop.
- 4. **Pull back the cadence or vary the creative.** Six near-identical emails in ten days and I'm starting to tune out the sender name that used to trigger an open.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**`Src:` (capital S) in all `@font-face` declarations — invalid CSS**
All 11 `@font-face` blocks use `Src:` instead of the required lowercase `src:`. CSS property names are case-insensitive in browsers but strictly lowercase in the spec; several email client renderers (particularly Outlook's WebKit layer) may silently drop the rule, causing fallback font rendering for all recipients.

```css
/* as written — invalid */
Src: url("https://braze-images.com/...woff") format('woff');

/* correct */
src: url("https://braze-images.com/...woff") format('woff');
```

**`font-style: normal` on all italic variants**
`HelveticaNow_Italic`, `HelveticaNow_MediumItalic`, and `HelveticaNow_BoldItalic` all declare `font-style: normal`. These faces will never be matched by the browser's font-matching algorithm when italic text is encountered — they are effectively unreachable unless called explicitly by family name.

**`maximum-scale=1` blocks pinch-to-zoom**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Prevents user zoom on mobile. Fails WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text, AA). Many accessibility validators flag this automatically.

**`text-size-adjust: none` in global reset**
```css
* { -ms-text-size-adjust: none; text-size-adjust: none }
```
The value `none` fully disables text scaling, stricter than the conventional `100%`. This can make text illegible for users who rely on browser/OS text scaling.

**Missing `http-equiv` attributes on two meta tags**

```html
<!-- charset — missing http-equiv="Content-Type" -->
<meta content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />

<!-- IE compat — missing http-equiv="X-UA-Compatible" -->
<meta content="IE=Edge" />
```

Without `http-equiv`, these hints are inert in several older Outlook and webmail renderers. Charset in particular must be declared correctly or multi-byte characters may corrupt.

**Near-black color `#000001` used intentionally as a dark-mode escape hatch**
```css
.dm-font-black { color: #000001 !important; }
border-top: 1px #000001 solid !important;
```
This is a known technique to defeat Gmail's dark-mode color inversion (which targets `#000000` specifically). Functionally acceptable, but flag for QA so it isn't "corrected" to pure black by future editors.
