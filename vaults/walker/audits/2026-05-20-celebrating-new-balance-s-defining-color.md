# Celebrating New Balance's defining color.
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-05-20**
## Executive summary

- Four days ago New Balance sent me "Grey - a color all its own" and I gave it a 6 because I couldn't figure out what they wanted me to do with the color grey. Today I'm looking at "Grey Days 2026" and asking myself the same question. I'm a 62-year-old with a bad knee and flat feet who needs arch support to get through a Costco run without limping. A branded color campaign does not speak to me the way "25% off walking shoes" does.
- That said, the layout here is cleaner than I expected. Six shoe models in a grid, a lifestyle break in the middle, a "More in grey" section below — I can at least browse it on my phone without squinting. I recognize the 9060 and 990v6 by name, and that counts for something. But there's no price on anything. No "Walker, as a member you save X." No mention of cushioning, arch support, or fit. Just grey shoes being grey.
- I'd scroll through, maybe tap once, and probably leave when there's nothing to anchor the price in my head. Not delete-and-block — New Balance is a brand I respect — but this doesn't make me reach for my wallet.

## What's working

- Six shoe models in a clean grid, easy to browse one-handed on my phone
- "Grey Days 2026" reads like a real campaign event, not a generic clearance push
- Free shipping threshold is right at the top where I catch it immediately — $75 is reasonable
- "Shop now" is a visible, tappable button — no guessing what to do next
- Familiar model names (9060, 990v6) build trust; I know what I'm looking at

## What's weak

- Not one word about comfort, cushioning, or fit anywhere in the entire email — I need a reason to buy, not just a reason to admire a color
- No price, not even a starting-at figure — can't do the math without clicking, and I probably won't bother
- Models look like they're in their mid-30s; I don't see myself in any of these shots
- This is the second grey-focused send in five days — May 15's "Grey - a color all its own" hit the same beat; the creative idea hasn't meaningfully evolved
- No loyalty reference or member pricing — I've been on their list long enough that ignoring that completely feels like they don't know who I am

## Recommendations

- 1. Drop even one line of comfort copy into the hero alongside the campaign branding — "Cushioned for all-day wear" costs zero extra space and gives me a reason to care
- 2. Add a price anchor — "starting at $XX" or a member-exclusive percentage off; free shipping alone doesn't move me toward checkout
- 3. Include at least one older male model — not every frame, just one that signals this shoe belongs on a 60-year-old's foot too
- 4. Differentiate this send from May 15 explicitly — if Grey Days is a standalone annual event, say so ("Grey Days 2026 — Now Through Sunday") so it doesn't read as the same promo reshuffled
- 5. Make the CTA specific: "Shop Men's Grey Day Picks" beats "Shop now" every time for someone like me who needs direction

## Technical Audit — New Balance "Celebrating New Balance's defining color"

---

### 1. Technical Summary

Email is built on an HTML 4.01 Transitional doctype with inline CSS class architecture and partial dark mode support. Several CSS authoring defects, a broken background image token, and a missing meta attribute are confirmed in the provided source.

---

### 2. Link & Tracking Issues

**HTTP background image (confirmed insecure)**
`.bgwhite` references a non-HTTPS asset:
```css
background-image: url('http://image.XXXXX.png');
```
HTTP images are blocked by default in Gmail, Outlook.com, and iOS Mail. Additionally, `XXXXX` appears to be an unreplaced template token — this background will render broken in send.

**UTM / click tracking** — Cannot assess from truncated source. Verify all `<a href>` values carry `utm_source=email&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=...` before deploy.

---

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**CSS class `.bg1` defined twice with conflicting values**
```css
/* definition 1 */
.bg1 { background-color: #ffffff; }

/* definition 2 — later in same <style> block */
.bg1 { background: #58546d; }
```
The second declaration wins in all clients, making the first a dead rule. Any element relying on white background via `.bg1` will render purple-gray (`#58546d`). Confirmed conflict at two points in the same `<style>` block.

**`@font-face` blocks have no `src:` declarations**
All nine `@font-face` rules (proxima-nova, linotype-didot, proxima-nova-extra-condensed, etc.) omit the `src` property entirely. These are non-functional as written. Fonts likely load via an external Typekit/Adobe Fonts `<link>` tag not shown in the truncated source — if so, the `@font-face` blocks are dead weight; if not, all web fonts will silently fall back to Arial/Times New Roman.

**Malformed `@font-face` — duplicate `font-family` property**
```css
@font-face {
  font-family: proxima-nova,sans-serif;
  font-weight: 700;
  font-style: italic;
  font-family: proxima-nova,sans-serif; /* duplicate — invalid */
}
```
The second `font-family` declaration is a copy-paste error. No functional breakage since both values are identical, but signals template hygiene issues.

**`<meta content="IE=edge" />` missing `http-equiv` attribute**
```html
<!-- as authored -->
<meta content="IE=edge" />

<!-- correct -->
<meta http-equiv="X-UA-Compatible" content="IE=edge" />
```
Without `http-equiv`, Outlook desktop ignores this directive.

**Single responsive breakpoint**
Only `max-width: 414px` is defined. No `600px` breakpoint means layouts between 415px–600px (many Android devices, iPad Mini) receive the desktop layout unmodified.

No subscriber-level merge tokens (first name, loyalty status, etc.) are visible in the truncated source. If the subject line or preheader uses personalization, confirm token fill rates are monitored post-send.
