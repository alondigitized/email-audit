# The 740 in new colors
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-12**
## Executive summary

- Well, at least it wasn't another kids' shoes send. I'll give New Balance that much. After what feels like two weeks of emails about children's styles, a pink edit, and a "footwear favorites" with nothing for me in it, I actually paused when I saw "The 740 in new colors" in the subject. That's a real shoe name. Something I could conceivably buy. So I opened it.
- And then I scrolled and saw two young women, a color grid of pastels and neutrals, and a headline that said "Colorways for all ages." No price. No cushioning claim. No mention of arch support or how this shoe actually feels on a 62-year-old foot after an hour of walking. Nothing about width options. Just pretty colors arranged in a grid like a paint chip display at the hardware store.
- I'd mark this as read and keep scrolling. Wouldn't click, wouldn't forward. It's not offensive — it's just empty. A catalog page dressed up as an email.

## What's working

- **Honest subject line.** "The 740 in new colors" is exactly what this is. No bait-and-switch, no mystery teaser. After the "Now's your chance" debacle that turned out to be about kids' shoes, I'll take clarity over cleverness every time.
- **Clean layout.** The email renders without any visual breakage — shoe grid loads, CTA button shows up, nothing overlapping or missing. Easy enough to follow even on my phone.
- **"Colorways for all ages" — I see what you're trying to do.** It's a reach, but at least they're gesturing toward the idea that this shoe isn't only for 24-year-olds. Credit for the attempt, even if the models don't back it up.
- **Specific product featured throughout.** The whole email stays on one shoe. That's focused. I don't have to figure out what they're selling.

## What's weak

- **Every model in this email is a young woman.** I'm a 62-year-old man. I can't picture myself in this shoe based on what I see. The 740 might be a great shoe for me — I have no way of knowing that from this email.
- **Zero comfort language.** No cushioning. No arch support. No mention of how this shoe performs. For a brand that built its reputation on fit and feel, this email reads like it forgot what it sells.
- **No price anywhere prominent.** I don't need a sale, but I need a number. If I'm clicking, I want to know whether I'm looking at a $90 shoe or a $150 one before I bother.
- **The preview text is a lifestyle punchline.** "If there's a wrong way to wear them, we haven't thought of it" — that's a fashion-mag caption. It tells me nothing about the shoe, the deal, or why I should care right now.
- **Still no break in cadence.** I've gotten seven-plus New Balance emails in the past three weeks. Most weren't for me. This one is closer, but sending this many in a row has trained me to scroll past the sender name without much hope.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Put one male model — ideally someone over 50 — in the hero.** The 740 is supposedly for all ages; show me that. One shot of a guy my age wearing these on a weekend walk would do more for me than any headline.
- 2. **Add a single comfort claim to the hero copy.** Even something like "Cushioned for all-day wear" under the shoe name would give me a reason to keep reading. Right now the copy is all aesthetic, nothing functional.
- 3. **Show the price.** Not a discount — just the number. Anchor me so I know what I'm considering before I click.
- 4. **Pull back the send frequency.** Seven NB emails in three weeks is too many. Consolidate into fewer, better-targeted sends. At this rate I'm going to start treating them like background noise.

- **Subject Alt B:** `New Balance 740: cushioned, versatile, new colors`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Available in men's and women's — starting at $X`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Retro look, everyday comfort. Shop the new 740 palette.`
