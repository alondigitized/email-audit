# Final hours $15 OFF!
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-02**
## Executive summary

- DSW again — seventh or eighth time in my inbox this week, I've honestly lost count. I spotted "Final hours" in the subject and my thumb twitched out of pure habit at this point. But I'll give it this: the countdown clock was the first thing my eyes went to, and "6 hours and change" felt real. That's better than the "Big weekend, even BIGGER offer" send from earlier today, which leaned on energy but had no actual ticking clock. This one has teeth.
- The tiered buy-more-save-more structure — $15 off $49, $20 off $99, $60 off $199 — is laid out clean enough that I didn't need reading glasses to parse it. I appreciated that. My gripe is the same one I have with most DSW sends: nothing on this page told me they carry wide-width walking shoes built for a guy whose feet have opinions. The semi-annual sale banner was a nice reminder I should probably get in-store, but the "Shop Wide Width" button buried below the fold is the only thing in here that speaks to me directly.
- I'd mark it read and probably click SHOP MEN out of mild curiosity — mostly because I'm already in a discount mindset this week and $15 off $49 actually gets me to a comfortable price on a pair of walking shoes. Wouldn't forward it. If a friend asked "is DSW doing anything right now?" I'd say yeah, but just Google it.

## What's working

- The countdown timer ("ENDS IN 06:52:39") is the single most effective element — gives me a real reason to act now instead of punting to later.
- Tiered discount is easy to scan: three rows, big dollar amounts, no math required. My brain lands on "$20 off $99" in about one second.
- "SHOP WIDE WIDTH" and "SHOP WIDE CALF" buttons are buried but genuinely relevant — the fact that they're there at all tells me DSW knows their audience has real-world feet.
- The semi-annual sale orange banner reinforces that this isn't a manufactured excuse to email — there's an actual sale event behind it.
- VIP "free shipping" callout is visible without digging; that matters to me because I hate paying shipping on a $50 shoe.

## What's weak

- Zero mention of comfort, cushioning, arch support, or walking — this could be an email for my daughter-in-law who buys heels. The entire hero section is generic "buy more save more" with no nod to why I'm subscribed.
- The "SO NEW, SO YOU" product grid is too small to read on a phone screen and the mix of shoe styles looks like it skews young/fashionable — not a 62-year-old guy's section.
- That asterisk on "$60 OFF $199*" makes me suspicious. I've been burned before by "exclusions apply" fine print that disqualifies the exact shoe I wanted. Makes me hesitate.
- This is the second email I've gotten from DSW today. I'm not annoyed enough to unsubscribe, but I'm getting close to ignoring them on reflex — good urgency signal can't fully fix email fatigue.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Men's comfort hook in the hero** — even one line under the discount tiers: "Hoka, New Balance, Skechers — stacked in Men's" would have stopped my scroll immediately.
- 2. **Surface the Wide Width CTA above the fold** — that button is doing real work for guys like me; it should be right under the tiered offer, not four sections down.
- 3. **Spell out the asterisk exclusions in plain English** — "includes clearance" or "some exclusions apply on select brands" right under the $60 line. The mystery asterisk reads as fine-print hiding.
- 4. **One hero product, not a grid of thumbnails** — pick one standout men's walking shoe at the clearance price and show it big. A guy my age buys the shoe that looks comfortable, not the one buried in a six-panel grid.
- 5. **Pull back on cadence** — at seven-plus sends this week, the urgency in this subject has less impact than it deserves.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**`http-equiv` attributes missing on two meta tags:**
```html
<!-- Missing http-equiv="Content-Type" -->
<meta content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />

<!-- Missing http-equiv="X-UA-Compatible" -->
<meta content="IE=Edge" />
```
Without `http-equiv`, these tags are no-ops. The charset declaration in particular will not be recognized by older Outlook and some webmail renderers, risking mojibake on special characters (em dashes, `$`, accented letters).

**`Src:` (capital S) in all 10 `@font-face` declarations:**
```css
Src: url("https://braze-images.com/appboy/...") format('woff');
```
CSS property names are case-insensitive per spec, but several email client preprocessors (notably older Samsung Mail, some Lotus Notes variants) silently drop malformed property names. Use lowercase `src:`.

**HelveticaNow fonts served as `.ttf` only — no `.woff` fallback:**
```css
/* Futura PT — .woff (good) */
Src: url("...original.woff") format('woff');

/* HelveticaNow — .ttf only (poor email client support) */
Src: url("...original.ttf?1755182939") format('truetype');
```
TTF has significantly lower support than WOFF in email clients. Apple Mail renders it; Outlook on Windows will not load it at all and falls back to system fonts, which may break intended typographic hierarchy.

**`supported-color-schemes` used as a CSS property:**
```css
:root {
  color-scheme: light dark;
  supported-color-schemes: light dark; /* not a valid CSS property */
}
```
`supported-color-schemes` is a `<meta>` tag attribute, not a CSS property. It is correctly placed as a meta tag elsewhere in the `<head>` and should be removed from the `:root` block to avoid spec noise (harmless in practice but technically invalid).

**10 custom font files loaded per send:**
Six Futura PT + four HelveticaNow variants from `braze-images.com`. No conditional loading. On slow connections this adds render latency before fallback fonts activate; not a blocking issue but worth noting for inbox load performance.
