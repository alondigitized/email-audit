# Classic styles for kids
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-14**
## Executive summary

- I've been getting New Balance emails almost every other day for two weeks now, and they've been missing me left and right — kids styles on June 6th, a pink edit on June 9th, a soft "footwear favorites" on June 10th. This one doesn't even try to hide it. "Classic styles for kids." I'm 62 years old. I don't have little ones running around the house. I opened it because "574 and 9060" in the preview sounded like my models — but these are junior sizes, and the whole email is photos of children in sneakers. Nothing here is for me.
- The execution is clean, I'll give them that. Layout holds up, the product grid is readable, and the brand looks like New Balance. But there's not a single frame in this email aimed at a guy like me. No cushioning callouts, no arch support, no men's colorways, no price break. Just kids running around in gray sneakers and a "Never too young for style" tagline that confirms I am absolutely the wrong audience.
- I'm not clicking. I'm not forwarding. I'm hitting delete and wondering if New Balance even has a list manager over there, because they've sent me something that didn't fit in four of the last six emails. At some point a brand's credibility starts sliding.

## What's working

- **The brand is recognizable.** New Balance in my inbox still makes me pause — I've been buying their walking shoes for years.
- **Free shipping call-out at the top** is visible immediately — $75+ threshold, no fine print games. Simple and honest.
- **The product grid is clean.** Six pairs of shoes, clearly labeled, no overlapping text. Readable even on a small screen.
- **"Featuring the 574 and 9060" in the preview** is smart copy on paper — those are models I know and respect.
- **No render bugs.** Everything loaded, nothing broken.

## What's weak

- **This is entirely a kids email.** I'm 62. I don't have grandkids in need of sneakers, and New Balance has no way of knowing if I do. Sending this to an adult comfort-shoe subscriber is a targeting miss, plain and simple.
- **The preview is a mild bait-and-switch.** "Featuring the 574 and 9060" made me think adult models. The 574 and 9060 I know come in men's sizes I actually buy. When I opened, every shoe was a child's size. That feels like a trick.
- **No offer for me.** No percentage off, no member pricing, no men's sale. Just free shipping on $75 — which I'd spend on kids shoes I don't need.
- **This is the seventh New Balance email in twelve days**, and at least four of them have been off-target for me. The cadence alone is starting to feel like spam, and the repeated misses are burning goodwill faster than a sale could rebuild it.
- **The adult content at the bottom** — a few T-shirts and a hoodie — feels like an afterthought, not a save.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Segment me out of the kids list.** If I've only ever clicked on men's walking shoes, I should not be receiving children's footwear emails. This is a CRM/segmentation fix, not a creative fix — the email can be great and still land wrong on the wrong person.
- 2. **Don't use adult model names in the preview for a kids email.** "Featuring the 574 and 9060" sets an adult expectation. If the email is for kids shoes, the preview should say "Sizes for kids + free shipping" or something that accurately signals the content.
- 3. **Give me a reason to stay subscribed.** Seven emails in twelve days with repeated misses is how you end up in my junk folder. A preference center nudge — "Tell us who you're shopping for" — would stop the bleed.
- 4. **If this goes to adults anyway, lead with a men's crossover hook.** Something like "The shoes they'll grow into — and the ones you won't outgrow." Then show kids AND a men's option side by side.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Shop the 574 + 9060 — now in kids' sizes`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Kids' classics. Free shipping on $75+.`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `The 574 and 9060 your little one will actually want to wear.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Classic New Balance. Now sized for the next generation.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Critical — Duplicate `.bg1` class with conflicting values:**
```css
/* Declaration 1 (line ~10) */
.bg1 { background-color: #ffffff; }

/* Declaration 2 (line ~118, later in cascade) */
.bg1 { background: #58546d; }
```
The second declaration (purple-gray `#58546d`) wins everywhere `.bg1` is applied. The white background intent of the first is silently discarded. Any element relying on `.bg1` for a white background will render purple-gray.

**All `@font-face` blocks are non-functional — no `src:` property:**
```css
@font-face { font-family: proxima-nova, sans-serif; font-weight: 700; font-style: normal; }
```
None of the nine `@font-face` declarations include a `src:` URL. These blocks load nothing. Fallback fonts (`Arial`, `Times New Roman`) will render instead — functionally harmless but the `@font-face` blocks are dead weight that should either be wired to a CDN source or removed.

**Malformed IE compatibility meta tag:**
```html
<meta content="IE=edge" />
```
Missing `http-equiv="X-UA-Compatible"`. Without the `http-equiv` attribute this tag is ignored; it provides no Outlook/IE rendering mode signal.

**Dark mode global link override uses `!important` on all `<a>` tags:**
```css
@media (prefers-color-scheme: dark) {
  a { color: #cf0a2c !important; }
}
```
This overrides any explicitly colored links (white CTAs on dark backgrounds, footer links, etc.) in dark mode, which will make white-on-dark buttons unreadable — links turn red regardless of button background.
