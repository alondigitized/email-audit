# For the BEST DAD EVER: 30% off Nike & Jordan
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-10**
## Executive summary

- First thing my eye goes to: that red-and-yellow Inflation Relief banner is back. I specifically remember the June 9th send — "So many compliments. Love the color!" — because I noticed the banner had finally disappeared. One email without it, and now it's sitting right back up top like it never left. I've trained myself to scroll past it without reading a word.
- Past the banner, "Best DAD ever!" caught me for about a half-second — I am a dad. Then I checked the calendar. Father's Day was Sunday, June 21st. This landed in my inbox on the 25th, four days late. If I was buying a Father's Day gift, I missed it. If I'm supposed to treat myself, the hero is Nike and Jordan, and that's not my comfort-first playbook. I eventually scrolled down and found a Brooks and Skechers 30% off module buried near the bottom — that's actually the offer I'd click — but they made me work for it.
- The thing that almost ended this before it started: the preview text in my inbox was a raw tracking URL. Looked like a phishing link. On a good day I'd tap through. On a bad day I'd hit delete and never know the Brooks deal was in there.

## What's working

- The 30% off number is clear and immediate — no math required, no squinting.
- The stacking offer (Inflation Relief code on top of a sale) gives a real sense of savings if you're paying attention.
- Brooks and Skechers both appear in the lower module at 30% off — those are my brands, and that offer is genuinely interesting.
- The father-and-kid hero image is warm and appropriate; feels like someone who shops here, not a billboard aimed at a 25-year-old.
- The email body renders cleanly on my phone — no layout weirdness, no overlap, readable without zooming.

## What's weak

- This is a Father's Day email that arrived four days after Father's Day. That's a timing miss I can't overlook.
- The preview text was a raw click-tracking URL in my inbox. That's the first thing I see. It looked like spam.
- Nike and Jordan are the hero, but Brooks and Skechers are what I actually shop — the email buried the most relevant offer below the fold.
- The Inflation Relief banner is back. After weeks of seeing it, I don't read it anymore. It's visual noise at this point.
- No expiration date on the 30% off or the SAVEMORE code — I have no idea if I have two days or two weeks, so I put it off.

## Recommendations

- 1. Fix the preheader before anything else — a leaked URL in the inbox preview is a trust killer and it's the first impression, not the last.
- 2. Don't send a Father's Day email after Father's Day. If the offer is still live, reframe the subject line around the sale, not the holiday.
- 3. Move Brooks and Skechers into the hero position. Those are the comfort-first brands that drive purchases from someone like me. Nike can be a secondary module.
- 4. Put a deadline on the offer. "Ends Sunday" or "3 days left" gives me a reason to click now instead of saving the email and forgetting about it.
- 5. Rotate or retire the Inflation Relief banner — it's been in so many sends that it doesn't register as a real offer anymore.

### 1. Technical Summary

The email is structurally sound with no render-blocking defects. Two issues warrant pre-send correction: a probable stale campaign tag (brand mismatch in UTM) and an unresolved or unverifiable merge token in an open-tracking pixel.

### 2. Link & Tracking Issues

**Campaign tag brand mismatch — high confidence bug**
The open-tracking pixel URL contains:
```
utm_campaign=SCA_20260610_nike_jordan_brooks_AM
```
The substring `brooks` appears in a Nike/Jordan campaign. This strongly suggests the send was cloned from a Brooks campaign and the UTM `campaign` value was not updated. If this parameter propagates to any outbound links, analytics will misattribute opens and clicks to the wrong brand campaign.

**`[UNIQUE]` token in open-tracking pixel — verify before send**
```
https://0di86k1v.emltrk.com/v2/0di86k1v?d=[UNIQUE]
```
`[UNIQUE]` appears as a literal string. Most ESPs resolve this to a subscriber-scoped identifier at send time; confirm the ESP will substitute it. If it sends as a literal, every open is attributed to the same record, corrupting open-rate data.
