# Want First Dibs on $20 off? Become a VIP!
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-20**
## Executive summary

- Here we go again. I hadn't even finished my coffee when another DSW birthday email hit my inbox — and look, I know I just said yesterday that the bday campaign was wearing me out. Third time this week on this exact 25%-or-$20-off birthday angle. At least this one pivots the ask a little — instead of "buy something now," it's "sign up to be a VIP and *then* get $20 off." That's a different animal, and not necessarily a worse one, but I had to stop and recalibrate when I saw "BECOME A VIP" as the big green button. I came here expecting a sale, not a sign-up form.
- The other thing I noticed pretty quick: the shoes in this email aren't for me. The "Today, Tomorrow, Etc." section is showing brown mules and strappy sandals. The "Shop by Size" grid at the bottom? Women's sizes — W6, W6.5, W7 — all the way across. I'm a size 10½ wide and I want something cushioned for a long walk. This email didn't even try to speak to that.
- I'd mark this read and move on. I'm not clicking to "Become a VIP" when I don't know what that commitment looks like, and I'm not browsing women's summer sandals. A friend? No. My wife might appreciate the forward, but even then, she's seen this birthday pitch enough times already.

## What's working

- The $20 off number is front and center in the hero — I clocked it immediately, no hunting required.
- The "BECOME A VIP" button is big and green; there's no confusion about what they want me to do.
- No broken images or layout disasters. The email rendered cleanly top to bottom.
- The birthday theme is at least internally consistent — BDAY SALE shows up in the hero, the ICYMI section, even the sweepstakes banner. It's coherent.

## What's weak

- Every featured product is women's shoes. The size grid is women's sizing. A 62-year-old guy shopping for walking shoes has zero reason to keep scrolling.
- "BECOME A VIP" as the primary ask buries the actual shopping. I came for shoes, not a membership sign-up.
- This is the fourth DSW email in roughly 48 hours counting yesterday's triple-bday barrage. The well is dry — my goodwill for another "it's our birthday" hook is just about gone.
- The preview text trailing those spacer characters looks messy in my inbox — the "+ Styles made for soaking up summer" is fine, but then it just kind of dissolves into nothing visible.
- No urgency. No deadline. If $20 off requires becoming a VIP, I'd like to know when that offer expires. Right now it feels open-ended, which actually *reduces* my urgency to act.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Segment by gender before anything else.** Walker should be seeing men's walking shoes — Skechers Arch Fit, New Balance, slip-on sneakers — not women's mules. This is the single biggest lift available.
- 2. **Lead the VIP value prop, not just the button.** Tell me in 10 words what VIP gets me *beyond* this $20. Is it early access? Free shipping every time? Points that don't expire? I need a reason to commit.
- 3. **Add a deadline.** "Join VIP by July 25 to lock in $20 off your next pair" gives me a reason to act today instead of forgetting about it.
- 4. **Rest the birthday hook, or at least vary the creative.** Three bday emails in one day and now a fourth the next morning — the theme is fatigue, not celebration.
- 5. **Pull one specific men's comfort shoe into the hero.** A featured product (arch support, slip-on, cushioned) with a direct link converts better than a generic "shop all."

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**`Src:` (capital S) in all 10 `@font-face` declarations**
All `@font-face` blocks use `Src:` instead of the lowercase `src:`:
```css
/* Example from source */
@font-face {
  font-family: 'Futura_PT_Regular';
  Src: url("https://braze-images.com/appboy/...") format('woff');
}
```
While the CSS spec treats property names as case-insensitive, several email clients (Yahoo Mail, older Outlook WebApp) apply stricter parsing and will silently skip the entire `@font-face` rule, falling back to system fonts with unpredictable metrics.

**`-ms-text-size-adjust` declared twice with conflicting values in a single rule**
```css
* {
  -ms-text-size-adjust: 100%;   /* line 1 */
  -webkit-text-size-adjust: 100%;
  -ms-text-size-adjust: none;   /* line 2 — overrides line 1 */
  text-size-adjust: none;
}
```
The second declaration wins. Effective value for Windows clients is `none`, not `100%`. This suppresses automatic text scaling on Windows desktop Outlook Web, which can break responsive text on zoomed views.

**Font format gaps**
- Futura PT family: `.woff` only — no `woff2` (better compression), no TTF fallback
- HelveticaNow family: `.ttf` only — no `woff`/`woff2`
- HelveticaNow TTFs include cache-busting query strings (`?1755182939`); Futura PT WOFFs do not. Cache invalidation strategy is inconsistent across the two families.

**Invalid `<meta>` attributes — two occurrences**
```html
<meta content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />           <!-- missing http-equiv -->
<meta content="IE=Edge" />                             <!-- missing http-equiv -->
```
Both are missing the `http-equiv` attribute. Correct forms:
```html
<meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=utf-8" />
<meta http-equiv="X-UA-Compatible" content="IE=Edge" />
```
Without `http-equiv`, these tags are effectively no-ops. The charset declaration not firing means character encoding falls to the client's heuristic — risk of mojibake in non-UTF-8 environments.
