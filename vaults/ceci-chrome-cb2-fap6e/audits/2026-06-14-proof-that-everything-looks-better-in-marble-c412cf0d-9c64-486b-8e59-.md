# Proof that everything looks better in marble
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-14**
## Executive summary

- This is CB2's smart editorial maneuver on Day 4 of their Warehouse Sale drumbeat. Instead of another blunt sale push (they've already sent "WAREHOUSE SALE IS ON" June 10, a refresh June 13, and a Design Legends close-out June 13), they're threading the inventory through a material story — marble — to keep the campaign feeling fresh while still funneling into sale product. The hero is gorgeous: a tight architectural shot of marble surfaces that earns the subject line immediately.
- The structural tension here is that "proof that everything looks better in marble" promises editorial inspiration but the email delivers a curated sale category. That's not a fatal mismatch — it's actually a mature retention move — but the transition from hero to "WAREHOUSE SALE" could feel abrupt to anyone who opened expecting a pure design story. The lower product grid dilutes the marble theme with non-marble pieces, undercutting the editorial premise.
- Net judgement: a competent editorial-meets-sale hybrid that scores above average on execution but below its potential because the marble theme doesn't hold all the way through.

## What's working

- **Subject line intrigue**: "Proof that everything looks better in marble" is one of CB2's better editorial hooks in this run — confident, declarative, and category-specific without reading like a mass discount push.
- **Hero photography**: The marble slab/surface shot is genuinely striking and immediately validates the subject line claim the moment the email opens.
- **Warehouse Sale context**: Anchoring a material story inside an active sale event is efficient — it gives deal-seekers the discount signal and design browsers a reason to linger.
- **Cadence break**: After two aggressive sale emails in a row on June 13, the tonal shift to editorial is exactly the right pacing move to reduce unsubscribe pressure.

## What's weak

- **Theme doesn't hold through the grid**: The lower product grid includes non-marble pieces (chairs, side tables in other materials), which breaks the "everything looks better in marble" promise mid-scroll.
- **No specific discount callout in the hero**: The "WAREHOUSE SALE" banner is visible but the hero doesn't telegraph a concrete percentage — after "UP TO 60% OFF" was the bar in the June 10 launch, the omission feels like a missed anchor.
- **Urgency is absent**: No deadline, no "ending soon," no inventory signal — unusual for a sale email this deep into a campaign that's already sent an "ALMOST OVER" message on June 13.
- **CTA copy indistinct at a glance**: Buttons appear present but aren't legibly specific from a mobile-scaled view — "Shop Now" generics don't earn the click as well as "Shop Marble Sale" would.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Keep the marble theme pure through the grid** — if the inventory doesn't support full marble curation, cut the grid to 4 tight picks rather than showing off-theme product that undercuts the editorial line.
- 2. **Put a percentage in the hero or sub-hero** — something like "Up to 50% off marble favorites, this week only" bridges the editorial and the offer without destroying the design tone.
- 3. **Add a credible close signal** — the June 13 "ALMOST OVER" Design Legends close proved urgency language works; a lightweight "while quantities last" module would sharpen the click incentive without feeling crass.
- 4. **Sharpen the CTA copy** — "Shop Marble Sale" beats "Shop Now" on specificity and reinforces the theme.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Everything is better in marble. Shop the sale.`
- **Subject Alt B:** `The marble edit — up to 60% off this week`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Marble tables, lamps and more — Warehouse Sale prices while they last.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Curated from the Warehouse Sale: our favorite marble pieces right now.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Issue — un-removed template placeholder (HIGH):**
```html
<!--[IMPUT HERE CLIENT FONT IMPORT SCRIPT if needed]-->
```
This staging instruction comment shipped in the live send. It is also misspelled (`IMPUT` → `INPUT`). While invisible to recipients, it is visible in view-source and indicates incomplete QA on template finalization.

**Issue — `maximum-scale=1` blocks user zoom (MEDIUM):**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Prevents pinch-to-zoom on mobile. Fails WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text, Level AA). Many modern iOS/Android versions ignore this, but it is still a compliance flag for accessibility audits.

**Issue — wildcard `text-size-adjust: none` (MEDIUM):**
```css
*{ -webkit-text-size-adjust:none; -ms-text-size-adjust:none; ... }
```
Applying `text-size-adjust:none` via the `*` selector suppresses OS-level font scaling across all elements, not just the reset target. Standard practice limits this to `body` only.

**Issue — conflicting `img max-width` media queries (LOW):**
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img { max-width: 100% !important } }
@media only screen and (min-width: 690px) { img { max-width: 690px } }
```
The 690px query is declared after the 768px query. On viewports ≥768px, both rules match; `!important` on the 768px rule wins, making the 690px rule dead code for that range. On 690–767px viewports the 690px rule applies correctly. The ordering creates maintainability risk — removing `!important` from the 768px rule would silently break desktop image sizing.

**`<title>` is boilerplate, not campaign-specific:**
```html
<title>CB2, FURNITURE AND DECOR FOR THE MODERN HOME</title>
```
Some clients (Apple Mail, Outlook on Mac) surface the `<title>` value. A campaign-specific title improves threading and client-side search. Low severity.
