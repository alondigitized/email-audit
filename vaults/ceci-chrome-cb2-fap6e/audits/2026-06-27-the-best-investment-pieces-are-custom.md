# The best investment pieces are custom
**Score:** 6/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-27**
## Executive summary

- CB2's June 27 send attempts the same editorial escape the June 23 "dinner party" email pulled off successfully — using an aspirational concept ("custom as investment") to break from the warehouse sale drumbeat that's dominated the inbox since early June. The hook is genuinely smart: "investment pieces" triggers a different mental frame than discount-hunting, and the upper portion of the email commits to warm lifestyle photography with a coherent custom furnishings narrative.
- The problem is the warehouse sale section pasted below it. CB2 runs this same collision every time they try an editorial send: they attach the promotional grid rather than let the narrative breathe. The June 23 interior design services send scored a 9 precisely because it had no warehouse appendage competing for attention. Here, the premium "investment" framing in the hero and the bargain "Warehouse Sale" block two scrolls down are targeting completely different mental states in the same open. That's not segmentation — it's confusion.
- Overall this is a structurally split email that does the editorial half well enough to be worth studying, but sabotages its own positioning before the recipient finishes scrolling.

## What's working

- **"Investment pieces" framing is fresh.** First use of this angle across the entire recent sale run — meaningfully differentiates from "warehouse," "July 4th," and "back in stock" language CB2 has been cycling.
- **Warm, cohesive photography.** The camel/terracotta/cream palette in the hero and product sections is consistent with CB2's premium identity and carries editorial weight.
- **Custom-specific product curation.** The upper product rows appear dedicated to the custom offer — not generic SKUs from the warehouse grid, which gives the editorial section actual substance.
- **No render bugs.** Layout is clean; imagery loads; no broken gaps or overlapping type visible.

## What's weak

- **Warehouse sale section undercuts the investment narrative.** A "best investment pieces" email with a discount clearance block below it sends conflicting price-anchoring signals — the premium frame collapses the moment the warehouse banner appears.
- **No urgency or offer in the subject.** "The best investment pieces are custom" is aspirational but doesn't signal a discount, deadline, or exclusive — open rates from any deal-motivated segment will be suppressed.
- **The email is too long for one idea.** The editorial custom section alone would be a focused, high-quality send. Adding the warehouse grid turns it into the June 25 three-offer stack problem again.
- **Custom offer mechanics are not obvious.** From the render, it's unclear what the specific custom offer is — percentage off, lead time, configuration options — which weakens click intent.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Cut the warehouse sale section entirely.** Send this as a standalone custom-furniture editorial. Let the "investment" framing do its job without a clearance anchor dragging down perceived value. Reserve the warehouse grid for its own dedicated send — CB2 has already proven that's a viable standalone (June 25, June 26 sends).
- 2. **Add the offer to the subject.** "Investment pieces, now 25% off custom orders" is more clickable than pure aspiration. The custom angle is the hook; a number is the trigger.
- 3. **Clarify what "custom" means in the hero.** The editorial copy should specify what options are available (fabric, size, configuration) and what the lead time is — these are the details that make a custom purchase feel possible, not overwhelming.
- 4. **Time-bound the custom offer.** If custom orders require a deadline to ship before a certain date, say it. "Order by July 14 for fall delivery" converts the aspirational browser into an intentional buyer.

- **Preheader Alt A:** `Shop our custom collection before the July 4th sale ends.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Hundreds of fabric and configuration options. Same-sale pricing this week only.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Responsive coverage gap (confirmed bug):** The CSS defines two breakpoints with no overlap:
- Mobile: `@media only screen and (max-width: 418px)`
- Desktop images: `@media only screen and (min-width: 690px)`

Viewports between **419px–689px** receive neither mobile layout fixes nor desktop image constraints. This range covers common Android phones (Samsung Galaxy S series: 412px logical width), tablets in portrait, and browser zoom states. Elements will render at their default widths with no responsive adjustment.

**`maximum-scale=1` on viewport meta (accessibility):**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
This blocks pinch-to-zoom for low-vision users. WCAG 1.4.4 (Level AA) requires content to be resizable up to 200% without loss of functionality.

**`-webkit-text-size-adjust: none` on `*` selector:**
```css
* { -webkit-text-size-adjust: none; ... }
```
Applied globally, this disables OS-level font scaling on iOS. Combined with `maximum-scale=1`, text is effectively locked in size for users who rely on system accessibility settings.

**`line-height: 100%` global reset:**
```css
* { line-height: 100%; }
```
A line-height of 1.0 is tighter than WCAG's recommended minimum of 1.5 for body text (1.4.8). This override applies to all elements including paragraph copy.
