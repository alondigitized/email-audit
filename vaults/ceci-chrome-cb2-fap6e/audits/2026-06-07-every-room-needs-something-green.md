# Every room needs something green
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-07**
## Executive summary

- CB2 is threading an editorial content concept — "green as a design principle" — around what is still essentially a Midseason Sale push. The hero product, the Caldwell Velvet Sofa, is photographed beautifully: moody room, warm ambient light, deep forest green as the anchor. The top half of the email could run in a design magazine. The bottom half drops into a grid-of-product sale layout, which undercuts the editorial mood but gives the shopper somewhere concrete to go.
- The strategic logic is sound. After burning "MIDSEASON SALE" as a subject line twice (June 3rd, June 5th) and getting strong results on the editorial pivots like "*This* is midcentury" (June 6th, 8/10), CB2 is clearly learning to wrap the sale in a content frame. "Every room needs something green" continues that pattern. The problem is execution at the subject line — this one is softer and less distinctly CB2 than the recent high-water marks. It's a lifestyle claim that could come from any home goods brand.
- Arriving on the third day of consecutive CB2 sends, this email earns its keep on visual merit and product selection. But the open rate is going to take a hit from cadence fatigue before a single recipient reads a word.

## What's working

- The hero image of the green velvet sofa is genuinely stunning — warm, editorial, and makes the product feel covetable rather than merely available.
- The green color theme is cohesive across both the hero and the product grid below, which reinforces the editorial premise rather than abandoning it at scroll depth.
- The "MIDSEASON SALE" framing is present without being the entire creative premise — a clear improvement over the repetitive subject lines from June 3rd and 5th.
- The Caldwell Velvet Sofa functions as a specific, named anchor product — giving shoppers a concrete object to desire before they hit the sale grid.
- Clean layout with no visible render bugs; the hierarchy from hero → product shots → grid reads naturally.

## What's weak

- "Every room needs something green" is a lifestyle platitude, not a proposition — it lacks the tension and specificity of CB2's best recent subjects (*"*This* is midcentury"*, *"Fine dining. Free design advice."*).
- No specific discount percentage is visible in the hero — the Midseason Sale exists in a section header below the fold, which buries the price signal for skimmers.
- The product grid in the lower half is stylistically discordant with the moody editorial hero — it looks like a different email grafted on.
- Heavy cadence: this is the third CB2 send in two days (June 6th alone had two). Even a well-made email bleeds open rate when subscribers feel volume-fatigued.
- CTA buttons are visually small relative to the scale of the imagery — they don't compete for attention the way the photography does.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Sharpen the subject to name the product or tie it to the sale price signal.** The editorial angle is good but this subject is too passive. "The Caldwell Sofa is greener than you think" or "Green velvet. On sale now." would preserve the concept while adding specificity.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Green velvet, on sale now`
- **Subject Alt B:** `The sofa that turns a room green`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Shop the Caldwell and the full green edit — Midseason prices.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Add something living to every room — the Midseason Sale is still on.`
- 2. **Surface a specific discount in the hero section.** Even a subtle "Up to X% off" badge near the sofa CTA bridges the editorial-to-sale gap without breaking the mood.
- 3. **Give the lower product grid a unifying editorial header.** Something like "Shop the green edit — Midseason pricing" makes the grid feel like a curated collection rather than a generic sale dump.
- 4. **Consider pulling back to one email tomorrow.** The cadence from June 5th–7th is aggressive. A rest day or a digest format would preserve list health and make each send feel more considered.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Issue A — Unremoved template placeholder (HIGH)**
```html
<!--[IMPUT HERE CLIENT FONT IMPORT SCRIPT if needed]-->
```
This is a production build artifact. The word "IMPUT" is also a typo for "INPUT." This comment is visible in raw source to any recipient who inspects the email. Remove before send.

**Issue B — Internal version tag leaked**
```html
<!--V2-->
```
Internal versioning comment rendered at the top of the document. Cosmetically harmless, but leaks internal workflow metadata.

**Issue C — `maximum-scale=1` blocks user zoom (MEDIUM)**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Prevents pinch-zoom on mobile. WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text) requires content to be resizable without assistive technology. This is a compliance risk for accessibility.

**Issue D — Global text-size-adjust suppression (MEDIUM)**
```css
* { -webkit-text-size-adjust: none; -ms-text-size-adjust: none; ... }
```
Applying `text-size-adjust: none` to the universal selector (`*`) overrides OS-level text scaling for visually impaired users. Scope this to `body` only, or remove it — the `body`-level rule above it already sets both values to `100%`.

**Issue E — Mobile breakpoint is too narrow (LOW)**
```css
@media only screen and (max-width: 418px) { ... }
```
414px targets iPhone SE/6/7/8. Most 2024–2026 Android flagships have viewport widths of 430–480px. This breakpoint misses a large segment of the Android audience; they receive the desktop layout on a phone screen.
