# Have a birthday party coming up?
**Score:** 6/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-21**
## Executive summary

- Crate & Kids pivots to occasion-based marketing with a birthday gift guide, and the format makes sense — parents searching for gift ideas are a natural audience. The hero sets the scene well with a lifestyle shot tied to the birthday theme, and the multi-section layout tries to cover gifting categories in one scroll. But "Have a birthday party coming up?" is the weakest possible expression of this angle: it's a question with no answer in the subject line. You open knowing only that the brand has detected an occasion; you don't know if there's a deal, a curated list, a new arrival, or anything specific worth your tap.
- The email's bigger problem is structural. This is the second Crate & Kids send today (following "Got the dining table? These are the chairs to go with it →") and at least the seventh in three days across Crate & Barrel / Crate & Kids combined. Gift guide content has shelf life — this same email could land tomorrow and feel fresher if the cadence around it weren't so aggressive. The list is being burned.
- The render itself is clean and on-brand, and for parents in the birthday-planning moment, the curated gift sections do real work. But without a discount, a deadline, or a single standout product as the hero, this email is entirely dependent on the reader being in the exact right mindset at send time.

## What's working

- **Hero lifestyle shot** establishes the birthday context immediately — the eye knows what this is before reading a word.
- **Multi-category structure** serves the gift guide format; showing multiple product sections in one email covers different budgets and tastes.
- **Subject line length** at 33 characters is mobile-friendly and doesn't get cut off.
- **Clean render** — no broken images, overlapping text, or layout bugs visible.
- **Crate & Kids brand recognition** is strong; the sender name alone carries credibility for its core audience.

## What's weak

- **No offer visible** — this appears to be a straight gift guide with no discount, no free shipping threshold, and no loyalty pricing. That removes the primary purchase trigger for a comparison-shopping occasion like birthday gifts.
- **Subject is pure question, no payoff** — "Have a birthday party coming up?" gives the reader zero reason to open; it names the occasion but promises nothing.
- **Multi-section fragmentation** — multiple product sections compete for attention with no single hero product elevated. The eye scatters.
- **Heavy send cadence** is undermining this email before it lands — the 7th send in three days dulls any sense of occasion or urgency.
- **No social proof or urgency signal** visible anywhere in the scroll — no "bestsellers," no star ratings, no "limited stock" — the gift guide format needs a hook to move someone from browsing to buying.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Lead with a hero gift product, not the occasion** — pick the single most giftable item (a price-pointed toy, a bestselling set) and give it a full-width hero with a specific CTA. The gift guide grid can live below.
- 2. **Add a concrete offer** — even free shipping on orders over $X, or a birthday-tier discount code, would give this email a reason to exist beyond "here are some products."
- 3. **Rewrite the subject to deliver a payoff, not ask a question** — the question format works only when the answer in the email is satisfying. Right now the reader has to open to find out there's no deal.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Birthday gifts they'll actually love (from $25)`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Our best birthday gifts — curated by age`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `From first birthdays to big kids — find the perfect pick.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Top-rated, ship-fast picks for every kid on your list.`
- 4. **Pull back cadence** — this gift guide would perform better dropped as a standalone or after a 2-day gap from the dining chairs send.
- 5. **Add a bestseller signal** — a "top-rated" badge or "X gifted this week" label on even one product section would add the social proof that a gifting email desperately needs.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Brand name mismatch in `<title>`**
```html
<title>Crate & Barrel</title>
```
Sender is "Crate & Kids"; the `<title>` resolves to the parent brand. Gmail's tab label and some screen readers expose this value. Should read `Crate & Kids`.

**Conflicting desktop `img` max-width rules — last rule wins incorrectly**
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img {max-width: 600px} }
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img {max-width: 640px} }
```
Both queries match at ≥768px; the 640px block appears later in the stylesheet, so `max-width: 640px` overrides the 600px cap on all desktop viewports. The 600px rule is effectively dead.

**`maximum-scale=1` blocks user zoom (accessibility)**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text, AA) requires text to be resizable up to 200% without assistive technology. `maximum-scale=1` prevents pinch-zoom on iOS, which is a failure for users with low vision.

**Global `text-size-adjust: none` applied via wildcard selector**
```css
* { -webkit-text-size-adjust: none; -ms-text-size-adjust: none; ... }
```
Applying this to all elements rather than just `body` can suppress browser-side font scaling for users who have increased their default font size — compounding the zoom issue above.

**Duplicate `format-detection` meta tags**
Three auto-detection directives appear twice: once in the combined meta and once as individual entries:
```html
<meta name="format-detection" content="telephone=no,address=no,email=no,date=no,url=no">
<!-- then separately: -->
<meta name="format-detection" content="date=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="address=no">
<meta name="format-detection" content="telephone=no">
```
Redundant but harmless; minor HTML hygiene issue.

**Mobile breakpoint at 460px (below industry standard)**
The responsive trigger is `max-width: 460px`. Most email clients use 600px as the mobile threshold. Devices with viewports between 461–599px receive the desktop layout, which may be too wide for portrait phones.
