# New items added! Up to 50% off our 4th of July Warehouse Sale
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-25**
## Executive summary

- This is Crate & Kids' version of the 4th of July Warehouse Sale email — a parallel sibling to the Crate & Barrel send from the same day (June 25, scored 6/10 in this audit), which carried nearly identical subject-line language. For any subscriber on both lists, that's two warehouse sale emails landing on the same day from the same family of brands. That cadence hit is the dominant story here, not the creative.
- The email itself is competently produced. The hero is clean, the offer is clear — up to 50% off — and the Crate & Kids brand identity comes through. Below the fold, the email sprawls: a product grid, a Design Desk consultation pitch, an email signup acquisition block, personalized product recommendations, and an app download prompt. That's five different conversion goals crammed into one scroll.
- The kids-specific angle does add a layer of relevance the Crate & Barrel version lacks, but the execution doesn't fully commit to it. The hero image reads as abstract sculptural decor rather than immediately signaling "this is for your kid's room." If you're a parent subscriber, you can piece it together — but the email doesn't do the work for you.

## What's working

- **Offer is concrete and prominent.** "Up to 50% off" is in the subject and visible in the hero — no hunting required.
- **CTA is immediately visible.** "Shop Now" button appears in the hero before any scrolling.
- **4th of July campaign timing is credible.** The seasonal hook gives the email a real-world deadline anchor.
- **"We love these for you" personalized section.** Product recommendations with visible items (stroller, kids furniture) add relevance for parents.
- **No render bugs.** The email renders cleanly — no broken images, no overlapping text, no layout breakage.

## What's weak

- **Same-day double send with the parent brand.** Any household on both Crate & Barrel and Crate & Kids lists received two near-identical warehouse sale emails on June 25. This is the single biggest failure.
- **Hero image doesn't read as kids content.** The sculptural/decorative shapes in the hero look like home decor accessories, not children's furniture or nursery items. The Crate & Kids brand position is diluted at first glance.
- **Five conversion goals, zero priority.** Product grid → Design Desk → email signup → app download → personalized picks. Each section competes for attention and none wins.
- **"Shop Now" CTA is maximally generic.** For a brand that has real product depth (beds, cribs, strollers), a CTA like "Shop Kids Beds" or "Browse the Sale" would do more work.
- **No loyalty or member callout.** No member pricing, no CB2/Crate & Barrel rewards mention — a missed retention signal.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Coordinate the Crate & Barrel / Crate & Kids send calendar.** Never drop both brands' warehouse sale emails on the same day. Stagger by 48 hours minimum or collapse into one send with a kids section.
- 2. **Lead the hero with a kids-specific visual.** Swap the sculptural vase imagery for a styled kids bedroom or nursery shot — something that makes the Crate & Kids sub-brand immediately legible.
- 3. **Collapse the email to two goals.** Hero + product grid → one CTA. Kill the Design Desk section, the email acquisition block, and the app prompt for this send. Those are separate campaigns.
- 4. **Make the CTA product-specific.** "Shop Kids Beds Sale" or "Shop Cribs & Nursery" converts better than "Shop Now" because it pre-qualifies the click.
- 5. **Add a deadline.** "Sale ends July 4" gives the time-bounded urgency a concrete end date, which is more compelling than the implied holiday association.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Kids' 4th of July Sale — up to 50% off beds, cribs & more`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Up to 50% off kids furniture — sale ends July 4`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `New items just added to beds, nursery, and outdoor.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Biggest markdowns of the summer. Sale ends July 4.`

Not a rendering bug, but the redundancy is unnecessary.

**Conflicting image `max-width` media queries (confirmed):**

The 640px query appears *after* the 768px query in source order. For viewports ≥768px, both rules fire; the 640px rule wins via cascade order, capping images at 640px on desktop rather than 600px. The ordering appears inverted from intent. This does not break layout but produces unintended image sizing on wider screens.
