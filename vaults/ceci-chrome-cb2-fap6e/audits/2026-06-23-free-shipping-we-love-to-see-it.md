# FREE SHIPPING. We love to see it 👀
**Score:** 5/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-23**
## Executive summary

- Crate & Kids is running it back. The June 21 "These favorites ALWAYS ship free →" email already played the free-shipping card, and here's another one two days later with the same benefit and a near-identical creative structure. The hero rotates to a different product assortment — bags, bedding, a stroller — but the strategic beat is identical, which means subscribers who opened the Tuesday send have almost no reason to engage with this one.
- The email itself is competent but catalog-dense. It's structured as a multi-section grid with small CTA buttons, so the eye doesn't land anywhere in particular — it scrolls. The "QUINN" sub-brand section in the middle adds more visual fragmentation without explanation. Free shipping is a real offer, but it's buried in execution: the hero reads more like a seasonal lookbook than a conversion play.
- **Verdict:** A forgettable send that cannibalizes the June 21 free-shipping email's goodwill. The offer is real; the urgency and hierarchy are not.

## What's working

- Free shipping is a tangible, universally understood value prop — no ambiguity about what's on offer.
- The stroller/pram section has strong visual appeal and a clear product hero that reads well even at small render size.
- Crate & Kids logo and brand colors are immediately recognizable at the top — trust established instantly.
- The grid layout gets multiple product categories above the fold, increasing relevance across different buyer intents (bags, bedding, nursery gear).

## What's weak

- **Cadence self-sabotage:** This is the second free-shipping email in two days. The June 21 send already said "ALWAYS ships free" — running the exact same benefit 48 hours later makes both emails feel cheaper and disposable.
- **No urgency mechanism:** "FREE SHIPPING" is a standing benefit for this brand, not a time-limited offer. Nothing in the render suggests it expires, which kills any reason to act now vs. later.
- **Subject-line spam optics:** ALL CAPS "FREE SHIPPING" combined with the emoji-punctuated "We love to see it 👀" reads like a subject line A/B test that lost — too casual, and ALL CAPS triggers both spam filters and inbox fatigue.
- **Multi-section catalog dilutes the hero:** The grid of small product tiles means there's no single moment of desire. The stroller section actually has visual pull — it's wasted as the third section rather than the hero.
- **"QUINN" section unexplained:** The sub-brand appears mid-email with no context or transition copy. A subscriber who doesn't know Quinn will scroll past a section that probably has strong conversion potential.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Make the stroller the hero, not the bags.** The pram image has real visual weight. Lead with it, give it a product name and a price, and let the free-shipping callout be the secondary reward ("And it ships free"). A single, specific product with free shipping is a click; a grid of stuff with free shipping is wallpaper.
- 2. **Add a deadline to the free shipping.** "Free shipping this weekend only" or "Free shipping through Sunday" turns a standing benefit into an event. Without it, this is just a catalog mailer.
- 3. **Introduce QUINN with 10 words of copy.** "Meet Quinn — our new nursery essentials line." That one line doubles click intent on the section.
- 4. **Don't send another free-shipping email until at least 5 days have passed.** The June 21 send needed time to breathe.
- 5. **Rewrite the subject:** Ditch the ALL CAPS and the "We love to see it" TikTok phrasing — it's the right vibe for Instagram, wrong vibe for inbox.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Free shipping on the stroller you've been eyeing`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Ships free — strollers, beds & the new Quinn collection`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `This weekend only — free shipping on everything, no minimum.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `The pram, the backpack, the bed. All of it, on us.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**`maximum-scale=1` in viewport meta (confirmed)**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Prevents pinch-to-zoom on iOS/Android. Violates WCAG 1.4.4 (Resize Text, AA). Should be removed or set to `maximum-scale=5`.

**Global `-webkit-text-size-adjust: none` (confirmed)**
```css
* { -webkit-text-size-adjust: none; ... }
```
Applied to every element via universal selector. Suppresses OS-level accessibility font scaling for all text. Should be scoped to `body` only with value `100%`, not `none`.

**`<title>` tag brand mismatch (confirmed)**
```html
<title>Crate & Barrel</title>
```
Email is from *Crate & Kids* (`crateandkids@mail.crateandbarrel.com`); title reflects the parent brand. Screenreaders and some email clients surface this.

**Dark mode forced off (confirmed)**
```html
<meta name="color-scheme" content="light">
<meta name="supported-color-schemes" content="light">
```
Explicitly opts out of dark mode in Apple Mail and supported clients. No dark-mode `@media (prefers-color-scheme: dark)` block present. May render poorly for ~35–40% of Apple Mail users who use dark mode.

**Duplicate `format-detection` meta tags (confirmed)**
The combined tag (`telephone=no,address=no,email=no,date=no,url=no`) plus three separate individual tags for `date`, `address`, and `telephone` are all present — redundant, though not harmful.

**Conflicting `img` max-width media queries (confirmed)**
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img { max-width: 600px } }
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img { max-width: 640px } }
```
Both fire at ≥768px; the 600px rule (lower specificity, later in cascade) wins on desktop — the 640px rule is functionally dead above 767px. Likely a copy-paste artifact; consolidate to a single rule.

**Mobile fixed-width treatment (confirmed)**
```css
.showmobile { width: 414px !important; }
```
Fixed at 414px against a 460px breakpoint. On 375px-viewport devices (iPhone SE, older models) this forces horizontal scroll within that element class.
