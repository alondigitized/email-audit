# Not sure how to choose a coffee table? Start here →
**Score:** 6/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-24**
## Executive summary

- This is Crate & Barrel in buying-guide mode — a "how to choose a coffee table" editorial that walks through shape, scale, and style considerations with lifestyle photography and product vignettes. There's no sale, no discount, no deadline. The email is long and content-rich: multiple sections covering round vs. rectangular, proportions, material considerations, and at least one outdoor section near the footer.
- The strategy is sound — coffee tables are a considered, higher-AOV purchase, and a guide reduces friction before the browser-based research begins. But this is the third straight editorial email from Crate & Barrel in the last two days (plastic reduction guide, eclectic table styling, now this), and the format is starting to blur. There's no hook that makes today's guide feel timely or different from a static page on the website.
- The execution is visually polished but commercially passive. A subscriber who isn't actively shopping for a coffee table will breeze past this. One who is will find it genuinely useful — but that's a narrow slice of the list.

## What's working

- **Hero image is strong** — the lead lifestyle shot establishes aspiration immediately, and the white-on-neutral palette reads cleanly even at thumbnail scale.
- **Structured editorial format** — the guide breaks into scannable sections with clear visual separators; it doesn't feel like a wall of text.
- **Brand consistency** — tone, photography style, and type hierarchy are all on-brand; nothing feels rushed or off-template.
- **CTA placement throughout** — shop buttons are distributed across the scroll, so a reader who converts mid-guide doesn't have to scroll to the bottom.

## What's weak

- **No offer — nothing to act on today** — a guide email with no sale, no exclusive pricing, and no deadline gives the reader no reason to click *now* vs. bookmarking it for later (then forgetting).
- **Subject line hedges with uncertainty** — "Not sure how to choose?" is empathy-framing, but it also implicitly excludes anyone who already has a preference in mind; it's narrower than it needs to be.
- **Editorial fatigue building** — this is the third back-to-back content email from Crate & Barrel. Compared to the 8/10 Back-to-School sale launch from Crate & Kids on 7/23, this brand's recent streak is all content, no commerce.
- **Email length vs. guide depth tradeoff** — the email is long enough to feel comprehensive but short enough that the actual guidance is thin. A reader who needs real help choosing a coffee table would learn more from a 90-second video or a size-calculator tool; the email format can't deliver that depth.
- **Outdoor section feels tacked on** — surfacing outdoor tables at the bottom breaks the editorial throughline and reads like a category cross-sell that didn't earn its placement.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Add one anchor offer** — even "Free shipping on coffee tables this week" or "New arrivals: 6 shapes to consider" gives the email a commercial reason to exist. Pure editorial with no offer is a brand-equity play that works once a month, not three times in three days.
- 2. **Sharpen the subject to a specific decision** — the question hook is soft. Commit to the most useful or provocative answer instead.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Round or rectangular? How to pick your coffee table`
- **Subject Alt B:** `The one coffee table rule most people get wrong`
- 3. **Rewrite the preheader to tease the most useful section** — if sizing/scale is the most actionable section in the guide, lead with that.
- **Preheader Alt A:** `The right size for your sofa — and 3 shapes that actually work`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Scale matters more than style. Here's why`
- 4. **Cut the outdoor section** — or break it into a separate email. It undermines the guide's editorial integrity and dilutes the click target.
- 5. **Add a "What size for my sofa?" interactive element or sizing chart visual** — this is the #1 question a coffee-table shopper actually has; answering it with a scannable chart would make the guide genuinely differentiated from a Google search.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**CONFIRMED issues:**

**A. Conflicting `img` max-width media queries (rendering bug)**
```css
@media only screen and (min-width: 768px) { img { max-width: 600px } }
@media only screen and (min-width: 640px) { img { max-width: 640px } }
```
The 640px breakpoint fires first; the 768px breakpoint then overrides it to `600px`. Result: images on viewports ≥768px are capped at 600px, while viewports 640–767px get the larger 640px cap. This is backwards — desktop images are narrower than tablet images.

**B. `maximum-scale=1` in viewport meta**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Prevents user-initiated zoom. Fails WCAG 2.1 SC 1.4.4 (Resize Text). Many ESPs flag this automatically.

**C. Conflicting `-webkit-text-size-adjust` values**
```css
/* MI styling block */
body { -webkit-text-size-adjust: 100%; }

/* CLIENT styling block */
html { -webkit-text-size-adjust: none; }
```
Two style blocks set conflicting values. The `none` on `html` wins in WebKit, suppressing font scaling on iOS entirely.

**D. `line-height: 100%` on universal selector**
```css
* { line-height: 100%; }
```
Percentage `line-height` inherits the computed value through the cascade, causing collapsed or overlapping text in nested elements. Best practice is a unitless value (e.g., `1.5`) or removal from the `*` selector.

**E. Duplicate `format-detection` meta tags**
The following directives appear more than once:
- `telephone=no` — declared twice (once combined, once standalone)
- `address=no` — declared twice
- `date=no` — declared twice

Redundant but harmless; indicates a template assembly issue.

**F. Force light-mode only**
```html
<meta name="color-scheme" content="light">
<meta name="supported-color-schemes" content="light">
```
Dark mode is explicitly disabled. Apple Mail dark mode users will receive an uncontrolled color inversion rather than a designed dark variant.
