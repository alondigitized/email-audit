# What’s old is new again
**Score:** 5/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-21**
## Executive summary

- CB2 is attempting an editorial pivot here — the "IN OUR MIDCENTURY ERA" hero is a collection story built around warm woods, organic forms, and cream textiles. After a punishing promotional run that included the June 20 "Even more markdowns just added" (another warehouse sale beat), the midcentury creative is a genuine visual refresh. The photography is warm and cohesive; the product selection — rounded armchair, walnut side table, grid-front sideboard — tells a consistent aesthetic story.
- The problem is the same one that sank the goop collab email and the Todd Snyder drop: the warehouse sale pill at the very top of the email refuses to let the editorial breathe. The subscriber sees "WAREHOUSE SALE" banner, scrolls into a lifestyle shoot, and ends up confused about whether they're shopping a curated collection or clearing out old inventory. Both stories lose.
- The subject line is the single biggest failure. "What's old is new again" is a wordplay on midcentury revival that earns zero information — a subscriber scanning their inbox has no idea this is about furniture, a style, a sale, or anything actionable. It's the least compelling subject CB2 has sent in this run, and it's attached to one of their more visually confident emails.

## What's working

- The midcentury hero image is rich and cohesive — warm-toned lifestyle shot with architecture, textiles, and furniture working together.
- Product selection tells a clear aesthetic story: organic forms, walnut tones, cream upholstery — a subscriber into this style knows exactly what they're seeing.
- "SHOP THE COLLECTION" is a clean, visible CTA with no ambiguity.
- No render bugs — images load, layout holds, nothing overlapping.
- The email breaks from the pure discount cadence of the past week, which on its own reduces unsubscribe fatigue.

## What's weak

- "What's old is new again" as a subject communicates nothing scannable — no product category, no offer, no urgency.
- The warehouse sale banner at the top directly undercuts the curated editorial positioning; midcentury revival feels cheap when framed as clearance.
- No discount percentage is anchored in the editorial hero — the sale banner floats detached from the collection story, so neither the offer nor the editorial lands fully.
- The product grid below the hero is dense and small on mobile — no single item gets enough visual real estate to become a hero SKU.
- Like the goop and Todd Snyder editorials before it, there's no collaborator or designer name to give "midcentury era" cultural gravity — it's a vibe, not an event.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Rewrite the subject to signal something scannable.** "Midcentury Modern" is a recognized style that self-selects buyers. Lean into that noun, not the pun.
- **Subject Alt A:** `Midcentury modern — shop the new collection`
- **Subject Alt B:** `The warmth of midcentury, in stock now`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Walnut, warm textiles, and rounded forms — shop the full look`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Midcentury chairs, tables, and storage — now in the Warehouse Sale`
- 2. **Either commit to the editorial or commit to the sale — not both.** Move the warehouse sale pill to the footer or remove it from this email entirely. If the midcentury pieces are on sale, embed the percentage inside the hero copy ("Up to 40% off") rather than a floating banner that implies clearance.
- 3. **Promote one hero SKU.** The rounded armchair or the walnut side table is a strong anchor product. Give it a full-width frame with price, and let the rest of the grid support it. Right now no single product earns enough real estate to drive a click.
- 4. **Add urgency or scarcity to the collection CTA.** "Shop before it's gone" or a unit-count line ties the editorial to the warehouse sale in a way that feels earned, not bolted on.

## 3. What didn't

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Issue 1 — Developer placeholder comment in production output**
```html
<!--[IMPUT HERE CLIENT FONT IMPORT SCRIPT if needed]-->
```
This is an unfilled template instruction left in the sent HTML, including a typo (`IMPUT` vs `INPUT`). It exposes the ESP/template vendor's internal workflow to anyone who views source.

**Issue 2 — Conflicting `webkit-text-size-adjust` declarations**

Three declarations target the same property with different values:
- `body { -webkit-text-size-adjust: 100% }` (reset block, line ~13)
- `body { -webkit-text-size-adjust: none }` (linked styles block)
- `* { -webkit-text-size-adjust: none }` (universal selector, overrides both)

The universal `none` wins by specificity cascade, defeating the `100%` reset entirely. On iOS, this can cause text to remain unscaled even when the user explicitly requests it.

**Issue 3 — `maximum-scale=1` in viewport meta**
```html
<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1, maximum-scale=1" />
```
Blocks pinch-to-zoom on mobile. Fails WCAG 2.1 SC 1.4.4 (Resize Text). Some email clients strip this, but it should not be present.

**Issue 4 — Double-slash paths in font URLs**
```
https://www.cb2.com//Styles/CB2/fonts/BrownLight/lineto-brown-light.woff2
https://www.cb2.com//Styles/CB2/fonts/BrownRegular/lineto-brown-regular.woff2
https://www.cb2.com//Styles/CB2/fonts/GTSectra/GT-Sectra-Display-Regular.woff2
```
Three of the six `@font-face` `src:` values contain double slashes. Servers typically normalize these via 301 redirect, but it adds a redirect hop and indicates a template merge error.

**Issue 5 — Cross-brand font loading from `crateandbarrel.com`**

Four `HNW05` font variants are loaded from:
```
https://www.crateandbarrel.com/webfonts/*.woff2
```
CB2 and Crate & Barrel share ownership but are distinct brands and domains. This creates an unnecessary cross-origin dependency — if that domain has any CDN issue or CORS policy change, font loading silently fails. The font should be served from `cb2.com` or a shared static CDN.

**Issue 6 — `font-display: swap` in email**

Used on four `@font-face` declarations. This CSS descriptor is not recognized by any major email client (Gmail, Outlook, Apple Mail). It is harmless but dead code — safe to remove.

**Issue 7 — Non-standard mobile breakpoint**
```css
@media only screen and (max-width: 418px)
```
The standard breakpoint for narrow mobile is 480px or 600px. 418px targets only the narrowest phones and will leave medium-small devices (SE 3rd gen at 375pt, Pixel at 393pt) rendering the desktop layout.
