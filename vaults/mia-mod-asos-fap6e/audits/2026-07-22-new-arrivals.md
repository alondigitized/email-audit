# New arrivals
**Score:** 4/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-07-22**
## Executive summary

- This is ASOS in pure new-arrivals browse mode — no offer, no discount, no urgency. The email presents six product shots across three rows, two "SHOP NEW IN" CTAs, and a brand carousel at the bottom, then calls it a day. There is nothing wrong with a new-arrivals format, but executed this flatly, it competes for attention against the July 18 Extra-30%-off send and the July 20 "Up to 70% off Sale" email — both of which gave you an actual reason to click. This one doesn't.
- The visual layout is clean and renders without obvious bugs. The photography is confident — the models, styling, and green/earth-tone palette skew toward a late-20s/30s female shopper, which tracks with the ASOS Women tab at the top. But "New arrivals" as a subject line is the lowest-effort possible frame for an email. It signals nothing except that ASOS uploaded more inventory. Against a crowded inbox, that is not a reason to open.
- Clear judgement: this is a filler send — the kind that keeps the cadence alive between the actually-good offers. It will do fine for already-engaged subscribers browsing on mobile, but it is unlikely to pull in anyone who wasn't already thinking about shopping.

- **Photography is strong** — six varied looks covering dresses, separates, and shoes; muted greens and earth tones give visual coherence across the grid.

- **Navigation bar is present** — Women / Men / New tabs visible at top for quick re-direction.
- **App download CTA is neatly contained** — doesn't compete with the main content; feels like a sensible footer add.

## What's weak

- **No offer, no discount, no urgency** — two "SHOP NEW IN" buttons with zero incentive to click over just opening the website directly.
- **"New arrivals" hero copy is completely generic** — subhead "Styles now in. Keep your wardrobe fresh" adds no specificity; it could apply to every ASOS email ever sent.
- **No product names or prices visible** — a subscriber can't anchor on anything; there's no pull toward a specific item.
- **Follows two sale emails (July 18 extra-30% and July 20 70%-off)** — landing here with zero discount after that cadence feels like a step backward; the contrast undercuts this send's perceived value.
- **"SHOP OUR TOP BRANDS" carousel at the bottom is noise** — it dilutes focus without adding relevance.

- 1. **Add a stacked new-arrivals discount or exclusive-for-early-access framing** — even "first look — 10% off new-in with code NEWJUL" transforms a browse email into an action email. This is the single highest-leverage change.
- 2. **Replace the hero subhead** — "Styles now in. Keep your wardrobe fresh" is dead copy. Try: "Just landed — wear it this weekend" or name a specific category ("72 new dresses. Zero repeats.").
- 3. **Pin one hero product** — pull the strongest single piece to the top with its name and price visible; browsers need an anchor.
- 4. **Cut the brand carousel** — it adds scroll weight and zero intent signal; move app download CTA up if retention is a goal.
- 5. **Add urgency hook** — even "fresh this week" with an implicit recency signal beats a blank CTA.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**[HIGH] Meta tags placed inside `<body><p>` — malformed HTML**

```html
<body>
  <div style="display:none…"></div>
  <p>
    <meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=UTF-8">
    <meta charset="utf-8">
    <meta name="viewport" …>
    …
  </p>
```

`<meta>` elements are not valid children of `<body>` or `<p>`. Most ESP renderers strip `<head>` and inject their own wrapper, which may explain the workaround pattern, but placing metas inside a `<p>` is invalid and will cause the `<p>` to be implicitly closed by the parser before the meta tags, potentially injecting phantom whitespace or a bare `<p>` node into the render tree. Validate against Gmail, Outlook 2021, and Apple Mail.

**[HIGH] Empty preheader div**

```html
<div style="display:none !important;…"></div>
```

The hidden preheader container has no text content. Email clients (Gmail, Apple Mail, Outlook) will fall through to the first visible text in the body as the envelope preview string — likely a font URL or structural text. This should contain a hand-authored summary string.

**[MEDIUM] Duplicate charset declarations**

Both `<meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=UTF-8">` and `<meta charset="utf-8">` are present. Redundant; harmless in practice, but the `http-equiv` form is the HTML4 pattern and `charset="utf-8"` is the HTML5 form. One should be removed.

**[LOW] SVG font format in `@font-face`**

```css
url("…/FuturaPT-Book.svg") format("svg")
```

SVG fonts are deprecated and unsupported in all current email clients and browsers. The declaration is dead weight but not harmful.

**[LOW] `font-size: 0em` on all table elements**

```css
table, tr, td, th { font-size: 0em; … }
```

Standard gap-suppression technique for HTML email, but it forces all descendant text to explicitly reset font size. Screen readers on some platforms interpret `font-size: 0` as hidden content. Confirm all text-bearing `<td>` elements explicitly set `font-size`.

**[LOW] Missing `lang` attribute on `<html>`**

No `lang="en"` (or locale-specific equivalent) visible on the `<html>` element. Required for screen readers to select the correct pronunciation dictionary and for some spam filters.
