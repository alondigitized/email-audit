# New arrivals
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-24**
## Executive summary

- ASOS drops a clean six-panel new arrivals editorial two days after firing their biggest promotional gun of the season — the 60% off summer sale blast on June 22. That timing is the central problem. Any subscriber who opened the sale email is still mentally anchored to discounted pricing; asking them to browse full-price new arrivals two days later is a jarring pivot. The email itself is competently built — strong model photography, clean layout, no rendering issues — but it's structurally identical to "Need some inspo?" (June 22), "The Scandi edit" (June 19), and "Destination: the holiday shop" (June 16). ASOS has sent this same browse-editorial template four times in five days.
- The subject line is the weakest link. "New arrivals" is 12 characters of zero motivation. It doesn't name a trend, a brand, a season, or a reason to care. After a 60% off announcement, it's the easiest email in the inbox to skip. There's no offer, no urgency, no personalization — just product images, which are genuinely strong, doing all the heavy lifting.
- Verdict: a well-rendered email in the wrong slot at the wrong moment. The creative is fine; the strategy undercuts it.

## What's working

- Six strong model shots spanning dresses, denim, and elevated casual — the visual range does communicate breadth without feeling cluttered.
- "SHOP NEW IN" appears both above and below the image grid, covering both the impulse-clicker and the scroll-through reader.
- Visual hierarchy is clean — headline, images, CTA, brand logos, footer — no confusion about where to look.
- No render bugs, broken images, or layout breaks; the email renders correctly on what appears to be a desktop-width viewport.

## What's weak

- Subject line "New arrivals" is the lowest-effort hook ASOS sends — no product, no trend, no price, no personality.
- No offer whatsoever makes conversion entirely dependent on impulse browsing, which is a hard ask when the sale email from two days ago is still in the same inbox.
- Cadence is broken: this is the fourth editorial or near-editorial send in five days, following two sends on June 22 alone. Subscriber fatigue is real at this frequency.
- The "SHOP OUR TOP BRANDS" logo grid (Reclaimed Vintage, Levi's, New Balance, etc.) reads as footer filler — no editorial framing, no reason to engage.
- Preview text appears to reference a person or editor picks but is barely legible at any inbox thumbnail size — it doesn't pull weight where it counts.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Tie new arrivals to sale momentum.** The single highest-impact fix: frame these arrivals as things selling through fast *because* of the sale traffic, or surface new-in pieces already included in the sale. "New in — and already going fast" works during sale season in a way that a pure browse editorial doesn't.
- 2. **Name something specific in the subject.** Category, trend, a standout product — anything. "New in this week: green minis, denim, + more" gives the reader a hook. "New arrivals" gives them nothing.
- 3. **Add a price anchor.** Even "from £X" on one hero image converts hesitant browsers. There's no pricing visible anywhere in this email.
- 4. **Replace the brand logo grid with one editorial callout.** A two-line "our editors can't stop wearing this" pull quote linked to the green romper (the strongest individual shot) would generate more engagement than six logo tiles.
- 5. **Reschedule this send.** New arrivals editorial belongs before a sale (building a want-list) or after it ends (refreshing the feed). Mid-sale is its worst placement.

- **Preheader Alt A:** `Fresh dresses, denim minis, and pieces you haven't seen yet.`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**[HIGH] Malformed document structure — `<meta>` tags in `<body>`**
The HTML omits a `<head>` element entirely. All `<meta>` tags (`Content-Type`, `charset`, `viewport`, `format-detection`, `x-apple-disable-message-reformatting`) are emitted inside `<body>` wrapped in a `<p>` tag:
```html
<html><body>
  <div><!-- preheader --></div>
  <p>
    <meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=UTF-8">
    <meta charset="utf-8">
    ...
  </p>
```
`<meta>` is a void element valid only in `<head>`. Parsers will typically move or discard these, making `viewport` and `format-detection` directives unreliable on mobile clients.

**[MEDIUM] Duplicate charset declaration**
`<meta http-equiv="Content-Type" content="text/html; charset=UTF-8">` and `<meta charset="utf-8">` both present. Redundant; one should be removed (prefer `<meta charset="utf-8">`).

**[MEDIUM] `-webkit-text-size-adjust: none` vs. `100%` conflict**
`body` declares `-webkit-text-size-adjust: none` (disables user font scaling), while the `*` rule declares `-webkit-text-size-adjust: 100%`. The `body` rule wins for body but child elements inherit `100%` via `*`. This inconsistency can produce unpredictable scaling behavior on iOS. The `none` value is also an accessibility concern — it prevents users from applying system-level text size preferences. Change body to `100%`.

**[LOW] Empty preheader div**
The preheader `<div>` contains no text content. Email clients (Gmail, Outlook) will fall back to pulling the first visible body text as preview copy, which will be whatever renders first after the style block — likely a nav link or heading.

**[LOW] `font-size: 0em` on `table, tr, td, th`**
Global zero font-size reset on all table elements is a common Outlook whitespace hack, but any cell with direct text content (not wrapped in a `<span>` or `<p>`) will render invisibly. Verify all table text is wrapped.

**[LOW] MSO conditional block placement**
`<!--[if gt mso 15]>` block appears after the `<body>` tag rather than inside `<head>`. Outlook's preprocessor generally tolerates this, but it's outside spec.
