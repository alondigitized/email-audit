# What's Your Passion? The Game. The Style. The Outdoors.
**Score:** 5/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-05-03**
## Executive summary

- This is a broad, store-wide "shop our categories" promotional email from DICK'S Sporting Goods. The hero drives to an unspecified store browse, a mid-email module offers up to 50% off this week, and a product carousel shows three specific items. There's no single focused offer or narrative thread — it reads like a homepage transplanted into email. The "Sports Matter" Foundation banner further fragments an already scattered message.

## What's working

- **The deals module is strong in isolation.** The green "UP TO 50% OFF" block with the "THIS WEEK'S DEALS" label and a clear "SHOP DEALS" CTA is the best-executed section in the email — high contrast, unambiguous offer, timely framing.
- **Product carousel has specific items.** Three named products (Under Armour fleece hoodie, HOKA Women's running shoes, VRST camo pants) give a concrete shopping hook, which most generic category-nav emails skip entirely.
- **Clean render.** No broken images, overlapping text, or layout breakage. The email is visually readable end-to-end.

## What's weak

- **Hero is a category nav, not an offer.** "Explore the Store for Every Athlete" over six category buttons (Apparel, Footwear, Team Sports, Running, Golf, Camp & Hiking) is a homepage, not an email. There is no headline offer, no urgency, no reason to click NOW.
- **No personalization or category focus.** The email covers six unrelated departments simultaneously. It can't be relevant to any specific recipient because it's trying to be relevant to all of them.
- **The "Sports Matter" Foundation banner is a non-sequitur.** Placed directly between the deals module and the product carousel, it breaks purchase intent with a corporate cause message at the worst possible moment.
- **Product carousel feels algorithmic, not curated.** Three items across men's fleece, women's running, and men's pants with no unifying message ("Check out these picks" is not a reason to buy).
- **No price points visible on product cards.** The carousel shows products but no prices, discounts, or ratings — no actual incentive to click through.
- **Subject line doesn't match a deal.** The subject sets up a lifestyle question but the email's best asset is the 50% off module. These two things don't connect.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Lead with the deal, not the nav.** Move the "Up to 50% Off" module to the top. That's the open-worthy hook — bury it and you lose the click before they get there.
- 2. **Cut the category grid or reduce it to one department.** Six categories in row one signals "we don't know who you are." Pick a lane — run the email for runners, or for outdoors, not both simultaneously.
- 3. **Remove the Sports Matter banner from mid-funnel.** It kills purchase momentum. Reserve cause marketing for a dedicated send or a footer footnote.
- 4. **Add price/sale callouts to the product carousel.** Even "$89.99 → $49.99" on each card would dramatically lift click likelihood.
- 5. **Align subject line to the actual offer.** "Up to 50% Off This Week — Shop the Deals" would outperform the lifestyle-question subject by matching intent to content.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Confirmed issue — CSS parse error in `brand.min.css`:**

```css
a {
  text-decoration:none text-align: center   /* ← missing semicolon */
}
```

The missing `;` between `text-decoration:none` and `text-align:center` causes a parse error. The `text-align:center` declaration is silently dropped in standards-mode parsers. Links that rely on this rule for centering will misalign in standards-compliant clients (Apple Mail, Thunderbird, modern webmail). Outlook's quirks-mode renderer may mask this locally.

**Google Fonts via `fonts.gstatic.com`:**

```css

```

Remote font loading is blocked by default in Gmail (webmail), Outlook desktop (all versions), and many corporate webmail proxies. The fallback stack `Arial, sans-serif` appears defined, so rendering degrades gracefully — but Roboto will not render for the majority of recipients. This is expected behavior for email; flagging it as informational.
