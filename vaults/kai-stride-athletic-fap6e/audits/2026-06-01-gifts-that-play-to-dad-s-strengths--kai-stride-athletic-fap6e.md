# Gifts that play to Dad’s strengths
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-01**
## Executive summary

- So lululemon comes to me on June 1 with a Father's Day gift guide, and my honest first reaction is — this is the most demographically coherent email they've sent me in a month. All men. Athletic products. A recognizable athlete in the hero. After the Breezily Shorts debacle and the tennis-dress double-tap, I'll take it. At least they remembered I have a Y chromosome.
- But here's the problem: I buy lululemon for *me*. I'm 33, I run 50 miles a week, I captain a club. When I open a lululemon email on a Sunday, I want the next Pace Breaker colorway or a restock alert — the May 30 "Catch the latest restock" email is the template they should be working from. The gift-guide framing instantly routes this into low-priority territory in my brain. Jordan Clarkson in the hero is an interesting call — basketball isn't my sport but the athleticism reads — and run categories do appear in the body grid, so it's not a total whiff. But I'm marking this as read, not clicking.
- Would I forward to a friend? Maybe — if I had a running buddy whose dad was into athletics. But for myself? No.

## What's working

- **All-male cast throughout** — Every model in this email is a guy doing physical things. After the consecutive women-in-casual-pants sends, this baseline respect for my segment is worth noting.
- **Run categories are present in the body** — "Shop Run Bottoms" and "Shop Run Tops" links exist in the product grid. If my dad runs, this is actually useful and more thought-out than adidas' "Wear a piece of history" send, which led with a retro Japan lifestyle sneaker to a runner — at least lululemon put run categories in the mix.
- **Price filters ($50/$100) reduce gifting friction** — Smart framing for a gift-guide send. Nike rarely does this in gift emails; lululemon figured out that the first gift-shopper question is "what's reasonable to spend," not "what category."
- **Visual hierarchy is clean** — Hero → category grid → "more favourites" → footer. The eye moves down without confusion and there are zero render bugs or broken layouts.
- **Jordan Clarkson athlete tie-in** — The orange hero and Clarkson's athleticism elevate this above generic catalog slop. It's a reason the email exists beyond "we need a Father's Day send."

## What's weak

- **Gift framing kills my personal purchase intent** — I'm buying for myself, not gifting someone. The entire email is architected around someone else's cart, so my instinct is to deprioritize immediately.
- **Basketball hero, not a runner** — Jordan Clarkson plays in the NBA. My dad doesn't play basketball. If they'd cast a trail runner or even a track athlete in the hero, the email would speak directly to my segment — and to every running-club member I'd forward this to.
- **No promotional offer** — No percentage off, no member pricing, nothing that creates urgency or pull. Compare this to adidas' personalized voucher send from last week ("⏳ Last day to redeem your voucher") — that felt like money in my pocket. This just feels like a catalog.
- **Cadence is wearing me down** — This is roughly the eighth email in ten days from lululemon. That pace is eroding the equity of every individual send. Even when the email is good, I'm starting to feel blasted.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Swap the hero athlete to a running context.** Jordan Clarkson is fine but basketball → running is a cognitive jump for someone in my segment. A trail runner or marathon pacer in the orange-hero slot changes who clicks and who forwards.
- 2. **Add one run-specific CTA above the fold.** "Get Dad race-ready →" pinned to the Surge or Pace Breaker converts runners immediately. "Shop Father's Day Gifts" is so broad it lands nowhere specific.
- 3. **Add a shipping deadline.** "Order by June 12 for Father's Day delivery" would create real, credible urgency. Right now the email floats with no hook. Even Nike's transactional "Here's your one-time code" email outperformed because it had a clear job to do — this one needs a deadline.
- 4. **Cut email frequency to every 3–4 days.** The cumulative volume is making each individual send feel cheaper. This email would hit harder if it wasn't the fourth email this week.

- **Preheader Alt B:** `Built for the dad who logs miles — from run to golf to train.`
