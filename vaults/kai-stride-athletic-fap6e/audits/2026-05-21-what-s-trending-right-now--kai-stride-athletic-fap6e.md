# What’s trending right now
**Score:** 5/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-05-21**
## Executive summary

- Six emails in, and lululemon still has no idea I'm a runner. "What's trending right now" drops into my inbox and the hero is a woman in a red athletic dress — which, fine, great product, but I run 50 miles a week and buy the Surge shorts and ABC pants from these people. Nothing in this send signals that lululemon knows that. The product grid is almost entirely women's silhouettes, the two "New this week" split-panels show tennis-court imagery (for the third time this week, I might add — Monday was "fast-drying tennis kits," Tuesday was "Ready to rally"), and the membership tease at the bottom is just "energizing hue" with zero context about what that means or which product it applies to.
- This is a catalog dump dressed up as trend curation. "Trending Top 10" is the kind of copy that works if you're browsing; it does nothing if you came here because you needed to know whether the Pace Breaker shorts got a new colorway for summer. adidas sent me a genuinely specific story about the 5.10 climbing shoe last week — felt like they wrote it for someone who cares about gear culture, not just someone with a credit card. This doesn't come close to that.
- Mark as read. No click, no forward. The brand history here is basically a slow-motion drift: lululemon keeps sending me content built for the yoga-and-tennis set, and every week it costs them a little more of my attention.

## What's working

- **lululemon sender name** gets the open reflex every time — I've spent real money here and that brand equity carries.
- **Visual hierarchy is clean** — hero image, then grid, then "New this week" panels, then membership section. The scroll logic is clear and nothing is fighting for attention.
- **No render bugs** — full-bleed images load, text is legible, layout holds on mobile. Executional table stakes, but not a given (Nike's code email was fine; some brands can't even clear this bar).
- **Membership early access callout** is a nice loyalty signal — I like knowing I'm getting something before the general public, even if the teaser here is too vague to act on.

## What's weak

- **Zero running DNA in the entire email** — no Surge, no Pace Breaker, no men's training content above the fold. The hero model is female, the product grid reads as women's-first, and the "New this week" panels are tennis (again — third tennis touchpoint in three days).
- **"What's trending right now" is dead copy** — it tells me nothing about what category, what product, or why I should care. Compare that to how adidas titled "adidas 5.10's Sticky Secret" — that's a subject line with a pull; this is a content calendar placeholder.
- **The membership tease is all friction, no payoff** — "Coming soon: energizing hue" with a "Download the App" CTA asks me to take three steps (download, log in, navigate) to find out what color something is. If you have early access, show me the product.
- **No offer** — no percentage off, no member pricing, no free shipping threshold. This is a pure browse email, which is fine, but without any hook it's asking me to shop for no reason other than "it's trending." After five previous sends without a runner-relevant promotion, I need more than vibes.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Segment the men's and women's sends** — or at minimum lead with a men's hero and product grid for the men's list. A female hero above the fold is a signal that whoever built the list didn't bother to split by gender, and that erodes trust fast.
- 2. **Make "Trending Top 10" actually mean something** — show me 10 ranked products with brief reason-to-buy copy, or drop the conceit entirely. Right now it's a label with no payoff.
- 3. **Put a running module in every send** — I've bought training-specific product from lululemon, so the behavioral data exists. One running-specific row ("Built for the long run → Shop Men's Training") would have salvaged this entire email for me.
- 4. **Flesh out the membership early access** — either show the product (name it, image it, link it) or don't tease it. "Energizing hue" plus a download nudge is a friction-for-nothing exchange.
- 5. **Rest the tennis content** — two tennis emails in two days, and now tennis imagery in the "New this week" panels of a third email. Cadence matters. Nike paced their re-engagement with one sharp transactional send and let it breathe; three hits on the same sport in three days is how you train people to scroll past.

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**Confirmed bug — invalid `letter-spacing` values:**

```css
/* .guest-voice-lg, .guest-voice-md, .guest-voice-sm */
letter-spacing: -2%;   /* INVALID — percentage is not a valid CSS unit for letter-spacing */
letter-spacing: -1%;   /* INVALID */
```

`letter-spacing` only accepts length units (`px`, `em`, `rem`). Percentage values are ignored by all CSS parsers. These three guest voice classes will render with default (0) letter-spacing in all email clients, diverging from intended design.

**Fix:** Replace with `letter-spacing: -0.02em` and `letter-spacing: -0.01em` respectively.

---

**Non-standard font weight:**

```css
font-weight: 380;  /* used on p, h1–h5, a, and several named classes */
```

`380` is valid in the CSS Fonts Level 4 spec (variable fonts) but is unsupported in most email clients, which clamp to the nearest standard value (300 or 400). If `Saans-Regular` is served as a variable font via `@font-face`, the fallback stack (`Helvetica Neue, sans-serif`) will render at 400, not 380 — acceptable but worth verifying the visual delta.

---

**Custom fonts will not render in most clients:**

`Saans-Regular`, `Saans-Medium`, `Saans-Semibold`, `Saans-Light`, `KHGiga-Light` — none are web-safe. Email clients that block remote `@font-face` (Gmail web, most Outlook versions) will fall back to `Helvetica Neue` / `Arial` / `Times New Roman`. Fallbacks are well-specified; no fix needed, but QA should verify fallback rendering in Gmail and Outlook.

---

**Near-black/near-white hex values:**

```css
.black { background-color: #000001; }   /* 1-step off true black */
color: #140F0F;                          /* near-black text */
color: #fdfeff;                          /* near-white text */
```

The `#000001` pattern is an intentional technique to prevent dark mode clients from auto-inverting "true black" (`#000000`) to white. This is valid and deliberate — not a bug.
