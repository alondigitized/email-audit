# $6 tees and $5 tanks are yours for the taking
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-05-16**
## Executive summary

- "$6 tees and $5 tanks" — I clocked that subject line in about half a second. That's the Old Navy I signed up for: flat dollar prices on the exact items I stock up on before the weather turns. I buy tees and tanks the way other people buy paper towels, and this is the price point that triggers the auto-add-to-cart brain. I opened immediately.
- Once I was in, the email just... kept going. Hero for the $6 tees, then a section for $5 tanks, then an extra-off-when-you-spend block, then 50% off deals, then Dream Dresses under $37, then three rows of Clearance at the bottom. That's six distinct promotional moments in one scroll. The subject line promised one clean thing; the email delivered a clearance table during semi-annual. The gauze dress email from May 14th was sharper — one offer, one category, one reason to click. This one scattered the attention.
- I'd absolutely click on the $6 tees and probably the $5 tanks — those are restocking purchases, not browsing. But I wouldn't forward this to anyone. Too much noise. My friend who shops Hanna Andersson would think Old Navy is running the email equivalent of the grab bin.

## What's working

- **Dollar figures do all the heavy lifting.** $6 and $5 in big text above the fold — no percentage math, no "up to" hedging. This is how family-staple pricing should be communicated.
- **"2 DAYS ONLY!" lands with real urgency.** Not vague "limited time" — an actual window I can plan a trip or a cart around.
- **The dual offer stacks naturally.** Tees and tanks in the same email means one checkout covers both. That's the kind of bundled logic that matches how I shop.
- **Adult female models in the hero match my shopping context.** I'm buying for me first, extrapolating to kids second. Leading with women's fashion is right.
- **Clean render throughout.** No broken images, no layout collapse — everything read legibly on first scroll.

## What's weak

- **Six competing offers is too many.** By the time I hit "Dream Dresses Under $37," I'd already lost the thread. One email, one offer — the May 14th gauze send proved Old Navy can do this.
- **No kids-sizing signal anywhere in the hero.** I'm buying these tees for two kids AND myself. A single "Kids, too!" badge on the $6 section would have closed the loop. Instead I'm left guessing whether the deal applies to the sizes I actually need.
- **"Yours for the taking" is trying too hard.** Reads like they workshopped a fun tagline and landed somewhere between casual and corporate. Hanna Andersson's welcome email from May 8th — "❤️ Welcome To Hanna!" — is warm without forcing it. This phrasing is the opposite.
- **Three stacked Clearance blocks at the bottom feel like a different email got stapled on.** It breaks the rhythm and makes the whole thing feel like a newsletter for a store closing sale, not a curated summer promo.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Pick one offer and commit.** Run the $6 tees + $5 tanks email standalone. The Dream Dresses and Clearance blocks belong in a separate send — or at least pushed into a secondary module with much less visual weight.
- 2. **Add a kids-sizing callout in the first hero block.** Even a one-line "In adults & kids" under the $6 price point would double the relevance signal for the family-shopper cohort.
- 3. **Collapse the Clearance stack into a footer strip.** Three full-bleed Clearance CTAs undercut the premium-enough feel of the fashion blocks above them.
- 4. **Kill "yours for the taking."** Replace with something that makes the value feel earned — "Lowest prices of the season" or "Deal-drawer prices are back."
- **Subject Alt A:** `$6 tees + $5 tanks: stock up before Sunday`
- **Subject Alt B:** `Fill the drawer: tees for $6, tanks for $5`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Kids' and adults' sizes — 2 days only, then they're gone.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `These move fast. Shop the $6 and $5 deals now.`
