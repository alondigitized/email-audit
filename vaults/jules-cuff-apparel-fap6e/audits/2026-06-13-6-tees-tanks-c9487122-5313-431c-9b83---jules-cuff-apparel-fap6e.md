# 👕 $6 TEES & TANKS
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-13**
## Executive summary

- Twenty-six. Wait, twenty-seven? At this point I've genuinely lost count and that is the problem Old Navy has created for itself. I opened this one because the subject actually landed — $6 tees and tanks is a number I can use, and I am absolutely someone who buys those things by the stack for picture day and baseball practice. The "2 DAYS ONLY!" hero hit me where I live. But by the time I scrolled through Father's Day, the Outlet, Active Gear, and that epilepsy-inducing Clearance stack at the bottom, I had forgotten what I came for.
- The offer is real and the price is right. For me — someone who annually loads a cart with Old Navy basics for two kids who will destroy them by October — $4 tees is a genuine reason to click. But Old Navy has been in my inbox every single day for weeks at this point, and the formula is so predictable I can almost recite it: "2 DAYS ONLY, here is a number, here are five other things, Clearance." At some point that predictability stops reading as urgency and starts reading as background noise.
- I'd click the tees offer. I would not forward this. I'd probably batch-delete the next three Old Navy emails without opening them.

## What's working

- **The hero number is concrete.** "$4 tees / $6 tanks" plus "2 DAYS ONLY" is an honest, scannable value prop — no "up to" hedge, no buried asterisk at the hero level.
- **Urgency is visible without scrolling.** "2 DAYS ONLY!" in large red type at the top of the render is exactly how you do it; I didn't have to hunt for the deadline.
- **Season is right.** Summer tees and tanks for adults, Father's Day section, active gear — this is a June email that feels like June, which is more than I can say for some brands I see running spring sale language in mid-June.
- **Mixed adult model imagery.** I can see women *and* men in the render, which matters to me because I shop for both; at least Old Navy isn't making me dig for the men's section when I'm buying husband stuff too.
- **Extra 30% off storewide.** The stacked discount at the top banner means the $4 tee math might actually go lower — that is legitimately compelling for a volume buyer like me.

## What's weak

- **The scroll is exhausting.** By the time I hit the Clearance section, I had already mentally checked out. Hanna Andersson's welcome email — one offer, one strong hero, done — makes this look like a clearance bin exploded on my phone.
- **The Clearance section is visually chaotic.** Stacked "CLEARANCE CLEARANCE CLEARANCE" in clashing colors is meant to feel energetic; it just feels cheap and a little desperate for a brand I was already scanning for the kids section.
- **No kids callout that I can see.** Old Navy is where I buy kids' uniforms and basics, and I'm scrolling through adult tees, men's shirts, active gear, and outlet stuff with zero pull toward the kids I'm actually buying for.
- **Twenty-seven emails in two weeks.** The cadence is the miss. This offer might have slapped on Day 3. On Day 14 it's just more wallpaper.
- **The Father's Day section feels grafted on.** Dark background, men's shirts, separate visual treatment — it reads like two emails forced into one, and the transition is jarring after the bright summer hero.

## Recommendations

- 1. **One offer, full email.** Tees & tanks at $4/$6 is strong enough to carry a standalone send. Cut Father's Day, Active Gear, and Clearance to their own emails. The dilution is real.
- 2. **Lead with kids.** If I'm a known Old Navy customer who has bought kids' uniforms, put the kids tees in the hero or at least a secondary block. That's the purchase I'm actually planning.
- 3. **Give the clearance section a visual overhaul.** The stacked word treatment looks frantic. Hanna Andersson would never — and when I open a Hanna email, the restraint makes me trust the brand more. Old Navy should learn from that.
- 4. **Cool the cadence.** A "2 DAYS ONLY" email means nothing when I've gotten 26 before it. If this were the third email in two weeks instead of the twenty-seventh, the urgency would feel real.
- 5. **Use my purchase history.** Even a simple "Stock up on the basics you bought last fall" acknowledgment would have made this feel targeted, not broadcast.
