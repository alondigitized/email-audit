# FYI: 60% off dresses + 50% off everything* else (yes, really!)
**Score:** 8/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-22**
## Executive summary

- Day six. I've been counting. And I'll give Old Navy this much: "FYI" as a subject opener is a low-key casual move, and leading with 60% off dresses is at least a pivot from the "60% off shorts and swim" drum I've been hearing since Sunday. But "TODAY ONLY!" in the hero, on the sixth email of the same rolling sale? I spotted that instantly and my brain auto-filed it under noise before I even started scrolling.
- The dresses angle is legitimately useful for me — I need something for a school event next month and Old Navy dresses punch above their price point. The "HOT NEW GEAR" section mid-scroll with the bright athletic colorways is actually the most visually energetic thing this brand has shown me all week. If I'd gotten this email on day one instead of day six, I'd probably have clicked three separate sections. Instead I'm clicking once, reluctantly, because the purchase intent was already there — not because this email earned it.
- I'd mark it as read and eventually click from the dresses category out of existing need. I would not forward this to anyone. If my friend asked about the Old Navy sale I'd say "yeah, still going" with zero enthusiasm.

## What's working

- The dresses lead is a genuine content shift — finally a category angle that's different from what I've been staring at all week.
- "TODAY ONLY!" in the hero creates a hard visual anchor; my eye went there first before anything else, which is good hierarchy.
- The "HOT NEW GEAR" block with the bright blue, pink, and neon athletic models is the most alive this email gets — it doesn't read like clearance, it reads like something actually worth browsing.
- The hero model in the black dress reads age-appropriate for me — not a teen influencer, not a catalog stiff. She feels real.

## What's weak

- "TODAY ONLY" on day six of a rolling sale is the brand crying wolf to someone who has been watching the wolf not show up for a week straight. The urgency is completely hollow at this point.
- "(yes, really!)" in the subject line is Old Navy acknowledging I don't believe them — which is not the same as fixing the problem. Hanna Andersson's welcome email earned my attention with a clean 20% code, zero games; six emails in and Old Navy is bargaining with punctuation.
- No kids section in sight. I'm buying for two kids every season and the family angle would double my cart. Showing me women's-only above the fold is leaving money on the table.
- The clearance grid at the bottom is a visual dump — no "last in your size" editorial hook, no specificity. It's just stuff. Compare that to how Hanna handles end-of-season: curated, tight, "these are going."
- Zero loyalty acknowledgment. No SuperCash balance, no member-only pricing, no "as a subscriber" moment. Day six of emailing me and I'm still being treated like a stranger on a list.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Kill the "TODAY ONLY" unless it's actually true — and if it is, prove it.** A visible end-date or countdown clock would make me believe it. Without that, the scarcity copy is actively eroding trust.
- 2. **Add a family / kids-plus-mom moment above the fold.** One "dress the whole crew" hero — mom + kids in complementary summer looks — and I'm pulling up a second browser tab.
- 3. **Give clearance editorial purpose.** "Last chance in your size" with three to five specific hero items beats the dump grid every time. Make the urgency real where it actually is real.
- 4. **Surface SuperCash or rewards status.** That number moves my behavior more than any percentage banner.
- 5. **Lean into the cadence joke in the hero, but commit to it.** Something like "OK, genuinely last day, we mean it" reads self-aware in a way that builds goodwill. "(yes, really!)" in the subject is half-hearted and lands as desperate.

- **Preheader Alt A:** `Grab a summer dress before sizes go — this one wraps tonight.`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `Dresses, tees, and kids' clothes all on sale. Stock up.`

This resets all `<td>` line-height to zero in Outlook 2013+. Correctness depends entirely on every `<td>` having an explicit inline `line-height` override. If any content cell lacks one, text will collapse. This is a common Outlook hack but high-risk if the template is modified without awareness.
