# Hot temps, hotter prices: 60% off all shorts & swim*, $5 dresses & half off everything* else
**Score:** 7/10 · **Type:** Email audit · **2026-06-20**
## Executive summary

- Okay, I'm going to be straight with you: my thumb was already primed to swipe this into a folder before I even tapped. We have officially hit day four of Old Navy's "60% off shorts and swim" tour, and the only thing that changed is the subject line got a catchy "hot temps, hotter prices" opener stapled to the front. The June 19 send had three stacked deals. The June 16 send had 60% off plus an extra 20%. Now here we are again, same percentage, same hero, same asterisks, slightly different subject copy. I've started mentally filing these under "sale that never actually ends."
- What saves this from being a full skip is the NEW TECH section mid-scroll — that's genuinely different from the past week's parade of shorts-and-swim. If you buried something new in the middle of a mass-promo email, though, you've already lost me. I've trained myself to stop scrolling when I know what's coming, and the hero told me I knew exactly what was coming.
- Mark as read. Not forwarding this. Not clicking unless I'm specifically running low on swim coverage for the kids and haven't already bought it on the three previous 60%-off days.

## What's working

- The "60% OFF" hero is visually dominant — no hunting required, the deal is the first thing you see.
- "SHOP CLEARANCE IN YOUR SIZE" is the smartest copy in this email; that's the hook that makes me feel like I'm not sifting through sold-out sizes.
- Color palette is punchy and summer-appropriate — the orange block for the NEW TECH section breaks up the monotony and gives the eye somewhere new to land.
- The multi-section layout efficiently communicates that there's more than one offer without completely burying each one.
- No broken images, no layout collisions — renders cleanly, which is more than I can say for some emails I've seen this week.

## What's weak

- This is the fourth 60%-off send in five days — Hanna Andersson's welcome email from May sent one 20%-off code and made it feel like a gift; Old Navy has now made 60% off feel like the default price, which means it's not really a sale anymore.
- The $5 dresses are in the subject line but nearly invisible in the body — if that's the real hook for me (and $5 dresses for my daughter absolutely is), it should have its own hero moment, not disappear into the multi-column grid.
- No kids or family-specific callout in the hero section — I'm shopping for two children and myself; the hero models look adult-only and fashion-forward, not "mom who needs ten tees by Friday."
- The asterisks multiply like rabbits: "shorts & swim\*", "everything\* else" — I've been burned before by Old Navy exclusions and I know those asterisks mean "not the stuff you actually want." Kills the trust.
- The NEW TECH section is genuinely interesting but completely mislabeled for where it sits — mid-scroll after two giant promo blocks, most recipients are gone before they reach it.

## Recommendations

- 1. **Give the $5 dresses their own hero panel** — that's the one genuinely specific, jaw-dropping price point in this email and it's hiding in the subject line. Lead with it for the family-shopper segment: a $5 dress for my four-year-old is the kind of thing I screenshot and text to my sister.
- 2. **Surface kids/family content above the fold** — Hanna Andersson earns its place in my cart by being laser-focused on the kid. Old Navy has just as much kids inventory; the email just doesn't show it to me until I've already mentally checked out.
- 3. **Retire the 60%-off hero for at least 48 hours** — the credibility of the offer collapses when it's permanent. Even a single send at "ends Sunday" with a real countdown would restore urgency. Right now "60% off" means nothing.
- 4. **Move NEW TECH up** — if you've got new product, lead with it on a week when the promo is stale. New is the only thing that cuts through promo fatigue.
- 5. **Kill one asterisk** — explain the main exclusion in plain English (e.g., "excludes Fleece") so it doesn't feel like a trap.
- **Subject Alt A:** `$5 dresses + 60% off swim — this weekend only`
- **Subject Alt B:** `The $5 dress situation is back (and swim is 60% off)`
- **Preheader Alt A:** `Plus half off almost everything else — sizes going fast`
- **Preheader Alt B:** `New tech styles just landed, and they're half off too`

### 3. Rendering & Accessibility

**[WARN] Missing alt text on tracking pixel**

The Marigold open-tracking pixel (`cp.oldnavy.com/o/3d7780af...`) has no `alt` attribute:
```html
<img src="https://cp.oldnavy.com/o/3d7780af-..." width="1" height="1">
```
Screen readers may announce this as an unlabeled image. Best practice is `alt=""` on purely decorative/tracking pixels.

**CSS class duplication in responsive stylesheet**

`.MspaceTop` and `.MspaceBot` are each defined twice with conflicting values (5px and 10px). The second declaration wins in all conforming parsers, but the dead rule adds noise and can mask future edits:
```css
.space-top5, .MspaceTop {padding-top: 5px;}   /* dead */
.space-top10, .MspaceTop {padding-top: 10px;}  /* wins */
```

**Outlook line-height zero override**

The MSO conditional block sets `td { line-height: 0px }` globally for Outlook 2013+. This is a common but aggressive workaround — any `<td>` that relies on inherited line-height for spacing in Outlook will collapse. Confirm all spacer rows use explicit `height` attributes rather than padding/line-height.
